# AI Fashion Design Tools: Creative Homogenization, Copyright Fault Lines, and the Resolution Landscape

## Executive Summary

**Are AI fashion design tools leading to creative homogenization?**
The answer is conditional and depends on the level of analysis. At the **machine, cognitive, and fashion-imagery levels**, convergence pressure is real, directional, and measurable. Pooled meta-analyses of human–AI co-creation show that while generative AI boosts individual creative performance—especially for novices—it significantly reduces collective diversity across users ($d = 0.334$ in de Rooij & Biskjaer, 2026; $g = -0.863$ in Holzner et al., 2025), with the strongest narrowing occurring in semantically constrained ideation ($d = 0.70$), the exact stage where fashion studios use AI for moodboards and concepts. Audits of 27 text-to-image generators (Alvero et al., 2026) and controlled experiments on Midjourney fashion imagery (Feng & Kim, 2026) prove that models converge on a uniform, idealized Western body/face standard ($100\%$ youthful, smooth-skinned, idealized faces across all racial prompts) while compressing non-Western traditions into stereotyped surface motifs. Furthermore, demand-side trend forecasting (e.g., Heuritech, WGSN) and ultra-fast-fashion algorithmic scraping (e.g., Shein) coordinate market supply around identical trend signals.

However, **an industry-wide decline in the diversity of manufactured clothing is not established**. No longitudinal computer-vision study has measured fashion catalog diversity before and after AI adoption against fashion's already high pre-AI copying baseline ("dupe culture"). Crucially, current generative AI lacks the technical "Design Language" of garment construction—it cannot execute digital pattern-making, draping, or 3D structural prototyping (Rizzi & Bertola, 2025). Moreover, three counter-forces resist homogenization: **active designer steering** (using sketch-driven refinement or "Focused Collaboration" rather than blind prompting), **data curation** (fine-tuning personal LoRA adapters or cultural heritage datasets like Indonesian Ulos motifs), and **market competition** (game-theoretic and consumer evidence showing that competitive markets and avant-garde shoppers penalize generic AI sameness and reward visible human design effort).

**How can the copyright disputes between independent designers and algorithms be resolved?**
Because an algorithm is a production mechanism rather than a legal person, disputes actually run between designers and AI developers (over unlicensed training data), brands/platforms (over imitative outputs and algorithmic trend-scraping), and copyright registries (over whether AI-assisted work is protectable at all). These conflicts are acute in fashion because copyright in major markets like the United States protects only separable 2D prints (*Star Athletica*), leaving garment cuts, silhouettes, and overall "style" legally unowned. Meanwhile, UK and EU unregistered design rights—built specifically for seasonal fashion shapes—suffer from a near-fatal evidentiary flaw against AI: proving causal copying through a proprietary algorithmic black box is practically impossible (*Edwards v. boohoo*; Sugumar, 2026).

No single mechanism resolves these disputes; resolution requires a **ten-layered institutional, legal, and technical stack** adapted to six diverging jurisdictions (US, EU, UK, China, Japan, France):
1. **Targeted Litigation & Aggregation:** Leveraging class actions and enterprise theories like civil RICO (*Perry v. Shein*) or French *parasitisme* tort law to overcome individual litigation cost barriers, alongside pirated-dataset settlements ($1.5\text{ billion}$ / $\sim\$3,000$-per-work in *Bartz v. Anthropic*).
2. **Collective & Direct Licensing:** Closing the five-order-of-magnitude gap between micro-royalties ($\sim\$0.029$/image in Adobe Firefly) and litigation awards by building fashion collecting societies and state-backed exchanges (e.g., the UK's summer 2026 Creative Content Exchange pilot), subject to the Berne Convention's three-step test (Lucchi et al., 2026).
3. **Statutory Transparency & Machine-Readable Opt-Outs:** Enforcing EU AI Act Article 53 training-content summaries, the GPAI Code of Practice complaint channels, and W3C TDM Reservation Protocols (`TDMRep`), while recognizing that opt-outs fail without auditability.
4. **Design-Law & Likeness Reform:** Utilizing modernized registration routes (the 2025–2027 EU Design Act reforms) that remove the burden of proving copying; stripping protection from purely machine-generated designs to prevent prior-art flooding (UKIPO CP 1401); and extending statutory consent-and-pay models from fashion models (NY Fashion Workers Act) to designers.
5. **Studio Workflow, Technical Provenance & Self-Help:** Combining C2PA Content Credentials and step-by-step human-authorship logs (essential for US registration under USCO Part 2) with adversarial pre-publication cloaking (Glaze, Nightshade), personal archive fine-tuning, and visible human-craft storytelling that directly drives consumer purchase intent (Zhang et al., 2026).

## 1. Framing the Problem: Tool Taxonomy and Measurement Levels

Evaluating both homogenization and copyright requires disaggregating "AI fashion design tools" into five functional categories, each carrying a distinct creative mechanism and legal profile:

1. **General-Purpose Generative Ideation Tools** (Midjourney, DALL·E / GPT Image, Stable Diffusion, Runway, Adobe Firefly, ChatGPT): Used by designers for moodboards, text-to-image concept sketches, print generation, and virtual show videos. They drive the cognitive fixation and corpus-prior mechanisms and sit at the center of training-data litigation.
2. **Fashion-Specific Design & Sketch-Refinement Platforms** (CALA, Raspberry.ai, Yoona.ai, NewArc.ai, Refabric, Resleeve, The New Black, Caimera, fashionINSTA, Viscom, plus research architectures like FashionQ and StyleMe): These often use sketch-to-render conditioning, masking, and fabric controls to preserve a designer's hand-drawn geometry, and several market themselves as "commercially safe" (trained on licensed data).
3. **Trend-Forecasting, Scraping, and Retail Demand Engines** (Heuritech, WGSN, H&M's purchasing-quantity models, Stitch Fix's styling algorithms, and Shein's proprietary trend-scraping system): These generate no garment designs directly (though some now embed generative visualization). Instead, they coordinate demand and supply by quantifying up to 2,000 stylistic attributes per social-media image (Heuritech claims $\sim 90\%$ accuracy a year ahead; unaudited vendor claim) or directing suppliers to replicate viral items at scale.
4. **Synthetic Models and Digital Replicas** (Levi's $\times$ Lalaland.ai, H&M's "AI twins," Guess's AI model in *Vogue*, Miami Fashion Week's Meta AI sponsorship): Used for marketing and e-commerce visualization, triggering labor and likeness-rights disputes rather than garment copyright claims.
5. **Consumer-Facing AI Dupe-Finders and 3D Virtual Prototyping** (Sky News-profiled visual search tools that locate cheap look-alike "dupes" from a product URL/photo vs. conventional 3D CAD/draping software like CLO3D): While dupe-finders accelerate downstream copying disputes, 3D construction software remains non-generative and dependent on human pattern-making knowledge.

Similarly, "creative homogenization" is not a single metric. It operates across five distinct levels that can move in opposite directions simultaneously:

| Analytical Level | Core Question | Appropriate Evidence | Common Measurement Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Individual Exploration** | Does a designer investigate more distinct structural concepts? | Concept counts after grouping near-duplicates; variation in cut, material, and function. | Counting 50 colorways or surface renders of one silhouette as 50 ideas. |
| **2. Individual Creative Quality** | Is a single output novel and useful for its brief? | Blind domain-expert ratings separating novelty from visual polish and manufacturability. | Mistaking photorealism, high resolution, or aesthetic appeal for originality. |
| **3. Cross-Designer Diversity** | Do different designers produce more similar work when using AI? | Pairwise semantic, visual, or structural similarity across creators given matched briefs. | Inferring population diversity from the quality of one standout collection. |
| **4. Market / Assortment Diversity** | Does the clothing actually manufactured and sold become narrower? | Longitudinal computational stylometry of retail catalogs, production runs, and sales data. | Treating online prompt galleries as the industry's manufactured output. |
| **5. Cultural & Bodily Range** | Which bodies, heritages, and craft traditions remain visible? | Stratified audits of demographic representation, cultural motifs, and garment systems. | Assuming average visual distance guarantees authentic cultural inclusion. |

*Note: Table adapted from analytical standards in the research literature; rows represent distinct empirical targets rather than interchangeable proxies.*

### Industry Adoption and Economic Stakes
Adoption in fashion is broad in executive ambition but shallow in core creative execution. In Business of Fashion (BoF) and McKinsey's *State of Fashion 2024* survey, **$73\%$ of fashion executives** called generative AI a business priority and **$62\%$** reported company-wide utilization (per NC State's textile roundup), yet **only $28\%$ had tried it in creative processes for design and product development** (Rizzi & Bertola, 2025). By the *State of Fashion 2026* report, **more than $35\%$ of executives** reported using generative AI automation in select functions—online customer service, image creation, copywriting, consumer search, or product discovery (rendered by one industry guide as daily use)—while $64\%$ of retail CEOs ranked AI as their top productivity investment (KPMG *AI in Retail*, Jan 2026) and $40\%$ of workers in developed markets are projected to need reskilling by 2030 (EDITED).

High-profile deployments span luxury and mass retail: Acne Studios with Robbie Barrat (Men's FW 2020), Moncler with Maison Meta (London Fashion Week 2023), Valentino's AI campaign (Jan 2023), the first AI Fashion Week in Brooklyn (April 2023, created by Maison Meta and Revolve, drawing $350+$ submissions / $130+$ collections with winners physically manufactured via an incubator), Collina Strada's AI-assisted garments (NYFW 2024), Christian Cowan's FW 2024 Adobe collaboration (a laser-cut polymer dress with programmable petals), Puma's AI-collaborative Inverse sneaker (Dec 2024), Selkie's Valentine's Day collection, and moodboard/pattern-variation workflows at Louis Vuitton, Dior, Fendi, Revolve, and Gucci. Financial projections vary widely by perimeter: McKinsey estimates generative AI could add **$150\text{–}275\text{ billion}$** in operating earnings to the fashion, apparel, and luxury sector within three to five years, whereas vendor Market.us sizes the direct generative-AI-in-fashion software market at **$176.9\text{ million}$ in 2025**, projecting expansion to **$4,090.8\text{ million}$ by 2035**. Operational speed claims are steep—vendor fashionINSTA markets a $70\%$ design-development acceleration, and a secondary roundup (Gitnux, May 2026, underlying source unstated) claims AI cut Zara's design-to-store lead time by $\sim 40\%$—explaining why competitive pressure pushes studios toward adoption despite creative and legal risks.

## 2. The Homogenization Debate: Mechanisms and Empirical Evidence

### 2.1 Eight Mechanisms of Convergence
The research literature identifies eight distinct pathways through which AI tools narrow creative output, operating before, during, and after the design process:

1. **Shared, Skewed Training Corpora and Preference Tuning (Pre-Creation):** Foundation models are trained on overlapping web-scale datasets dominated by Western, commercial imagery and optimized via human-preference reward models that favor conventional attractiveness. In latent diffusion architectures (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022), an encoder compresses images into a latent space where cross-attention steers noise removal; common statistical regularities become effortless defaults. For example, the CoCoStyle trend-clustering model was trained on *Vogue* runway data from New York, London, Milan, and Paris (Boutier, 2025), and professional designers note AI "operates based on existing content and doesn't conform to uncommon design principles" (Jin et al., *Archives of Design Research*, 2024).
2. **Mode Collapse and Autonomous Generative-Loop Compression (Machine-Level):** Pre-trained models exhibit mode collapse, concentrating probability mass on typical outputs while dropping rare tails. Hintze, Åström, and Schossau (*Patterns*, Jan 2026) linked a text-to-image and image-to-text system in an autonomous caption-to-image loop with no retraining and no new data; regardless of prompt diversity or randomness parameters, outputs rapidly forgot complex prompts (e.g., a prime minister under wartime stress) and converged onto bland, generic cityscapes and interiors—what the authors term **"visual elevator music."** As Rutgers professor Ahmed Elgammal notes, without explicit deviation incentives built into model architectures, generative pipelines naturally strip detail toward the statistical average.
3. **Semantic Attractors, Anchoring, and Cognitive Debt (Point of Creation):** Fluent, polished AI suggestions act as psychological *semantic attractors* that reduce uncertainty and induce design fixation (de Rooij & Biskjaer, 2026; Wadinambiarachchi et al., 2024). Cognitively, MIT Media Lab's "Your Brain on ChatGPT" preprint (Kosmyna et al., June 2025, $N = 54$, contested on headline neural claims) measured weaker neural connectivity, poorer recall, and accumulating "cognitive debt" among AI-assisted writers, turning creators into passive consumers of their own thinking. Similarly, Luchini et al. (Paris Brain Institute, 2025) found human–AI pairs generated fewer original ideas and converged on fewer common themes than human–human pairs.
4. **Prompt, Template, and Interface Convergence (Workflow-Level):** Linear prompt–output workflows create path dependence, while public galleries, preset buttons, and shared prompting recipes cause users to converge. An industry observation notes designers repeatedly typing identical tokens ("Balenciaga campaign," "streetwear fashion"; Glossy / Gruppo Teddy disclosures). In a study of 71 professional, semi-professional, and non-professional designers using online platform tools, Tang et al. (*Sustainable Futures*, Dec 2025) found embedded templates and material libraries strongly pulled non-professionals toward default styles, while professionals resisted better but remained constrained by platform logic. Ledendal, Petersson McIntyre, and Christoforidou (2026, abstract-level walkthrough of four AI fashion platforms) similarly document how interfaces demand prompt fluency while privileging narrow body and aesthetic norms.
5. **Recursive Model Collapse (Post-Creation Feedback Loop):** As AI-generated designs flood the open web (where AI content already exceeds half of new articles, per Elgammal), web scrapers ingest them into subsequent training runs. Shumailov et al. (*Nature*, July 2024) demonstrated mathematically and empirically that models trained recursively on synthetic outputs progressively lose variance and forget the tails of the true distribution (*Communications of the ACM*, March 2026 argues this narrowing is already visible).
6. **Demand-Side Trend-Tool Coordination and Self-Fulfilling Forecasts (Market-Level):** Trend-analytics vendors (Heuritech, WGSN) are few while their brand subscribers are many. When thousands of retail buyers purchase the same ranked trend signals up to a year in advance and lock in production capital before real sales data can correct the forecast, collections land simultaneously with identical silhouettes (a self-fulfilling prophecy; H&M uses AI to quantify case-buying across $\sim 16$ collections a year). As designer Kari Fry warned in *1 Granary* (Feb 2026): *"buying has turned towards a data game, and I think that's frightening."* Adjacent research on **algorithmic monoculture** in deployed hiring systems (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, May 2026) proves that shared decision models produce systemic, synchronized exclusions across firms (requiring a flagged job applicant $\sim 25$ applications to match normal odds), though an MIT follow-up (Sept 2026) notes monoculture effects depend heavily on institutional details.
7. **Automated Trend-Scraping and Output-Flood Dilution (Distribution-Level):** Generative AI and automated scraping collapse the gap between trend detection and manufacturing into seconds (PatSnap, April 2026; Elyon Adede of knitwear label Elexiay in *1 Granary*, Feb 2026). When marginal design costs drop toward zero, fast-fashion platforms and AI dupe-finders flood marketplaces before an independent designer's first production run sells out. The US Copyright Office (Part 3 Report, May 2025) recognizes this **market dilution**—where vast volumes of style-imitative outputs drown out human creators in search and feeds even without copying a specific work—as a cognizable economic harm.
8. **Educational Pipeline and Skill Hollowing (Human-Capital Level):** Across fashion design schools (2023–2025 institutional policies), AI is widely permitted for moodboards and ideation but restricted for final submissions. Educators warn that students who route early ideation and rendering through AI bypass foundational craft training—drawing, draping, pattern intuition—thinning the next cohort's capacity for independent stylistic divergence before they ever enter the market (Vogue, Oct 2026; Lupi's cohort warning).

In the fashion domain, a 2025 Elsevier study ("Nondeterministic evaluations finds converged attitudes towards AI-generated fashion model images across aesthetic sensibilities," ISSN 2095-2635 family, abstract-level) used focus groups across three aesthetic sensibilities and found audience attitudes converged for the "universal beauty" type.

#### Directionality and Cultural Appropriation ("Data Colonialism")
Homogenization is not directionally neutral. As Feng & Kim (2026), Boutier (Glasgow Caledonian University, 2025), and commentary on traditional cultural expressions (TCE) demonstrate, convergence pulls toward a Euro-American commercial center. Boutier documents how prompting for "a beautiful woman" repeatedly returned only European ideals, alongside historical failures like the 2016 Beauty.AI contest (near-uniformly white winners), the Shudu Gram "digital blackface" controversy, and Levi's $\times$ Lalaland.ai (criticized as commodified diversity). Worse, non-Western and indigenous designs (e.g., Maori and Nigerian heritage motifs) sit in a legal blind spot: treated as unowned "public domain" under Western IP law, they are scraped, exoticized into surface motifs, and commercialized at zero cost without community consent or attribution—a dynamic scholars term **data colonialism**. While WIPO's Intergovernmental Committee is debating TCE protections against AI extraction and UNESCO's 2021 AI Ethics Recommendation calls for cultural custodian inclusion, no binding international enforcement mechanism exists yet.

### 2.3 The Counter-Evidence: Why Industry-Wide Homogenization Is Not Inevitable

A confident "yes" to industry-wide garment homogenization is refuted by five distinct lines of evidence:

1. **Passive Exposure vs. Active Co-Creation Reverses the Sign:** Ashkinaze et al. (*ACM Collective Intelligence*, 2025; arXiv:2401.13481) ran a dynamic feed-forward experiment ($N = 844$ participants, $3,414$ responses across 48 countries on an alternative-uses task) where earlier participants' ideas were shown to later ones. High exposure to AI ideas **increased collective semantic diversity** (Cliff's $\delta = 0.26\text{–}0.31$) and accelerated diversity growth without changing individual creativity ("AI made ideas different, not better"). While actively delegating creation to AI narrows outputs (Doshi & Hauser), passively encountering diverse AI stimuli as sparks among human ideas can disrupt human herd behavior.
2. **Stage-Sensitivity and Steering Intensity ("Directors vs. Delegators"):** In *Information Systems Research* ("The Double-Edged Roles of Generative AI," 2025), randomized experiments with 192 students (using Stable Diffusion v1.1) and a field competition with 120 professional graphic designers (using Midjourney v6.1) showed AI improved ideation, helped novices during implementation, but caused experienced designers to spend longer in revision without creative gains ($p = .079$ on revision behavior, interpreted via expertise fixation). Penn State researcher Roger Beaty's distinction holds across the literature: **creative delegators** who accept early prompts converge on generic variations, whereas **creative directors** who sketch first, constrain geometry, and use AI only for material/colorway refinement (like New York designer Jane Wade in her SS 2027 collection, or Merrell design director Ian Cobb using Viscom) preserve or expand variety (as in Rizzi & Bertola's *Focused Collaboration* archetype).
3. **Market Competition Selects for Model Diversity:** Manish Raghavan (*Management Science*, 2026; arXiv:2412.08610) modeled producers competing for consumer attention by choosing among generative AI tools. Theorems 4.4 and 4.5 prove that **stronger competition and penalties for similarity induce more diverse tool equilibria**, bounded by a price of anarchy of 2 (Theorem 4.11). A model that scores highest on an isolated benchmark can lose market share under competition to a lower-scoring model with a wider output distribution (Lemma 5.2)—validated empirically in Scattergories text games (where Phi-3.5 at $68\%$ solo accuracy beat Llama-3.1 at $92\%$ solo accuracy under multi-player competition) and in text-to-image app-icon generation measured via DreamSim perceptual similarity (where top benchmark model CogView4 could not lock out rivals). Délos (*Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism*, 2026) and Alvero et al. (finding 3 of 27 commercial models had zero or negative idealization bias) confirm that model priors are contingent and diversifiable.
4. **Data Curation and Heritage Fine-Tuning Work:** The shared-prior link breaks when communities curate their own training data. Simanjuntak et al. (arXiv, July 2026) fine-tuned Protogen v3.4 and Stable Diffusion v1.4 on high-resolution Indonesian Batak **Ulos woven motifs**, achieving a $\sim 10.5\times$ lower Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and $2.0\times$ higher Inception Score (IS) with Protogen, mapping the fidelity–diversity tradeoff (recommending guidance scale 5–9) with traditional weavers validating cultural integrity. Similarly, Deng, Liu, and Chen (*Electronic Research Archive*, 2023) used MUNIT image-to-image translation to enrich Chinese **Miao ethnic apparel**, alongside parallel heritage initiatives in African print design (Boateng, 2025; Scharff, AAAI 2024) and Saudi Al-Qatt motifs (Abdulghaffar, 2026).
5. **The Pre-AI Fast-Fashion Baseline and the Consumer Brake:** Fashion was heavily homogenized by fast-fashion supply chains and "dupe culture" long before generative AI (e.g., Shein copying Chopova Lowena's carabiner skirt and Simone Rocha's pearl egg clutch; GANNI v. Steve Madden over the Buckle Ballerina shoe; Circana reporting **$58\%$ of shoppers** bought a dupe in 2026, and ASG/Brandshake reporting $71\%$ of Gen Z and $67\%$ of Millennials actively choose dupes). As Refabric co-founder Seda Dominac states, *"Copying did not begin with AI, but it can accelerate the process."* Moreover, while programs like The Fabricant's "Design Forward 2026" and 1 Granary commentators frame AI as a budget equalizer for emerging talent, Latin Fashion Week founder Sobeidy Vidal (April 2026) notes that AI lowers the barrier to *creation* but not to *scale* (capital, distribution, manufacturing).

### 2.4 The Consumer Response Layer: How Shoppers Price Homogenization and Effort
Consumers actively detect and penalize AI-driven sameness, creating a powerful market brake. In general marketing surveys (using distinct instruments and populations that must not be averaged), **$31\%$ of consumers** trust a brand less when they see AI-generated content vs. $7\%$ more (eMarketer, May 2026); **$55\%$ feel less favorable** toward a brand using AI creative vs. $24\%$ more favorable (Clutch, June 2026); **$36\%$ are less likely to engage** with AI content (Menlo Ventures, Sept 2026); and Harris Poll majorities (June 2026) distrust AI ads. After fierce consumer backlash against AI-generated campaigns at Gucci, Levi's, H&M ("AI twins"), FreePeople, Miami Fashion Week, and a *Vogue* Guess ad (Aug 2025, sparking subscription cancellations), brands like Aerie, Equinox, and Almond Breeze launched explicitly anti-AI campaigns in early 2026 (Adweek) and shifted budgets to experiential marketing as an "antidote to AI slop" (Digiday, Dec 2025).

Three peer-reviewed fashion consumer studies quantify the exact psychological mechanisms behind this reaction:
* **Innovativeness Moderation (Lee, Kim, Kim, Huh & Park, *Journal of Fashion Marketing and Management*, 2026, 30(2):390–416):** Across three MTurk experiments building on their 2024 *JRCS* authenticity and 2025 Wiley ascribed-mind studies, consumers consistently preferred human-designed over AI-designed fashion (and Duffek, 2026 notes AI designs are also perceived as less sustainable). Crucially, negative attitudes toward AI designs were strong when product innovativeness was **low** (mediated by lower perceived originality), **but vanished when product innovativeness was high** (deconstruction, avant-garde designs). The market penalizes AI specifically when it produces generic clothes.
* **Brand Positioning and Dual Pathways (Song, Wang, Yang & He [also indexed in Crossref pre-prints as Song, Kim & Park], *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*, Vol. 95 Part A, Feb 2027 [online 2026]):** In a $2\times 2$ experiment ($N = 412$ Chinese adults via Credamo), AI attribution simultaneously raised **perceived design homogeneity** (inferred as a technological deficit and opportunistic cost-cutting) and **perceived trend relevance**, both operating through **perceived manipulative intent** (Persuasion Knowledge Model) to pull brand sincerity and purchase intention in opposite directions. Brand positioning flips the outcome: **the negative homogeneity penalty dominates for avant-garde/designer brands**, whereas the positive trend-relevance path benefits mainstream brands.
* **Human Effort Drives Independent-Brand Purchases (Zhang, Huang, Jia & Cui, *J. Theoretical and Applied Electronic Commerce Research*, Aug 2026, 21(8):270):** In a PLS-SEM survey of $N = 568$ young Chinese consumers interested in AI-generated clothing (AGC) from independent designer brands ($R^2 = 0.401$ for purchase intention, $0.480$ for attitude), **perceived effort of the brand in design (PEBD, $\beta = 0.284, p < .001$)** was the strongest design-related direct driver of purchase intention (alongside overall attitude $\beta = 0.294$ and subjective norms $\beta = 0.198$). For independent designers, visible human creative investment—not better AI prompts—is what converts shoppers.

## 3. The Designer's Vulnerability and the Five Legal Fronts

### 3.1 Labor Displacement and Sector-Specific Channels
Independent creators face measurable economic compression, though quantitative labor studies currently measure general digital freelancing rather than garment construction:
* **Freelance Platform Losses:** Following the release of image- and text-generating AI, Hui, Reshef, and Zhou (*Organization Science*, 35(6), 2024; Brookings, July 2025) found freelancers in affected occupations were **$1.2\%$ less likely to be employed** in a given month, took **$4.7\%$ fewer jobs** conditional on working, and suffered a **$\sim 5\%$ drop in total monthly earnings**. Demirci et al. (2024) measured the demand-side driver: a **$17\%$ decline in image-creation job postings**. Yiu, Seamans, Raj, and Liu (*Organization Science*, 2026) found top performers were hit even harder: each $1\%$ increase in past earnings predicted an additional **$0.5\%$ drop in job opportunities** and **$1.7\%$ drop in monthly income**. In Europe, $65\%$ of Dutch design respondents fear employment decline within five years (Cultuurmonitor, April 2026), while practitioners report a "productivity paradox" of lower rates and endless client iteration demands (*Blood in the Machine*; *It's Nice That*, Sept 2025).
* **The Fashion Digitizability Boundary and Four Real-World Harms:** Because current AI cannot execute pattern-making or physical assembly (Rizzi & Bertola, 2025), direct labor substitution in fashion hits the 2D concept, illustration, and modeling stages rather than 3D tailoring. However, independent designers suffer acute revenue loss through four documented commercial channels:
  1. *Algorithmic Dupe Speed and Channel Cannibalization:* When LA label SUBSURFACE founder Kari Fry had a dress go viral, fast-fashion dupes sold out before she could fulfill orders, and one of her own retail stockists bought the cheaper dupe instead (*1 Granary*, Feb 2026). Similarly, Blogilates/Popflex designer Cassey Ho spent up to three years developing her patented Pirouette Skort, only to see knockoffs flood the market within days after Taylor Swift wore it in 2024.
  2. *Product Imagery Theft + AI Face-Swapping:* Both Fry and Ho documented counterfeiters stealing their original product photography and using generative AI to swap the model's or designer's face into fake advertisements, turning stolen marketing assets into deniable listings.
  3. *Automated Listing Floods:* Illustrator Dani Donovan spent **$\sim\$4,000\text{ per month}$** removing $2,500$ counterfeit listings of her Anti-Planner across Shein, Temu, and Amazon in a single year, driven by automated listing bots (InvestigateTV, Sept 2025).
  4. *Algorithmic Targeting and Damage Evasion:* As alleged in *Perry v. Shein*, ultra-fast-fashion algorithms scrape social media to target independent pieces with the highest commercial potential, produce small initial batches ($10,000$ new items listed daily on $>\$30\text{ billion}$ annual revenue), and layer AI-generated minor variations around copies so each individual SKU stays below the financial threshold where a solo designer can afford to sue.

### 3.2 Decomposing "Designer vs. Algorithm" into Five Legal Fronts
Because an algorithm cannot be sued, every dispute attaches to human or corporate actors (dataset curators, model developers, brands, marketplaces, or users) across five distinct legal fronts:

| Legal Front | What Was Allegedly Taken or Claimed? | Primary Legal Doctrine | Core Structural Limitation in Fashion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Training Data (Input)** | Unlicensed ingestion of lookbooks, sketches, prints, or photos to train or fine-tune models. | Copyright reproduction right vs. Fair Use (US) or Text-and-Data Mining (TDM) exceptions (EU/UK/JP). | Designer often does not own the photographer's copyright in lookbook photos; proving a specific image is inside an opaque training set is difficult. |
| **2. Output Ownership (Registration)** | Designer uses AI to generate or refine a print/garment and seeks IP protection to stop copyists. | Copyright authorship standard ("human authorship" vs. "intellectual creation" vs. computer-generated works). | In the US, pure prompt outputs are uncopyrightable public domain—meaning **anyone can legally copy an AI-generated garment** (e.g., FreePeople critique). |
| **3. Output Infringement & Style Imitation** | AI generates a design "in the style of" an independent designer or reproduces an existing piece. | Copyright substantial similarity / recognizability; French *parasitisme* (unfair competition). | **Style is not copyrightable.** Unless a specific separable print is copied verbatim, statistical recombination of a designer's "look" is generally lawful. |
| **4. ### 4.1 Deep Dive: The Evidentiary Collapse of Unregistered Design Rights Against AI
For independent designers in the UK and EU, unregistered design rights—the UK Supplementary Unregistered Design Right (SUDR) and the EU Unregistered Design (formerly UCD)—were specifically engineered for seasonal fashion. They arise automatically upon public disclosure at zero cost and protect overall product appearance (including 3D cut, texture, and 2D ornamentation) for three years. Under the CJEU's *Karen Millen v. Dunnes Stores* (C-345/13) rule, "individual character" is judged against specific prior designs individually rather than a "mosaicked" composite of commonplace features.

However, in the age of AI fast fashion, this regime suffers from three structural failures documented by Haswanthini Sugumar (*Record of Law*, July 2026) and illustrated in ***Edwards v. boohoo*** ([2025] EWHC 805 (IPEC)):
1. **The Black-Box Causation Problem:** Unlike registered designs, unregistered design rights are not monopolies; the claimant **must prove the defendant causally copied the design**, and independent creation is an absolute defense. In *Edwards v. boohoo*, a self-represented independent designer sued Boohoo over five garments under UKUDR. The court held that while UKUDR can subsist in shape-stable garment elements off the body (such as a puff sleeve or 3D ruching, pleating, and shirring, though not a multi-way bikini whose shape depends on the wearer's body), **Ms. Edwards lost on infringement because she could not prove Boohoo copied her**. Deputy Judge Mitcheson KC noted that when fast-fashion firms add $800+$ designs per week, arriving at similar clothing by chance is "completely unsurprising"—and Boohoo's employee churn and deleted design records made tracing copying impossible. When copying is mediated by an opaque trend-scraping algorithm (the Shein model), proving causal derivation without internal system discovery is virtually impossible.
2. **Probabilistic Recombination vs. Narrow Infringement Scope:** Even when a design qualifies for unregistered protection, infringement requires the copy to produce the same overall impression with at most minor differences. In ***Rosae Paris v. Seven August*** (Paris Judicial Court, April 2024), four of Rosae's six garments qualified for EU unregistered design protection, yet infringement failed because the accused garments varied the amplitude, gathering, and placement of ruffles (and copyright failed separately because combining commonplace features like oversized cuts and shoulder ruffles did not meet the *Cofemel* personality standard). Generative AI models that recombine statistical features across thousands of garments easily clear the *Karen Millen* individual-character bar while evading infringement through minor automated variations.
3. **Prior-Art Flooding and Paper-Trail Perversity:** Because human authorship is embedded in design originality (*THJ Systems v. Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354), purely AI-generated designs get no unregistered protection, creating perverse incentives for users to manufacture fake paper trails of human input. Simultaneously, AI's ability to generate millions of designs instantly threatens to flood the prior-art landscape, making it harder for *human* designers to prove their work has "individual character" over existing public designs.

This exact risk prompted the UK Intellectual Property Office's **Consultation on Changes to the UK Designs Framework (CP 1401 / CP 1357, Sept 4–Nov 27, 2025)**. In Section D, the UKIPO's **preferred Option 2 is to abolish protection for computer-generated designs without a human author**—representing the only instance in global policy where preventing AI volume from crowding out human designs is cited as a formal regulatory rationale to strip an IP right. CP 1401 also proposes collecting mandatory or optional **AI-use declarations** on registered design applications (Option 3, creating the first registry-level dataset on AI design use, backed by potential bad-faith cancellation sanctions), consolidating UKUDR and SUDR into a single unregistered right (Section F), evaluating criminal sanctions for unregistered design infringement (Section H), and adding registered designs to the low-cost IPEC small-claims track (Section I).

### 4.2 The International Treaty Constraint: The Berne Three-Step Test
National AI training exceptions do not operate in a vacuum. In a landmark analysis, Lucchi, Dornis, and Sierek (*American Business Law Journal*, Vol. 63, pp. 265–298, 2026) demonstrate that the **Berne Convention Article 9(2) and TRIPS Article 13 "three-step test"** places a hard international-law ceiling on TDM exceptions (such as EU CDSM Art. 4 and Japan Art. 30-4):
* **Step 1 (Certain Special Cases):** Regimes permitting unlimited commercial scraping of the entire public internet fail Step 1 because they are neither narrow nor special.
* **Step 2 (No Conflict with Normal Exploitation — The Absolute Threshold):** Step 2 operates as a strict threshold, not a balancing test. "Normal exploitation" covers both upstream training-licensing markets and downstream market substitution. When AI outputs flood a creative sector and economically invalidate human work via **indirect substitution / market dilution**—even without copying any single work's protected expression—the training exception conflicts with normal exploitation and fails Step 2.
* **Step 3 (No Unreasonable Prejudice / Compensation):** Crucially, **statutory levies or flat remuneration schemes belong only to Step 3 and cannot cure a Step 2 violation**. Therefore, compulsory levies or broad opt-out exceptions (like EU Art. 4(3)) cannot legally sustain large-scale commercial generative AI training without prior authorization and technical market-substitution safeguards (output filtering, refusal mechanisms). Ironically, US fair use—because its fourth factor dynamically weighs market dilution—may comply with the treaty better than the EU's codified TDM exception unless the EU reforms its framework.

### 6.2 Detailed Analysis of Key Resolution Mechanisms

#### 1. Closing the Licensing Arithmetic Gap and Building Collective Infrastructure
The most striking empirical finding in the resolution landscape is the **five-order-of-magnitude spread** between voluntary licensing payouts and litigation settlements:
* **Voluntary Prospective Licensing:** Adobe trained Firefly on licensed Adobe Stock and public-domain content (offering enterprise indemnification to buyers, though Stock contributors had no opt-out on submitted stock assets). Contributors on the Microstockgroup forum calculated the 2024 Firefly training bonus at roughly **$\$0.029\text{ per image}$** (single-source contributor calculation, not an official Adobe figure; Shutterstock operates a similar Contributor Fund via its six-year OpenAI partnership, and 14 major UK content licensing deals were recorded in 2024).
* **Litigated Retroactive Settlement:** In *Bartz v. Anthropic* (approved July 20, 2026), settling claims over pirated book libraries yielded $\$1.5\text{ billion}$ across $\sim 500,000$ works—approximately **$\$3,000\text{ per work}$**.

Because suing over unlicensed ingestion pays roughly $100,000\times$ more per work than current micro-licensing, rational rightsholders with resources litigate rather than license. To make voluntary licensing viable (as urged by USCO Part 3 and the UK government's March 2026 report), the industry requires **collective licensing societies** for visual and fashion designers—modeled on the Artists Rights Society (2023) proposals, Senftleben's (2025) AI output levy redistribution model, or the UK's **Creative Content Exchange pilot** (targeted for summer 2026)—alongside private procurement verification like **Fairly Trained** certification. However, per a July 2026 *PNAS* feasibility study (noting publisher opposition to compulsory schemes) and Lucchi et al.'s (2026) treaty analysis, licensing contracts must be granular: specifying whether permission covers base-model training, private fine-tuning, or commercial product generation, and pricing access via auditable upfront/tier fees rather than unmeasurable fractional output royalties. Furthermore, licensing programs must actively fund underrepresented cultural archives so that "commercially safe" models do not restrict training to homogeneous Western stock catalogs.

#### 2. Statutory Reforms: Style Rights, Model Likeness Templates, and Transparency Bills
Where copyright leaves gaps, targeted legislation is emerging:
* **The Likeness Template (Enacted for Models, Missing for Designers):** Following the Model Alliance campaign and Cornell Worker Institute's *"Fashion's Data Doubles"* report (Sept 2025), **New York's Fashion Workers Act (effective June 19, 2025)** requires explicit written consent specifying scope, purpose, pay rate, and duration before creating or using a model's AI digital replica, backed by $\$3,000\text{–}\$5,000$ civil penalties and a six-year private right of action (mirrored by California's AB 2602 and AB 1836, effective Jan 1, 2025, and SAG-AFTRA's 2026 collective bargaining agreements setting a minimum $1.5\times$ session fee for digital replicas). As Duquesne Law commentary (Oct 2026) notes, this treats a worker's identity as a licensable asset—the exact legal structure independent designers still lack for their designs.
* **Proposed US Style and Transparency Legislation:** On June 2, 2026, bipartisan lawmakers (backed by Adobe) introduced the **CREATOR Act (H.R. 9112)**, granting visual artists an exclusive right to authorize commercial exploitation of intentional AI impersonations of their "signature style" (parallel to a Senate digital-replica property bill advanced in June 2026). Critics (e.g., *Tedium*, June 6, 2026; USCO Part 1, July 2024) warn that defining "style" risks the interpretive chaos of music soundalike litigation and favors wealthy incumbent houses. To solve the black-box proof problem, the proposed **CLEAR Act (S. 3813 / H.R. 7209)** would mandate federal notices disclosing copyrighted works used in AI training.
* **EU AI Act and GPAI Code of Practice Stratification:** In the EU, Article 53 obligations for General-Purpose AI (GPAI) providers took effect on **August 2, 2025** (with Commission enforcement powers active **August 2, 2026**, and a grace period until **August 2, 2027** for legacy models placed on the market before Aug 2, 2025). Providers must publish training-content summaries using the Commission's mandatory template (July/Aug 2025) and respect machine-readable TDM opt-outs (such as W3C's `TDMRep` specification or `noai`/`noimageai` tags). The voluntary **GPAI Code of Practice (July 2025)** adds operational teeth: an accessible copyright complaint mechanism and technical filters against memorized outputs. However, coverage is stratified: while OpenAI, Google, Meta (with reservations), Microsoft, and Anthropic committed to the Code, **specialist visual tools heavily used in fashion—Midjourney and Stability AI—were not confirmed signatories**, leaving them subject to baseline statutory duties without the Code's streamlined complaint machinery.

#### 3. Technical Provenance, Developer Governance, and Machine Unlearning Limits
Technical interventions operate on three distinct planes, each with strict boundaries:
* **Provenance vs. EU Digital Product Passports:** **C2PA Content Credentials** (v2.2) attach cryptographic, tamper-evident metadata recording an asset's creation and human editing history. While invaluable for proving human authorship to the US Copyright Office and signaling human craft to consumers, C2PA proves *process*, not legal title, and social platforms frequently strip metadata upon upload (Goedecke, July 2026; TrueScreen, March 2026). In fashion specifically, the EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR, approved June 2024) mandates a QR-based **Digital Product Passport (DPP) for all textiles sold in the EU by 2027**; industry groups are actively lobbying to extend this per-garment digital fingerprint from sustainability tracking to IP authenticity verification and anti-dupe takedowns.
* **Adversarial Self-Help (Glaze and Nightshade):** Developed by Ben Zhao's SAND Lab at the University of Chicago, **Glaze** applies imperceptible pixel perturbations ("style cloaks") to artwork before web publication so AI models misclassify its style (e.g., reading an impressionist print as pop art), while **Nightshade** embeds poison samples that corrupt concept associations inside future models that scrape the image without consent. While integrated into artist platform Cara and invaluable in jurisdictions without legal opt-outs (like Japan under Art. 30-4), the SAND Lab's own August 2026 FAQs and *MIT Technology Review* (Oct 2024) note that Glaze does not consistently stop image-to-image tools, works only prospectively, and faces an ongoing bypass/fine-tuning arms race.
* **Developer Governance and the "Illusion of Unlearning":** Developer policies vary wildly: OpenAI blocks living-artist prompts in DALL·E/GPT-Image, yet its announced creator opt-out tool (**Media Manager**, May 2024) stalled and never launched; Stability AI uses website opt-out lists and output filters; and Midjourney (Terms effective **May 27, 2026**) offers no artist opt-out, disclaimers non-infringement, makes outputs public/remixable by default unless on conditional Stealth plans, and grants the platform broad license rights. Fashion tool **Resleeve** (Terms effective **June 15, 2026**) restricts training on private brand uploads without consent but relies on third-party foundation models under their own terms. When infringing data is already inside a model, **machine unlearning** in diffusion models is an active research frontier (Alberti et al., ICLR 2025), but recent studies prove that unlearned concepts can resurface after minor fine-tuning (*The Illusion of Unlearning*, George et al., CVPR 2025) and continual unlearning requests degrade overall model utility (ICLR 2026)—meaning negotiated settlements must specify API filters, weight recalls, and re-testing rather than trusting a simple "deletion" promise.

#### 4. The Theoretical Fork: Stronger IP vs. The "Piracy Paradox"
Underlying every policy proposal is an unresolved structural debate. In their classic theory, Kal Raustiala and Christopher Sprigman (*Virginia Law Review*, 2006; *The Knockoff Economy*, 2012; extended to generative AI in a Jan 2023 *LA Times* op-ed) argue that fashion's lack of copyright for garment silhouettes is actually an **innovation engine** (*the piracy paradox*). Because clothing is a positional good, rapid copying saturates a trend, induces obsolescence, and forces designers at the top of the market to invent new silhouettes continuously. Furthermore, if courts tighten infringement standards—such as Advocate General Szpunar's proposed **"recognizability" test** in *Mio/konektra* (imported from *Pelham I*, where copying any recognizable element infringes)—the JIPLP (2025) authors warn it would expose everyday fashion designers to crippling liability for sampling fashion's shared visual vocabulary.

**Why AI May Invert the Paradox:** The piracy paradox assumed human supply-chain speeds, where the originating designer enjoyed a several-month lead time to recoup R&D costs and build brand equity before knockoffs arrived. When algorithmic trend-scraping and generative variation compress months of development into seconds (*1 Granary*, Feb 2026) and flood retail channels *before* the independent designer's first production run even ships, induced obsolescence collapses into **pure predatory extraction**. Under algorithmic speed, the innovator captures zero first-mover return. Because empirical research has not yet tested whether the piracy paradox survives zero-marginal-cost algorithmic copying, the safest resolution architecture is one that works under both theories: leaving basic garment silhouettes unmonopolized by copyright so the shared vocabulary stays open, while aggressively enforcing **trademark/brand identity**, **cheap multi-design registrations**, **supply-chain speed**, and **aggregated anti-scraping remedies (RICO / *parasitisme* / algorithmic transparency)** against systematic free-riders.

## 7. Actionable Playbooks: Studio Anti-Homogenization & Dispute Resolution

### 7.1 Studio Playbook: Preserving Creative Diversity and Market Value
Independent designers and brand creative directors can defeat homogenization and maximize consumer willingness to pay by implementing five evidence-backed workflow rules:

1. **Enforce the "Refinement, Not Generation" Rule (Sketch First):** Following the cognitive-fixation findings (Wadinambiarachchi et al., 2024; Kosmyna et al., 2025; Dhawan and Wade in *Vogue*, Oct 2026), require designers to complete independent physical draping, material research, or hand sketches *before* opening an AI tool. Use sketch-conditioned tools (e.g., Viscom, NewArc, StyleMe) or *Focused Collaboration* workflows (Rizzi & Bertola, 2025) to explore colorways and fabrications rather than prompting text-to-image models from a blank page.
2. **Actively Counter Default Body and Cultural Priors:** Because text-to-image models default $100\%$ to youthful, smooth-skinned, slim, Euro-centric ideals and reduce non-Western traditions to stereotyped surface ornaments (Feng & Kim, 2026; Alvero et al., 2026), explicitly steer prompts and reference inputs across age, body geometry, skin texture, and structural garment construction rather than relying on cultural shorthand tokens.
3. **Fine-Tune on Personal or Community-Curated Archives:** Where a label has a sufficient body of past work, train a custom LoRA adapter or private model on the studio's own archive (or partner with cultural custodians using the Simanjuntak et al., 2026 Ulos methodology, balancing fidelity and diversity at guidance scales 5–9). This simultaneously breaks the shared cross-user semantic attractor and guarantees $100\%$ copyright-clean training provenance—while auditing outputs to ensure the brand does not trap itself in narrow self-repetition.
4. **Aim AI at High-Innovativeness Concepts and Guard Merchandising Selection:** Because consumer penalties against AI fashion vanish for highly innovative, avant-garde, and deconstructed designs (Lee et al., *JFMM*, 2026), never use AI to churn out basic staples. Audit diversity at the **merchandising and buying stage**, ensuring commercial buyers do not use shared trend forecasts (Heuritech/WGSN) to filter a diverse studio concept pool back down to safe market averages.
5. **Market Visible Human Effort, Not AI Novelty:** Based on Song et al. (*JRCS*, 2027) and Zhang et al. (*JTAER*, 2026, where perceived brand design effort $\beta = 0.284$ directly drove purchase intent while AI creativity $\beta = 0.038$ had zero direct effect), independent and avant-garde brands must frame AI strictly as a subordinate assistant and foreground human craft, tailoring, and design effort in all consumer storytelling.

### 7.2 Legal & Technical Playbook: Protecting Rights and Resolving Disputes
When selecting tools, protecting seasonal collections, or responding to suspected algorithmic copying, independent studios should execute a structured sequence:

#### Phase A: Pre-Publication Prevention & Registration Stack
* **Audit Tool Contracts by Specific Model:** Never assume a platform subscription guarantees exclusive ownership or indemnity. Check whether the specific model selected is trained on licensed data (e.g., Adobe Firefly native models vs. third-party partner models; Fairly Trained certified models) and verify confidentiality terms so uploaded studio sketches do not enter public remix galleries (as in default Midjourney plans) or train third-party models.
* **Maintain a Human-Authorship Audit Trail:** Because US copyright (USCO Part 2, Jan 2025) and EU/French standards protect only visible human creative choices, log every stage of AI-assisted work: initial hand sketches, prompt chains, in-painting/Photoshop layer edits, and physical 3D CLO3D/draping modifications (note that under the *Vegap v. Mango* transformative logic analyzed in JIPLP 2025, documented human personality at each step can convert raw outputs into protectable designs). Bind these logs with **C2PA Content Credentials**.
* **Register Early Where Registration Eliminates the Copying-Proof Burden:**
  * *In the US:* Register separable 2D textile prints and illustrations with the US Copyright Office **within three months of first publication** to preserve statutory damages and attorney's fees under **17 U.S.C. § 412** (disclaiming raw AI portions). Reserve US design patents for enduring signature hardware or footwear icons.
  * *In the EU and UK:* Do not rely solely on 3-year unregistered design rights (UCD/SUDR), which fail against algorithmic fast fashion due to the *Edwards v. boohoo* copying-proof barrier. Use the reformed **EU Design Act** multi-design registration (up to 50 designs per application at low fees) or UK registered designs (up to 2–100 per application, valid up to 25 years, with a 6-month post-disclosure grace period, though pre-disclosure filing is safer). Registered designs are infringed by any garment creating the same overall impression **without needing to prove the defendant copied**.
* **Deploy Machine-Readable Reservations & Cloaking:** Embed W3C `TDMRep` protocols, `robots.txt` blocks (legally recognized under Japan's Art. 30-4 guidelines), and `noai` meta tags on studio websites, and apply **Glaze** cloaking to portfolio images before posting to social media or host on Glaze-integrated platforms like **Cara**. Sell through marketplaces that police AI flooding (e.g., **Depop**'s ban on AI-generated listings; **Etsy**'s July 2024 Creativity Standards).

#### Phase B: Step-by-Step Dispute Escalation Protocol
When a designer discovers their work has been ingested or replicated:
1. **Separate the Asset, Act, and Actor:** Identify precisely what was taken before sending a notice:
   * *Scenario 1 (Copied 2D Floral Print on a Fast-Fashion Dress):* The claim is direct copyright infringement of a separable surface design against the brand/marketplace. You do **not** need to prove how their AI training pipeline works; preserve dated source files, registration certificates, and a purchased physical sample of the infringing garment.
   * *Scenario 2 (Garment Silhouette or "Minimalist Look" Copied Without 2D Print):* Copyright will fail in the US. Check for UK/EU registered design rights, trade dress (if the silhouette has secondary meaning), or, if sold in France, bring an unfair-competition tort claim for ***parasitisme*** (free-riding on commercial investment).
   * *Scenario 3 (Unreleased Studio Archive Leaked via Supplier AI):* Pursue breach of contract, confidentiality, and trade-secret misappropriation against the supplier/platform using server access logs, rather than a web-scraping theory.
   * *Scenario 4 (Stolen Product Photo with AI-Swapped Model Face):* File copyright takedowns over the underlying photograph (confirming whether the studio or commissioned photographer holds title) alongside deceptive-endorsement and state digital-replica claims.
2. **Use Low-Cost Takedown, Complaint, and Small-Claims Channels:**
   * File **DMCA Section 512** takedown notices (17 U.S.C. § 512) with e-commerce marketplaces and social platforms to halt sales immediately.
   * For EU training or output disputes involving signatory GPAI providers, file a formal notice through the **EU GPAI Code of Practice copyright complaint channel** demanding training-data provenance verification and output blocking.
   * For US copyright claims with damages under **$\$30,000$**, file in the streamlined **US Copyright Claims Board (CCB)** (noting respondents can opt out); in the UK, utilize the **IPEC small-claims track** (expected to expand to registered designs under CP 1401).
3. **Aggregate for Systemic Litigation & Specify Technical Settlement Terms:** Solo designers cannot outspend a $\$30\text{ billion}$ platform in federal court. Pool claims with industry coalitions or class counsel using the *Perry v. Shein* civil RICO template or copyright class actions (*Andersen*). When negotiating a settlement or model correction, do not accept vague promises to "delete" an image—require concrete commitments covering source-dataset removal, prompt/output similarity filtering, API blocklists, verification testing across paraphrased prompts (to prevent *Illusion of Unlearning* relapse), and strict non-training clauses for future model versions.

## 8. Open Questions and Pending Milestones

While mechanisms at the model, cognitive, and consumer levels are now well-documented, settling the industry-wide and doctrinal questions depends on specific empirical studies and legal milestones pending as of late October 2026:

1. **Longitudinal Garment-Catalog Stylometry:** No computer-vision study has yet tracked silhouette geometry, color-palette entropy, and structural diversity across runway and e-commerce garment catalogs before and after 2022–2023, comparing AI-adopting brands against non-adopting controls (adapting geographical diversity frameworks like **GeoDiv**, ICLR 2026, and testing Raghavan's model-diversification prediction).
2. **Multi-Model Replication of Fashion-Imagery Audits:** Expanding Feng & Kim's (2026) Midjourney experiment across multiple generators and time points, and securing full-text path coefficients for Song et al.'s (2027) consumer homogeneity experiment and the 2025 Elsevier focus-group study.
3. **Empirical Test of the Piracy Paradox Under Algorithmic Speed:** Measuring whether zero-marginal-cost, real-time algorithmic copying permanently destroys independent designers' first-mover returns or triggers new differentiation cycles.
4. **US Generative Fair Use & Authorship Rulings:** Summary judgment outcomes in *Andersen v. Stability AI / Midjourney*, *Disney/Universal v. Midjourney*, and the *NYT v. OpenAI* MDL (testing whether lawful-source training is fair use or fails under Factor 4 market dilution), alongside the district court ruling in *Suryast v. Perlmutter* on whether building/customizing an AI tool confers human authorship.
5. **Appellate Dispositions in *Getty* (UK) and *MetaBirkins* (US 2d Cir.):** The English Court of Appeal's review of *Getty v. Stability AI* (on intangible "articles" and secondary infringement) and the Second Circuit's long-pending opinion in *Hermès v. Rothschild* (defining the boundary between trademark enforcement and artistic expression in digital/AI fashion).
6. **Shein Discovery & UKIPO CP 1401 Final Response:** Whether discovery in follow-on suits (*Giana v. Shein*) reveals if fast-fashion pipelines generate designs with AI or purely scrape and route human trends; and whether the UK government formally enacts CP 1401's preferred repeal of computer-generated design protection, consolidates UKUDR/SUDR, introduces AI-use registry declarations, and opens the IPEC small-claims track.
7. **Viability of the UK Creative Content Exchange & EU Treaty Challenges:** Whether the UK's summer 2026 licensing exchange pilot prices creator compensation high enough to bridge the $\$0.029\text{ vs. }\$3,000$ gap, and whether rightsholders challenge the EU's commercial TDM opt-out (CDSM Art. 4) under the Berne Convention's three-step test.
