# Horizontal Gene Transfer in Plants and Animals: Mechanisms, Frequency, Evidence Standards, and Evolutionary Significance

## 1. Executive Answer

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT)—the movement of genetic material between organisms by routes other than parent-to-offspring inheritance—is a verified, recurring source of evolutionary innovation in both plants and animals. However, its genome-wide magnitude sits an order of magnitude below that of prokaryotes: in multicellular eukaryotes, detectably transferred genes typically account for **"at most a few percent" of nuclear genes** (Keeling 2024), compared to tens of percent or an average of ~81% of genes having horizontal history in bacteria (Li et al. 2026). Across 218 insect genomes, for example, 1,410 transferred genes represent a pooled fraction of just ~0.0502% (about 5 per 10,000 screened genes; derived from Li et al. 2022). 

Three core premises embedded in the question require immediate recalibration:

1. **Rarity is not uniform, and it depends on what is counted.** While functional nuclear gene transfers are rare as a share of any single genome, HGT is common by *incidence*: 88% of surveyed insect species (192 of 218; Li et al. 2022) and 90.1% of 1,496 surveyed eukaryotic genomes (Li et al. 2026) carry at least one transferred sequence. Furthermore, frequency is an ecological and lifestyle variable rather than a kingdom constant. Asexual bdelloid rotifers (~8% to 11.5% foreign genes; Wilson et al. 2024; Nowell et al. 2024), root-knot nematodes (≥3.34%; Paganini et al. 2012), parasitic plants (>600 plant-to-plant events; Mariault et al. 2025), and plant mitochondrial genomes (*Amborella* carries six whole foreign mitochondrial genome equivalents; Rice et al. 2013) sit orders of magnitude above vertebrates (19 teleost-to-teleost gene events across 242 fish genomes, or 0.02% of ortholog groups; Policarpo et al. 2025) and mammals (virtually zero confirmed heritable nuclear coding transfers in humans; Salzberg 2017).
2. **Rarity does not imply that transfers occur *for* an adaptive reason.** The intuition that "because it is rare, it must be an interesting adaptation" reverses the causality. Rarity is the arithmetic product of sequential barriers: germline sequestration, intracellular immune silencing (such as the germline piRNA pathway), incompatible gene architecture (lack of introns and promoters), and natural selection that rarely favors single imported enzymes in complex developmental systems. Most transferred DNA is neutral or deleterious and rapidly erodes into nonfunctional pseudogenes (such as ~68.3% of acquired genes in the grass *Alloteropsis semialata*, bulk *Wolbachia* inserts in insects, and *Amborella*'s silent foreign mitochondrial genomes; Collins et al. 2026; Dunning Hotopp 2011; Rice et al. 2013). The famous adaptive cases—whitefly toxin resistance, fern shade photoreceptors, aphid carotenoids, nematode parasitism enzymes, fish antifreeze proteins, the vertebrate visual cycle's IRBP, and mammalian placental syncytins—are a **survivorship-biased minority**. We detect them today precisely because selection preserved the rare transfers that offered an immediate, modular solution during an ecological transition, while the vast denominator of failed or neutral transfers decayed beyond recognition.
3. **Eukaryotic HGT is not a brand-new discovery, though genome-wide quantification and active mechanisms are.** Somatic bacterial T-DNA incorporation into plant tumors was demonstrated in 1977 (Chilton et al. 1977), cnidarian transposable element (TE) transfers in 1997 (Robertson 1997), nematode cellulases in 1998, and beetle *Wolbachia* inserts in 2002 (Kondo et al. 2002). What changed in the last decade—and especially between 2021 and 2026—is systematic genome-scale screening, a rigorous methodological reckoning that Exposed major contamination artifacts (tardigrades, sea slugs, human claims, and ~70% of old interkingdom plant claims; Aguirre-Carvajal et al. 2025), and the direct identification of physical transfer vehicles, including cell-to-cell plastid migration across plant grafts (Hertle et al. 2021), fusogen-equipped *Maverick* virus-like transposons in nematodes (Widen et al. 2023), and *Starship* giant transposons in fungi (2025 preprint).

## 2. Definitions, Scope, and the Six Categories of "Horizontal Transfer"

In the contemporary literature, HGT (used interchangeably with lateral gene transfer, LGT) is defined as the movement of genetic material between organisms outside the context of parent-to-offspring reproduction (Keeling 2024; Mariault et al. 2025; Van Etten & Ettema 2020). 

Because the prompt asks about plants and animals as "non-microbial systems," a critical scope distinction is required: **"non-microbial systems" refers to the multicellular recipients, not the absence of microbes.** While direct eukaryote-to-eukaryote transfers occur (plant-to-plant, hornwort-to-fern, plant-to-whitefly, fish-to-fish, nematode-to-nematode, fungus-to-insect/plant/oomycete), the majority of documented transfers into plants and animals originate from bacterial, fungal, or viral donors, or rely on microbial vectors. Excluding microbial donors would discard the bulk of the plant and animal HGT record. Conversely, microbial eukaryotes (protists, fungi, oomycetes, and extremophile unicellular algae) are included here specifically where they calibrate mechanisms, barriers, and evolutionary models.

Headline HGT numbers frequently conflict because six distinct biological processes get grouped under the label. Each answers a different evolutionary question:

| Process Category | Genetic Cargo & Route | Standing in Plants & Animals | How Mixing It Distorts the Record |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Nuclear coding-gene HGT** | Protein-coding genes (from microbes or other eukaryotes) integrated into host chromosomes and inherited via the germline. | Rare as a % of the genome ("at most a few percent"); concentrated in specific ecological niches. | **The core subject of this report.** |
| **2. Organellar HGT** | Mitochondrial or plastid DNA (up to whole organelle genomes) crossing between species. | Plant mitochondria are exceptionally active (*Amborella*, *Lophophytum*); animal mitochondria show virtually none; plastids cross graft junctions. | Inflates plant HGT volume if merged with nuclear genes; organellar acquisitions follow different recombination and expression rules and are often entirely neutral. |
| **3. Horizontal Transposon Transfer (HTT)** | Self-mobilizing "jumping" DNA (retrotransposons like *BovB*, DNA transposons like *SPIN* or *mariner*). | Quantitatively the largest DNA flow between animals (≥2,248 insect events in 10 Myr; ≥975 vertebrate events). | Inflates adaptive gene-transfer claims; TEs propagate selfishly for their own transmission without needing to benefit the host, though some are later domesticated. |
| **4. Viral Capture / Endogenization (EVEs)** | Retroviral or DNA-virus genes integrated into the host germline and exapted (repurposed). | Common and deeply consequential: mammalian *syncytin* genes (placenta) and parasitoid wasp polydnaviruses. | Excluding it removes the strongest functional vertebrate cases (embryonic-lethal knockouts); including it without qualification blurs viral infection with cellular gene transfer. |
| **5. Endosymbiotic Gene Transfer (EGT)** | Ancient, intracellular migration of genes from the bacterial ancestors of mitochondria and plastids into the host nucleus. | Universal to all eukaryotes; foundational to eukaryogenesis. | Must be excluded from contemporary HGT counts (Keeling 2024 proposes refined terms *EGT*, *hetero-EGT*, and *hetero-EPT*). Note: lost organelles leave essentially no detectable nuclear footprint beyond genes still serving an organelle. |
| **6. Somatic / Transient Uptake & Hybridization** | Bacterial DNA in somatic/tumor cells; mobile RNAs; stolen organelles (*kleptoplasty*); or sexual introgression between relatives. | Measurable in human tumors (*Pseudomonas* rRNA), grafted plants (mRNAs/sRNAs), and photosynthetic sea slugs (stolen plastids). | **Not heritable HGT.** Somatic insertions die with the individual; kleptoplasty moves organelles, not nuclear genes; sexual hybridization/introgression mimics HGT trees unless divergence time (>124 Myr in fish) rules out mating. |

### The Eleven Documented Directions of Transfer
Across the plant and animal literature, eleven directional pathways are supported by genomic evidence:
1. **Bacteria → Plant** (land-plant terrestrialization pulses, *Agrobacterium* T-DNA in sweet potato, *TaCSP* in wheat).
2. **Fungus → Plant** (*Fhb7* blight resistance in wheat, microbial terpene synthases in liverworts, ammonium transporters).
3. **Plant → Parasitic Plant** (via haustoria in *Cuscuta*, *Striga*, *Orobanchaceae*, and *Lophophytum*—the richest single plant route).
4. **Parasitic Plant → Host Plant** (an intronless *Striga* gene transferred into sorghum via an mRNA intermediate; Yoshida et al. 2010).
5. **Plant → Plant (Non-parasitic)** (hornwort-to-fern *neochrome* plus secondary fern-to-fern transfers; grass-to-grass blocks up to 170 kb in *Alloteropsis*).
6. **Plant → Insect** (49 plant-derived genes from ≥24 events in the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*, including *BtPMaT1*).
7. **Bacteria → Insect / Arachnid / Nematode** (*HhMAN1* mannanase in coffee berry borers, pectinases in leaf beetles, *KYNU* and courtship gene *LOC105383139* in lepidopterans, cysteine synthase in spider mites, cell-wall enzymes in nematodes).
8. **Fungus → Animal** (aphid, spider-mite, and gall-midge carotenoid pathway genes; coral *natterin* toxin).
9. **Virus → Animal** (mammalian *syncytins*, parasitoid wasp nudivirus/iridovirus domestications, lepidopteran parasitoid-killing factor).
10. **Animal → Animal** (herring-to-smelt antifreeze protein; 19 teleost-to-teleost gene events; *Maverick*-borne protease/kinase cargo between nematodes; insect-to-*Hydra* *mariner* TEs; snake-to-frog *BovB* retrotransposons).
11. **Bacteria → Chordate / Vertebrate** (*IRBP* visual-cycle gene in the vertebrate/chordate ancestor; *Ci-CesA* cellulose synthase in tunicates).

*Notable absences in the literature:* **Eukaryote → Microbe** back-transfers are rare compared to microbe-to-eukaryote transfers (Dunning Hotopp 2011; though *Drosomycin*-type antifungal peptides and certain viral captures exist), and **Animal → Plant** transfers are virtually absent from the validated record (save for a putative animal-derived actinoporin in moss; Ma et al. 2022). Whether this reflects biological barriers—animal cells rarely sit in sustained contact with plant meristems—or sampling bias remains unsettled.

## 3. Historical Context: Correcting the "New Discovery" Premise

The perception that plant and animal HGT is a brand-new discovery is partly an artifact of sequencing history. As Dunning Hotopp (2011) noted, early clusters of HGT reports in arthropods and nematodes reflected where sequence data were abundant, while poorly sampled groups (such as amoebae, ciliates, dinoflagellates, and non-parasitic excavates; Husnik & McCutcheon 2018) lacked reports due to sampling bias. 

| Year | Milestone / Discovery | Significance & Subsequent Standing |
|---|---|---|
| **1977** | *Agrobacterium* T-DNA shown to incorporate into plant tumor cells (Chilton et al. 1977). | Proved the eukaryotic cellular boundary is permeable to bacterial DNA (somatic transformation). |
| **1997** | Insect *mariner* transposons (4 subfamilies) detected by PCR in the cnidarian *Hydra vulgaris* (Robertson 1997). | Among the earliest metazoan HTT records; donor direction (insect → *Hydra*) resolved in 2020 (Grace & Carr 2020). |
| **1998** | Doolittle formulates the *"You are what you eat"* ratchet hypothesis; first bacterial cellulases reported in plant-parasitic nematodes (Smant et al. 1998; Jones et al. 2005). | Framed dietary phagotrophy as an HGT driver; established nematodes as the oldest animal functional coding-gene HGT system. |
| **2002–2004** | First *Wolbachia* → insect X-chromosome insert in bean beetles (Kondo et al. 2002); tunicate cellulose synthase (*Ci-CesA*) linked to bacterial HGT (Nakashima et al. 2004); *Amborella* shown to carry 26 foreign plant mitochondrial genes (Bergthorsson et al. 2004). | Established endosymbiont-to-nuclear transfer, chordate body-plan HGT, and plant mitochondrial promiscuity. |
| **2007–2008** | Near-complete ~1.4 Mb *Wolbachia* genome found in *Drosophila ananassae* (Dunning Hotopp et al. 2007); massive HGT reported in bdelloid rotifers (Gladyshev et al. 2008); *SPIN* transposon jumps across tetrapods (Pace et al. 2008); fish antifreeze HGT proposed (Graham et al. 2008). | Shifted animal HGT from isolated curiosities to multi-species phenomena; also saw the 2008 PCR/FISH claim of algal nuclear genes in the sea slug *Elysia chlorotica* (Rumpho et al. 2008). |
| **2009–2012** | Experimental genetic exchange across tobacco graft junctions (Stegemann & Bock 2009, 2012); aphid fungal carotenoid genes tied to red/green color (Moran & Jarvik 2010); coffee berry borer bacterial mannanase *HhMAN1* (Acuña et al. 2012); 57 gene families tied to land-plant origin (Yue et al. 2012). | Established the "gold standard" of phenotype-linked animal HGT and experimental graft transfer. |
| **2013–2014** | *Elysia chlorotica* egg-genome sequencing finds zero algal nuclear genes (Bhattacharya et al. 2013); *Amborella* mitogenome shown to hold 6 whole foreign genomes (Rice et al. 2013); hornwort-to-fern *neochrome* transfer dated to ~179 Ma; graft-induced allopolyploid *Nicotiana* species created (Fuentes et al. 2014). | Demonstrated germline refutation of somatic/contaminant artifacts alongside the first macroevolutionary plant-to-plant nuclear HGT (*neochrome*). |
| **2015–2017** | Sweet potato shown to be naturally transgenic via *Agrobacterium* (Kyndt et al. 2015). Claims of 145 foreign genes in humans (Crisp et al. 2015) and ~17.5% foreign genes in tardigrades (Boothby et al. 2015) published—and swiftly refuted as artifacts of gene loss and bacterial contamination (Koutsovoulos et al. 2016; Salzberg 2017; Ku & Martin 2016). | The field's methodological crisis: established that assembly contamination and differential gene loss easily mimic HGT unless long-read flanking and synteny tests are met. |
| **2019–2021** | Large grass-to-grass DNA blocks in *Alloteropsis* (Dunning et al. 2019); first plant-to-insect gene *BtPMaT1* in whiteflies (Xia et al. 2021); live cell-to-cell video of plastids crossing graft junctions (Hertle et al. 2021); definitive syntenic proof of herring-to-smelt antifreeze transfer (Graham et al. 2021). | Resolved long-standing mechanism and vertebrate questions with third-generation sequencing and live imaging. |
| **2022–2026** | Systematic insect screen (1,410 genes; Li et al. 2022); streptophyte two-pulse land-plant survey (593 families; Ma et al. 2022); whitefly 49-gene resurvey (Gilbert & Maumus 2022); *Maverick* viral-fusogen transposons caught moving nematode genes (Widen et al. 2023); vertebrate eye *IRBP* traced to bacteria (Kalluraya et al. 2023); bdelloid antifungal NRPS/PKS clusters (Nowell et al. 2024); germline piRNA silencing of HTTs (Brito et al. 2024); 242-fish genome survey (Policarpo et al. 2025); leaf-beetle pectinase-symbiont cycling (Kirsch et al. 2025); 29.3% survival reanalysis of plant interkingdom claims (Aguirre-Carvajal et al. 2025); silkmoth *SrKYNU* CRISPR validation (Guo et al. 2026); coordinated aphid carotenoid pathway transfer (Hu et al. 2026); *euHGT* metazoan compendium (Spaulding & Fierst 2026). | The modern synthesis: active transfer vehicles identified, pathway-level and behavioral traits proven via CRISPR, and strict re-testing separating robust recent transfers from inflated ancient claims. |

## 4. Why Eukaryotic HGT Is Rare: The Four-Filter Barrier Chain

In bacteria, an imported DNA fragment that integrates into the chromosome of a dividing cell is automatically inherited by all daughter cells, and modular metabolic networks allow a single new enzyme to pay off immediately. In multicellular plants and animals, any successful transfer must clear four sequential filters (Mariault et al. 2025; Keeling 2024; Husnik & McCutcheon 2018). If any link breaks, heritable HGT fails.

### Filter 1: Germline Access and the "Weak-Link" Model
Foreign DNA must exit the donor, enter the recipient, and reach cells that produce gametes. 
* **The Animal Weismann Barrier:** Animals sequester their germline early in embryonic development. Foreign DNA integrating into gut, skin, or muscle cells dies with the individual. A sharp natural experiment isolates this link: in insects, the intracellular bacterium *Wolbachia* resides directly inside eggs and reproductive tissues and has donated massive genomic inserts (up to a whole ~1.4 Mb genome in *D. ananassae*), whereas *Buchnera*, an obligate aphid symbiont restricted to specialized somatic bacteriocytes and largely excluded from germ cells, contributed only two truncated, silent fragments to the pea aphid genome (Dunning Hotopp 2011; Nikoh et al. 2010).
* **The Mammalian Barrier is Germline-Specific, Not Cellular:** Human somatic cells *do* incorporate bacterial DNA at measurable rates. Scanning the Human Genome Project, 1000 Genomes Project, and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Riley et al. (2013) found bacterial-to-human somatic integrations at $2.28 \times 10^{-6}$ per generation in 1000 Genomes data and $6.02 \times 10^{-6}$ in Trace Archive data, rising to $1.6 \times 10^{-5}$ in tumors—a **210-fold enrichment in tumors** over normal TCGA controls (672× in acute myeloid leukemia over normal breast controls; 13.2× in stomach adenocarcinoma). In leukemia samples, 99% of integration reads were bacterial rRNA (versus 68% in the microbiome background), consistent with an RNA intermediate and innate immune filtering that destroys unmethylated bacterial CpG DNA and mRNA while passing rRNA. In stomach adenocarcinoma, *Pseudomonas*-like sequences integrated into the 5′/3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of four proto-oncogenes (*CEACAM5*, *CEACAM6*, *TMSB10*, *CD74*), correlating with overexpression (though causation vs. passenger clonal expansion in tumors and cell-culture artifacts in 1000 Genomes remain open caveats; Riley et al. 2013). The scarcity of human HGT is therefore a property of the sequestered germline and immune surveillance, not cellular impermeability.
* **The Cyst-Nematode Mobilome Paradox:** Intimate contact alone does not guarantee uptake. Cyst nematodes (*Heterodera schachtii*) ingest living plant cytoplasm for weeks and share eukaryotic intron architecture with their hosts, yet plant-derived HGT in them is conspicuously absent while bacterial donors with transient contact dominate. Ko et al. (2024) demonstrated that although the host plant genome is actively mobilized into fragments ("the mobilome") during nematode infection, **cellular uptake and germline entry of that mobilome is the primary barrier** where transfer fails.
* **Plants and the "Weak-Link" Model:** Unlike animals, plants specify germ cells late in development from somatic meristems, can regenerate whole fertile shoots from wound calli, and often propagate clonally or via rhizomes (Mariault et al. 2025; Hibdige et al. 2021). Huang's (2013) **weak-link model** proposes that eukaryotic HGT concentrates at weakly protected unicellular or early developmental stages (exposed gametes, spores, free-living gametophytes, or rehydrating tissues). Ma et al. (2022) confirmed this gradient quantitatively across the green lineage: recently acquired foreign gene families are abundant in filamentous charophyte algae (*Klebsormidium nitens*: 95 families, ~1.5% of genes) and bryophytes (*Physcomitrium patens*: 30 families, where apical-cell damage and regeneration also offer entry), fewer in lycophytes (*Selaginella*: 14) and ferns (*Azolla*: 2), and dramatically rare in seed plants. Fern and hornwort gametophytes lack protective cuticles and grow in direct, damp physical contact with their gamete-producing antheridia and archegonia exposed to the environment ("promiscuous little plants"; Li et al. 2014), whereas seed plants insulate gametophytes behind impervious sporopollenin pollen walls and maternal sporophyte tissue.

### Filter 2: Intracellular Defense and the Germline piRNA Pathway
Even when foreign DNA enters an animal germ cell, it faces an active molecular immune system: the **piRNA (Piwi-interacting RNA) pathway** (Tóth et al. 2016). In animal germlines, transposon and foreign DNA fragments are captured into dedicated genomic loci called piRNA clusters. These clusters transcribe ~24–30 nucleotide small RNAs that guide Piwi-clade Argonaute proteins to transcriptionally and post-transcriptionally silence matching sequences genome-wide in *trans*, amplified by a "ping-pong" cleavage loop (Aubergine/Ago3 slicing with a characteristic 10-nt overlap and 1U/10A biases) and maternally deposited into eggs to enforce transgenerational silencing.

Brito et al. (2024) provided the first direct demonstration of this system repelling cross-phylum HGT. In the blood-feeding Chagas disease vector *Rhodnius prolixus*—which acquired *SPIN*, *OposCharlie1*, *hAT*, and *ExtraTerrestrial* transposons from vertebrate hosts (opossums, squirrel monkeys, bats) within the last ~50 Myr at up to 98.1% sequence identity (Gilbert et al. 2010)—all four horizontally transferred families are targeted by abundant ~28-nt embryonic piRNAs. Peak embryonic piRNA levels coincide with the lowest expression of both horizontally transferred and resident TEs across development. Crucially, unlike resident TEs (where 60–80% of piRNAs originate from specialized piRNA clusters with ping-pong amplification), fewer than 15% of piRNAs targeting the new horizontal invaders come from clusters, and they lack the ping-pong signature. This captures the germline defense in its primary, first-line response phase: generating primary piRNAs directly from scattered foreign copies to transcriptionally shut down newcomers before they can express or recombine. Plants lack this animal-style Piwi/piRNA germline apparatus in the same form, which aligns with (though does not alone prove) the higher plant HGT load.

### Filter 3: Chromosomal Integration and Expression Renovation
Foreign DNA usually integrates into eukaryotic chromosomes via **non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)**—an emergency double-strand break repair pathway that ligates broken DNA ends without requiring sequence homology (Husnik & McCutcheon 2018). NHEJ imposes a strict size filter: small fragments integrate and survive far more successfully than whole bacterial genomes, which routinely rearrange into silent junk. Moreover, Li et al. (2026) observed a "first do no harm" structural footprint: 75% of single-copy HGTs hit coding sequences, whereas multi-copy HGTs land in introns 71% of the time.

Once integrated, a bacterial gene lacks eukaryotic promoters, spliceosomal introns, polyadenylation signals, and compatible codon usage/GC content. How do foreign genes overcome transcriptional silencing?
* **Transposon-Driven Intron Acquisition (The Insect Renovation Mechanism):** In their screen of 1,410 transferred insect genes, Li et al. (2022) discovered a massive post-insertion renovation process. While 519 genes showed no intron change and 42 lost all 53 donor introns, **849 transferred genes lost 245 donor introns and gained 1,534 new introns** ($\ge 100$ bp) *after* insertion into the insect genome (leaving 561 intronless). Two-thirds of these gained introns (1,013 of 1,534; 66%) matched native insect sequences at ~86% mean identity, and **88% of those derived from host repeats**—51.6% DNA transposons, 25.8% LTR retrotransposons, and 10.6% unclassified repeats. This renovation had a dramatic functional payoff: in 26 of 32 transcriptome datasets (81.3%), **intron-containing transferred genes were expressed on average ~11-fold higher** than intronless ones (only 6 datasets, 18.7%, showed ~4-fold lower expression). Over evolutionary time (tracked across 822 genes shared by $\ge 2$ species), transferred genes lengthened from an average of 1.54 kb in donors to 5.00 kb in recipients (moving toward the 12.27 kb native insect average) purely via intron expansion (3.55 kb in recipients vs. 0.09 kb in donors and 10.81 kb in native genes), while coding sequence (CDS) length remained completely constant (~1.45–1.46 kb). The mealybug alphaproteobacterial riboflavin gene *ribD* similarly gained two canonical eukaryotic introns and reached native expression levels over millions of years of co-divergence (Husnik & McCutcheon 2018).
* **Promoter Co-option and Chimeric Splicing:** In the tunicate *Ciona intestinalis*, the bacterial-derived cellulose synthase gene (*Ci-CesA*) achieved epidermis-specific expression by landing adjacent to a host genomic region bound by the native AP-2 transcription factor (Sasakura et al. 2016). In the parasitic plant *Lophophytum*, foreign mitochondrial genes become functional by forming chimeras with native 5′ upstream regions, borrowing recognizable host promoters and utilizing native RNA-editing machinery, with selection favoring intronless donor copies that require less RNA editing (Roulet et al. 2026). In grasses (*Alloteropsis*), transferred genomic blocks up to 170 kb carry their own intact donor regulatory motifs alongside the coding gene, bypassing the need to invent a promoter de novo (Dunning et al. 2019; Collins et al. 2026).

### Filter 4: Selection, Need vs. Access, and the "Self-Contained Unit" Rule
Why don't phagotrophic eukaryotes—which engulf whole bacterial cells as food, exposing their cells to foreign DNA at rates estimated at "billions of times per millisecond in aggregate" across global populations over evolutionary time (Keeling 2024)—accumulate bacterial genomes? Keeling's (2024) **Chance vs. Necessity (Access vs. Need) framework** resolves this: *phagotrophy increases the chance of acquiring genes while removing the metabolic necessity for them*. Because a predator digests prey internally and absorbs ready-made amino acids and cofactors, it rarely needs new metabolic pathways, and eukaryotic adaptation mostly proceeds by tweaking complex developmental regulatory cascades ("a heterotrophic flagellate does not evolve the ability to eat larger cells by acquiring 'big mouth' genes"; Keeling 2024).

Conversely, HGT concentrates in eukaryotes that retain a "bacterial-like" relationship with external chemistry:
1. **Osmotrophs and External Digesters:** Fungi and oomycetes secrete digestive enzymes into the environment and import the breakdown products. Savory et al. (2015) re-analyzed 23 oomycete genomes and validated 48 transferred gene families (1,593 predicted proteins), of which **40 of 48 came from fungi**—the kingdom oomycetes convergently mimic. Crucially, **73% of the acquired proteins (1,152/1,593) and 69% of the families (33/48) encode secreted proteins** (NEP1-like necrosis factors, LysM-domain effectors, plant-cell-wall-degrading enzymes, nutrient transporters). Following Richards & Talbot (2013), osmotrophs play extracellular "public-goods" games where any borrowed secreted enzyme pays off immediately without re-engineering intracellular networks. Only 7 of 48 oomycete transfers mapped to the stem lineage, while 33 clustered in the plant-parasitic radiation, tracking feeding mode: highest in hemibiotrophic *Phytophthora* (which expands transfers to 4.37 copies per acquisition), lower in necrotrophic *Pythium* (2.12 copies), and scarce in obligate biotrophs (*Hyaloperonospora*, *Albugo*).
2. **The "Self-Contained Unit" Filter:** Studying pore-forming aerolysin toxins that crossed kingdoms at least six times, Moran et al. (2012) proposed that transferred genes are maintained when they are **"self-contained units"** that function autonomously without multi-protein complexes (e.g., pore-forming toxins that self-oligomerize, or single digestive/detoxifying enzymes) and provide **immediate ecological benefit**. (For exceptions where whole multi-step pathways were assembled via HGT, see the aphid carotenoid and bdelloid PKS/NRPS cases in Section 7.)

## 5. Mechanisms and Vectors: How DNA Crosses the Species Barrier

Until recently, eukaryotic HGT suffered from the **"footprints-not-footsteps" problem** (Mariault et al. 2025): researchers observed anciently integrated sequences long after the physical delivery vector had vanished. Today, seven delivery routes are documented across a gradient from live experimental visualization to phylogenetic reconstruction.

| Route / Vector Class | Primary Recipients | Key Evidence & Quantified Rates | What Remains Unresolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. *Agrobacterium* T-DNA transformation** | Dicot plants (*Ipomoea* sweet potato, *Nicotiana*, *Linaria*) | VirB/D4 type-IV secretion system traced across 6 protein components (Cascales & Christie 2004); *IbT-DNA1* fixed in 291/291 cultivated sweet potatoes (Kyndt et al. 2015). | Historical infection and regeneration steps for ancient wild events are reconstructed, not observed. |
| **2. Haustorial bridges & grafting** | Parasitic plants (*Cuscuta*, *Striga*, *Orobanchaceae*, *Lophophytum*); grafted *Nicotiana*/*Brassica* | Accounts for >42% of >600 plant-to-plant HGTs. Live imaging shows amoeboid plastids crossing 1.5 μm graft-wall pores in **1 of 20–40 cells** (boosted >5× by starvation; Hertle et al. 2021). Graft *eccDNA* enters *Brassica* germline (Zhang et al. 2026 preprint). | How large nuclear chromosome blocks cross wild haustoria; natural frequency of graft speciation in the wild (judged vanishingly rare; Gaut et al. 2019). |
| **3. Intracellular germline endosymbionts** | Insects, filarial nematodes, arachnids | *Wolbachia* infects 20–70% of insects via egg cytoplasm; ~70% of hosts carry nuclear inserts (Dunning Hotopp 2011). Insect HGT donor abundance correlates with symbiont abundance ($r = 0.68, P = 7.6 \times 10^{-9}$; Li et al. 2022). | Exact lysis and NHEJ capture moments; why some symbionts (*Rickettsia*, *Wolbachia*) donate heavily while *Buchnera* does not (germline proximity). |
| **4. Free viruses & domesticated Polydnaviruses** | Lepidopterans, whiteflies, parasitoid wasps, plants | Moth baculoviruses pick up host TEs in vivo at **1 per ~8,500 viral genomes** (4.8% of 21 viral populations carry host DNA; Gilbert et al. 2014; Aubin et al. 2021). Wasp bracoviruses inject 222 wasp virulence genes into caterpillars and reintegrate into host/wasp DNA (Herniou et al. 2013). | Direct experimental proof of plant viruses ferrying the 49 plant genes into whiteflies (currently an exposure inference). |
| **5. Ectoparasites & blood-feeding vectors** | Snakes, frogs, mammals, birds, kissing bugs (*Rhodnius*) | $\ge 54$ *BovB* transfers (85–1.3 Ma) run **predator (snake) → prey (frog)**, concentrated in Madagascar (91% frogs positive vs. 0% in Americas/Africa; Kambayashi et al. 2022). Chigger mites (99.8% match) and nematodes (100% match) carry snake *BovB*. | Whether transmission is mechanical (contaminated mouthparts/blood cells) or biological (integration into parasite genomes); awaits a controlled blood-meal transfer experiment. |

### Deep Dive into the Breakthrough Vector Systems

#### A. Grafting, Haustoria, and Extrachromosomal Circular DNA (eccDNA) in Plants
Grafting and parasitic haustoria create sustained cell-to-cell cytoplasmic bridges. Across experimental graft junctions (reviewed in Gaut et al. 2019), small RNAs move to direct heritable DNA methylation changes (persisting at least four selfed generations in mustard-cabbage chimeras), ~12% of grapevine protein-coding genes produce phloem-mobile mRNAs, complete plastid and mitochondrial genomes cross between sexually incompatible species (Stegemann & Bock 2009; Stegemann et al. 2012), and in the limit case, entire nuclear genomes migrate between *Nicotiana tabacum* (herb) and *N. glauca* (tree) callus cells to form fertile allopolyploid shoots—technically a new true-breeding species (*Nicotiana tabauca*; Fuentes et al. 2014). 

In 2021, Hertle et al. (*Science Advances*) filmed the cellular mechanism in real time using double-transgenic tobacco: during wound-induced callus proliferation, plastids dedifferentiate into small, mobile amoeboid organelles (~1 μm), local cell-wall openings up to 1.5 μm form between neighboring cells, and cytoplasmic protrusions carry DNA-containing plastids (verified by SYBR Green/dsRed colocalization) across the junction. This occurs bidirectionally in **1 of every 20 to 40 contacting cells without antibiotic selection**, and **carbon starvation (darkness, no sucrose) increases transfer frequency more than fivefold**—confirming that physiological stress amplifies genome exchange. When lateral shoots regenerate from the graft callus, these somatic transfers become permanently heritable.

Adding the missing somatic-to-germline link for nuclear DNA, a 2026 preprint by Zhang et al. examined graft-induced periclinal chimeras between *Brassica juncea* and *Brassica oleracea*. They found that **extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA)**—mobile DNA circles up to 400 kb enriched for coding genes, inverted repeats, hairpin sequences, and autonomous-replication-sequence (ARS) motifs (verified in an ARS-less yeast system)—crosses histological boundaries from somatic graft layers into the germline and is **transmitted through sexual reproduction across multiple generations**. Sexual progeny carrying graft-acquired eccDNA displayed reproducible changes in leaf morphology, drought tolerance, and active transcription of donor-specific genes (caveat: abstract-level preprint awaiting peer review).

#### B. Virus-Like Transposons (*Mavericks* and *Starships*): The Active Eukaryotic Vehicles
For decades, passive routes could not explain how intact genes jump between free-living animals. In 2023, Widen et al. (*Science*, Burga lab) discovered the culprit while tracing a selfish toxin-antidote incompatibility element in the nematode *Caenorhabditis briggsae*. They found its ancestral sequence embedded inside a **Maverick** (also called *Polinton*) transposon in the reproductively isolated species *C. plicata*—species separated by an evolutionary distance comparable to humans and fish. *Mavericks* are ancient, giant virus-like DNA transposons (present in pseudogenized form even in humans) that encode a viral capsid and DNA polymerase, but those two components cannot carry a transposon across cell membranes. Widen et al. discovered that nematode *Mavericks* captured a **herpesvirus-class fusogen**—a transmembrane glycoprotein structurally homologous to HSV-1 glycoprotein B that mediates membrane fusion. Equipped with capsid, polymerase, and fusogen, *Mavericks* form infectious virus-like particles that package host cargo genes (including serine-protease families across 53 worm species and kinase families at 20–23% *Maverick*-association rates, with copies between distant species sharing 97.5% nucleotide identity including introns) and inject them into the germlines of divergent worm genera worldwide.

In 2025, a parallel active mechanism was demonstrated in fungi (preprint, March 2025): **Starships**, giant transposable elements (16–403 kb, mean ~71.5 kb) mobilized by a tyrosine-recombinase "captain" enzyme. Simply co-culturing donor and recipient fungi on standard medium ("near-natural" conditions without protoplasting or chemical transformation) resulted in the tagged *Starship* *Hephaestus* (carrying hygromycin and heavy-metal resistance) transferring at rates of **62.5% within species, 96.9% between species, and 16.7% between genera** (*Paecilomyces* to *Aspergillus*). Whole-genome sequencing of 26 recipients confirmed that *only* the *Starship* DNA transferred (ruling out heterokaryons, parasex, or random DNA uptake), integrating precisely at its canonical `TTAC(N7)A` target site (with co-transfer of a second *Starship*, *Pegasus*, in 50–100% of recipients). Across 10,010 *Pezizomycotina* genomes, 18.2–27.3% of *Paecilomyces* *Starships* show natural HGT across up to ~180 Myr of divergence, conferring cadmium, zinc, and copper resistance to *Aspergillus fumigatus* recipients. Together, *Mavericks* and *Starships* prove that giant transposon-virus chimeras actively ferry multi-gene cargo across eukaryotic species barriers.

#### C. Polydnaviruses: Domesticated Viral Gene-Delivery Organs
Braconid parasitoid wasps took viral vectoring a step further ~100 million years ago by domesticating an ancestral nudivirus into a permanent, chromosomally integrated gene-delivery organ (Herniou et al. 2013; Drezen et al. 2014; ichneumonid wasps independently domesticated an iridovirus-like ancestor to build ichnoviruses). In braconids, 29 viral machinery genes (RNA polymerase subunits, capsids, and the PIF cell-entry complex) manufacture viral particles in the wasp's ovaries. Instead of packaging the viral genome, the particles package **hundreds of wasp-derived virulence genes** on circular DNA segments (e.g., 222 genes in 37 expanded families in *Cotesia* bracovirus, including ankyrin [*ank*] and protein-tyrosine-phosphatase [*ptp*] families inserted >86 Ma, plus wasp-derived sugar transporters in *Glyptapanteles*). When the wasp lays eggs inside a caterpillar, the injected circles enter host cells and express proteins that disable caterpillar immune encapsulation. Crucially for HGT, Herniou et al. (2013), Muller et al. (2021), and Matrougui et al. (2026) demonstrated that these bracovirus circles contain conserved integration motifs (in 12 of 15 circles) and **secondarily integrate into the chromosomes of parasitized lepidopteran cells in vitro and in vivo**. When parasitized caterpillars survive ("live to tell the tale"), this system acts as an active conduit for wasp-to-lepidopteran germline HGT.

#### D. Predator-to-Prey Vectoring: The *BovB* Reversal
While Doolittle's "you are what you eat" model predicts DNA moving from prey to predator (confirmed in a single frog $\rightarrow$ frog-eating xenopeltid snake *BovB* transfer; Kambayashi et al. 2022), blood-feeding vectors reverse the arrow. Screening 106 snake, 149 frog, and 42 parasite species, Kambayashi et al. (2022) identified **$\ge 54$ horizontal transfers of the *BovB* retrotransposon between 85 and 1.3 Ma running predominantly from predators (snakes) to prey (frogs)**. Transfer frequency is governed by geography and vector fauna rather than host phylogeny: Madagascar is a massive hotspot where **91% of sampled frogs (29/32) are *BovB*-positive** (with 14 transfers in the last 50 Ma, and two frog lineages—*Heterixalus* and *Ptychadena mascareniensis*—acquiring *BovB* only *after* migrating from *BovB*-free mainland Africa 19–30 Ma and 9.8–22.7 Ma), compared to 34% globally and 0% in Africa, North America, and South America. Madagascan chigger mites (Trombiculidae; carrying *BovB* 99.8% identical to a local snake's vs. 96.1% to their frog host), amphibian nematodes (*Cosmocerca simile*; 100% identical to a local colubrid snake's *BovB*), reptile ticks (*Amblyomma limbatum*), and leeches (*Chtonobdella vagans*) act as the shuttles. Once inside a host, *BovB* proliferates massively: entering mammals from snakes 76–85 Ma, it now occupies **18.4% of the cow genome**, 15.2% of sheep, 1.3% of opossum, and 0.53% of the frog *Boophis goudotii* (Note on perimeter: popular press claims that *BovB* makes up ~20–25% of the cow genome count all historical *BovB*-family retrotransposon copies, whereas Li et al. 2026 measured a single specific HGT-derived *BovB* region in cow at 36,868 copies totaling 36.3 Mb, or ~1.3% of the ~2.7 Gb genome).

### Resolving Apparent Contradictions in the Numbers
1. **Why nematodes rank highest (~3.34%) in proteome studies but lowest (10 regions) in Li et al. (2026):** Age and sequence amelioration. Root-knot nematode parasitism transfers occurred deep in the clade's history and have mutated over millions of years to match the host's GC content and codon usage ("amelioration"), making them invisible to nucleotide pipelines tuned for recent sequence similarity, whereas protein-phylogeny pipelines recover them.
2. **Why *Drosophila* had 40 foreign genes in Crisp et al. (2015) but Diptera averaged 2 in Li et al. (2022):** Assembly cleaning vs. false positives. Crisp et al.'s "Class C" was a loose BLAST-score screen (where 45% of candidates failed phylogenetic validation), but Crisp et al. also noted that genome-assembly pipelines routinely strip bacterial contigs as presumed contaminants—for example, all *D. ananassae* *Wolbachia* chromosomal inserts were deleted from its reference assembly, creating false negatives via over-cleaning.
3. **Why Whitefly Plant-Gene Counts Shifted (4 vs. 25 vs. 49 vs. 131 vs. 62+69):** Sampling depth and pipeline perimeters. Early papers reported 4 genes from 2 events (Lapadula et al. 2020; Xia et al. 2021); reviews cited "at least 25" (Mariault et al. 2025); Gilbert & Maumus (2022) systematically screened all 15,662 *B. tabaci* MEAM1 proteins to find **49 MEAM1 genes from $\ge 24$ events** (138 proteins across all *Aleyrodidae* cryptic species, 131 with plant best-hits: 49 MEAM1 + 45 SSA-ECA + 35 MED + 2 *Trialeurodes vaporariorum*). Finally, Li et al. (2026) ran a *nucleotide-level* screen on the whitefly genome: of 170 prior protein calls, they recovered 62 and found 69 new regions (36 entirely non-coding, which protein pipelines cannot see by definition).
4. **Why the insect order gradient (Lepidoptera 16 vs. Diptera 2) is biological:** Li et al. (2022) tested potential technical confounders—genome size, total gene count, species divergence time, and assembly completeness—and found only weak correlations with HGT count. Instead, HGT frequency tracked herbivorous, plant-associated lifestyles and the relative abundance of heritable endosymbiont donor genera ($r = 0.68$). Across all 1,410 insect HGT genes, donors broke down cleanly as **79.0% bacteria** (1,115 genes from 533 species), **13.8% fungi** (194 genes, 74 species), **3.0% plants** (43 genes, 25 species), **2.6% viruses** (36 genes, 29 species), and **1.6% other** (22 genes, 9 species), with striking donor-recipient specificity (*Exophiala* donated only to Hemiptera, *Listeria* only to Lepidoptera, *Streptomyces* to Coleoptera, *Erwinia* to Diptera).

## 7. Substantive Case Examinations: Plants and Animals

To evaluate evolutionary significance without conflating speculation and proof, every case must be assessed across four distinct axes (and graded on a 5-tier scale from **Tier 1: Experimental knockout/RNAi/rescue** to **Tier 5: Phylogeny-only**):
1. *Foreign evolutionary origin* (phylogenetic incongruence vs. gene loss).
2. *Physical integration and germline inheritance* (flanking host genes, long reads, synteny).
3. *Functional assimilation* (transcription, splicing, protein kinetics, tissue localization).
4. *Evolutionary benefit* (perturbation showing fitness impact vs. neutral replacement or decay).

#### Key Narrative Synthesis for Plants
The plant record demonstrates that HGT operates in two distinct modes. First, **ancient macroevolutionary pulses**: Ma et al. (2022) showed that 284 of the 593 foreign gene families in streptophytes arrived in just two bursts—Episode 1 (early streptophytes, 177 families) and Episode 2 (origin of land plants ~450 Ma, 107 families)—supplying the biochemical toolkit required to survive terrestrial UV radiation (*MAG*), desiccation (*LEA2*, *PYL*, *GRAS*), soil nutrient scarcity (10 transporter families including ammonium, phosphate, and *NIP* aquaporins), and pathogen/herbivore attack (phenylpropanoid *CHS/STS*, ricin/abrin toxins, *Tma12* in ferns, *FitD* in cycads, and a fungal FB lectin in liverworts that kills ~80% of diamondback moth larvae vs. 8.8% in controls). Once seed plants evolved enclosed gametophytes and stabilized their terrestrial physiology, the rate of new acquisitions dropped sharply, restricting recent seed-plant HGT primarily to biotic-stress weapons and grass-to-grass exchanges.

Second, **ongoing contact-mediated turnover**: In grasses (*Alloteropsis*) and parasitic plants (*Cuscuta*, *Lophophytum*), intimate tissue contact (rhizomes or haustoria) drives continuous DNA import. Yet the 2023–2026 *Alloteropsis* population studies (Raimondeau et al. 2023; Collins et al. 2026) prove that even in an HGT hotspot where imported *PEPC* genes provide a verified biochemical shortcut to C4 photosynthesis (Phansopa et al. 2020), **nearly 70% of imported genes are actively degenerating**, silenced by DNA methylation and lost at rates of 11–24% per million years.

## 8. Methodological Reckoning: Detection Standards and Famous Refutations

Because assembly contamination, reference-database errors, and differential gene loss easily masquerade as HGT, a credible modern claim requires passing **eight layered tests** (Mariault et al. 2025; Policarpo et al. 2025; Cornet & Baurain 2022):
1. **Phylogenetic incongruence:** The gene tree robustly places the recipient sequence nested *within* the donor clade, and formal topology tests (e.g., Approximately Unbiased [AU] test, concordance factors, or Duplication-Transfer-Loss [DTL] reconciliation in *SpeciesRax*/*Ranger-DTL*) reject vertical inheritance.
2. **Anomalous sequence similarity & synonymous divergence ($K_s$):** Sequence identity (sometimes extending across non-coding introns, as in the >95% identical herring-smelt *AFP* or 2–15 kb in the 19 teleost transfers) is far too high for the species' divergence time.
3. **Patchy taxonomic distribution costed against gene loss:** The alternative of ancient origin plus vertical loss requires an implausible number of independent losses (e.g., $\ge 76$ losses for one *aerolysin* cluster against a Last Universal Common Ancestor [LUCA] of only 500–1,000 genes; Moran et al. 2012; or $\ge 22$ losses for whitefly plant genes; Gilbert & Maumus 2022).
4. **Non-syntenic placement with bona fide host flanking DNA:** The foreign gene sits in a genomic neighborhood unrelated to the donor's, embedded on a long assembly scaffold flanked by verified native host genes (e.g., coral *natterin* flanked by 503 coral genes on a 1.04-Mb scaffold; Gacesa et al. 2020), spanned by continuous long-read sequencing coverage and PCR-amplified junctions.
5. **Receiver-specific mutation accumulation & renovation:** Unlike fresh bacterial contaminants (which are 100% identical to database bacteria and sit on tiny, unanchored contigs—such as a single *mariner* copy on a 53-bp contig with zero expression in the killifish *Cyprinodon variegatus* vs. 148 and 218 integrated, expressed copies of *Hvmar1* and *Hvmar2* in *Hydra*; Grace & Carr 2020), genuine transfers accumulate mutations in the receiver, experience purifying selection ($K_a/K_s < 0.5$), and acquire host-like introns.
6. **Multi-population / Sister-species conservation:** Independent genomes of the same species or sister taxa sequenced in different laboratories on different diets reproduce the insertion (ruling out sample-specific gut or surface contaminants).
7. **Exclusion of EGT, ERVs, and Hybridization:** Organellar transfers, endogenous retroviruses, and sexual introgression (ruled out when divergence exceeds meiotic compatibility, e.g., >124 Myr in teleost fish) are filtered out.
8. **Functional & Phenotypic Validation:** Transcription, proper splicing/RNA editing, protein activity, and perturbation (CRISPR/RNAi/rescue).

### The Skeptic Camp vs. The Survivalist Camp
* **Ku & Martin's (2016) "70% Rule" and the "Genome of Eden" Rebuttal:** Screening 30,358 eukaryotic sequences against 1,035,375 prokaryotic homologs, Ku & Martin (2016) observed that eukaryotic proteins sharing $\ge 70\%$ amino-acid identity with prokaryotes were almost always assembly singletons concentrated in contaminated genomes (*Nematostella*, *Amphimedon*). They argued that "the last one out looks like an LGT"—apparent ancient transfers are merely ancestral genes lost in other lineages. Leger et al. (2018) countered that invoking unlimited differential loss creates the **"Genome of Eden" paradox** (requiring ancestral genomes to hold impossibly bloated gene repertoires), that Rossoni et al. (2019) later documented five long-read-validated Cyanidiales genes breaking the 70% rule, and that host-specific weapons (such as genes targeting metazoan immunity or plant cell walls) could not have existed in a single-celled common ancestor.
* **The 2025–2026 Plant Interkingdom Reanalysis:** Skepticism toward *old, phylogeny-only* claims was vindicated quantitatively by Aguirre-Carvajal & Armijos-Jaramillo (2025; perspective in 2026). Re-testing **1,170 published plant interkingdom HGT candidates** from 35 papers using expanded taxon sampling and Bremer et al.'s (2025) "last-one-out" loss models, they found:
  * **19.66% (230)** remained supported as originally claimed.
  * **9.66% (113)** remained HGT but shifted to an earlier transfer date/lineage (**29.3% total HGT survival**).
  * **30.60% (358)** became inconclusive.
  * **36.84% (431)** were **rejected** in favor of vertical inheritance plus differential gene loss.
  * **2.14% (25)** were reclassified as **contamination**, and **1.11% (13)** as **endosymbiotic gene transfer (EGT)**.
  
  Why did so many published plant claims fail? Public sequence databases are massively skewed toward bacteria, while key eukaryotic lineages are severely undersampled (for example, the Catalogue of Life lists 12,253 bryophyte species, but NCBI held only 155 bryophyte genomes as of November 25, 2025). When a sparsely distributed native eukaryotic gene is queried against a bacteria-heavy database, its top BLAST hits land in bacteria by default—explaining why published plant lists bizarrely claimed 776 bacterial and 280 fungal donors against only 28 viral donors. Re-testing Wu et al.'s (2024) candidates against the standard NR database validated 32.2%, whereas adding broader eukaryotic databases dropped validation to 25.5%; across the whole reanalysis, newly published genomes flipped the topology of 188 candidates. Furthermore, Cornet & Baurain (2022) benchmarked six contamination-detection tools on an artificial genome known to be 11% contaminated and found estimates ranging wildly from **1.65% (CheckM) to 48.8% (Forty-Two)**, whileGodron et al. (2026) showed that 30 of 34 (88%) "confirmed" transfer events in a resistance-screening pipeline were actually contamination.

### The Cautionary Canon: Four Famous Claims That Collapsed
1. **The Tardigrade Genome (~17.5% $\rightarrow$ 0.2%):** Boothby et al. (2015) claimed ~6,000 foreign genes (~17.5% of the genome) in the tardigrade *Hypsibius dujardini* explained its extreme survival. Independent clean re-assembly by Koutsovoulos et al. (2016) proved the foreign contigs were unremoved bacterial contaminants; strong HGT candidates dropped to **0.2% bacterial and 0.2% non-metazoan eukaryotic** (at most 1–2% under permissive criteria).
2. **The Human Genome (145 Genes $\rightarrow$ ~0 Coding Genes):** Crisp et al. (2015) reported 145 foreign genes in humans (including *ABO* blood group, *HAS1–3* hyaluronan synthases, and *FTO*). Salzberg (2017) re-analyzed the 17 genes reclaimed from the 2001 human genome paper and the 28 highest-confidence Class A claims using a newer (May 2016 vs. Jan 2013) database: 12 of 17 failed Crisp's own BLAST threshold once newly sequenced metazoan relatives were added, and 26 of 28 new claims failed due to scores, bacterial contamination (three *PRAME* genes), paralogy (*HAS1–3* are three paralogs of a single vertebrate gene), or retrotransposon/mitochondrial origin. (Note: Li et al. 2026's newer report of 91 human HGT *regions* validated by long reads and shared with chimpanzees in 88/91 trees measures non-coding/repeat segments rather than functional coding genes.)
3. **The Photosynthetic Sea Slug (*Elysia chlorotica*):** *E. chlorotica* steals functional plastids (*kleptoplasty*) from the alga *Vaucheria litorea*. Early PCR and probe studies (Rumpho et al. 2008; Pierce et al. 2012) claimed algal nuclear genes (such as *psbO*) had transferred into the slug's chromosomes to maintain the plastids. However, whole-genome sequencing of *E. chlorotica* fertilized eggs—which never touch algal food—found **zero algal nuclear genes in the germline** (Bhattacharya et al. 2013), and single-individual genomes of the kleptoplastic slugs *Plakobranchus ocellatus* and *Elysia marginata* similarly confirmed that functional kleptoplasty operates without nuclear HGT (Maeda et al. 2021). (A 2014 larval chromosome FISH report for *prk* by Schwartz et al. remains an unconfirmed outlier lacking flanked genomic sequence.)
4. **The Octopus "Alien DNA" Claim:** In 2018, Steele et al. (*Progress in Biophysics and Molecular Biology*) claimed that octopus developmental genes and the Cambrian explosion resulted from extraterrestrial retroviruses ("borrowed from the cosmos"). Genomic researchers (Albertin, Moelling, Gilly, Stedman) dismantled the claim immediately: the 2015 octopus genome (Albertin et al. 2015) shows ordinary molluscan gene homology, with cephalopod neural novelty driven by native protocadherin tandem duplications (168 copies), transposon rearrangements, and extensive Adenosine-to-Inosine RNA editing (Liscovitch-Brauer et al. 2017)—not HGT, terrestrial or cosmic.

## 9. Evolutionary Significance: Evaluating the Adaptive Ledger

Does HGT confer benefits, and how much does it matter to eukaryotic evolution? The evidence supports a conditional synthesis that resolves the debate between HGT skeptics and enthusiasts.

### 1. The Five Post-Transfer Fates of Imported DNA
When foreign DNA integrates into a plant or animal germline, it follows one of five evolutionary trajectories (Husnik & McCutcheon 2018; Keeling 2024; Collins et al. 2026):
1. **Pseudogenization, silencing, and erosion (The Default Majority):** Because organisms "cannot cherry-pick the best genes" (Gogarten, in Vogan & Higgs 2011, whose mathematical models show acquired genes are useful at most ~25% of the time at low transfer rates and a few percent at high rates), most insertions lack regulatory compatibility or disrupt host sequences. They are silenced by germline piRNAs or DNA methylation and decay into junk (*Amborella*'s 6 mitochondrial genomes, ~68.3% of *Alloteropsis* grass transfers, and most *Wolbachia* chromosomal inserts). However, among transfers that survive long enough to be detected in modern coding screens, renovation is common: in insects, **at least 478 of 699 (68%) transcriptome-assayed HGT genes are actively expressed at $\ge 5$ RPKM**, averaging 350 RPKM vs. 608 RPKM for native genes (Li et al. 2022).
2. **Functional replacement (Maintenance without innovation):** Imported genes replace an existing native or endosymbiont gene without changing the organism's phenotype—such as *Lophophytum* replacing 23 native mitochondrial housekeeping genes with host copies (Garcia et al. 2021), filarial nematodes replacing lost *Wolbachia* genes, or the amitochondriate protist *Monocercomonoides* acquiring a bacterial cytosolic sulfur-mobilization system that allowed it to lose its mitochondria entirely (Husnik & McCutcheon 2018).
3. **Immediate ecological innovation (The "Self-Contained" Adaptive Pulses):** Standalone enzymes or toxins arrive and function immediately, unlocking a new diet, neutralizing a weapon, or opening a habitat (*BtPMaT1* in whiteflies, *HhMAN1* in coffee borers, pectinases in leaf beetles, *Fhb7* in wheat, *AFP* in smelt, *neochrome* in ferns).
4. **Delayed co-option, duplication, and pathway integration (The Integrated HGT Model):** A non-essential or single-purpose transfer persists under low selective pressure, duplicates, and is later wired into deep developmental, behavioral, or regulatory networks. Examples include the bacterial S41 peptidase duplicating and losing protease residues to become the vertebrate eye's retinoid shuttle *IRBP* (Kalluraya et al. 2023), the non-essential bacterial *SrKYNU* enzyme co-opted via promoter deletion to control silkmoth cocoon color (Guo et al. 2026), the bacterial alcohol dehydrogenase *LOC105383139* wired into moth courtship behavior (Li et al. 2022), three fungal genes assembling with a native gene into the aphid carotenoid pathway (Hu et al. 2026), and Van Etten et al.'s (2024) experimental **Integrated HGT Model (IHM)** in *Galdieria* red algae—where mercury resistance (*merA*) acts as an indivisible "one gene–one function" responder, while arsenic-response genes (*arsH*) duplicate and integrate into broader cell-wide stress circuitry.
5. **Selfish propagation and genetic conflict:** Transferred DNA acts in its own evolutionary interest rather than the host's. This includes horizontal transposons (*BovB*, *SPIN*, *mariner*) and the *Maverick*-transferred protease in *Caenorhabditis briggsae*, which mutated by one amino acid into a selfish toxin-antidote element that kills ~20% of hybrid larvae, actively constructing reproductive isolation and driving speciation (Widen et al. 2023).

### 2. Reconciling the Skeptical Denominator Argument with Macroevolutionary Impact
Commenting on the 2022 insect survey, evolutionary biologist Jerry Coyne argued that while HGT is widespread across species, Lepidoptera average only 16 transferred genes out of tens of thousands of native genes—"a very small bit of noise in an overwhelming phylogenetic signal"—concluding that the vast majority of eukaryotic adaptations arise via ordinary Darwinian mutation and selection on native genes, and that HGT "hardly affects our general view of evolution."

Both Coyne's numerical point and the enthusiasts' adaptive claims are correct, because **per-genome gene share and macroevolutionary leverage are different quantities**:
* **Per-generation and per-gene, vertical evolution rules:** HGT does not scramble eukaryotic species trees the way it does in prokaryotes, nor does it supply the bulk of developmental regulation.
* **At ecological bottlenecks, a single HGT event can redirect an entire clade:** Standard vertical mutation requires waiting for stepwise amino-acid substitutions or gene duplications to invent a novel catalytic fold. HGT bypasses that waiting time by importing a pre-evolved biochemical module from another kingdom that already solved the problem. Without HGT, land plants would not have acquired their initial terrestrial stress/repair toolkit in two concentrated pulses; ferns might not have radiated beneath closed angiosperm canopies (*neochrome*); leaf beetles (~50,000 species, a quarter of global herbivore diversity) and plant-parasitic nematodes could not digest plant cell walls; bdelloid rotifers could not synthesize bacterial-style antifungal peptides to survive millions of years without sex; tunicates would lack their defining cellulose tunic (*Ci-CesA*); the vertebrate eye would lack its interphotoreceptor retinoid shuttle (*IRBP*); and placental mammals would lack the retroviral *syncytin* envelope proteins required for live birth.

### 3. Plants vs. Animals: A Direct Kingdom Comparison
* **Total Documented Load & Time Depth:** **Plants lead** (>800 bacterial genes, 593 streptophyte families with 58–71% retention over >400 Myr, and >600 plant-to-plant events, versus an average of 2 to 16 genes per insect species). *Flip condition:* In animals where ancient, sequence-ameliorated transfers are counted (such as root-knot nematodes at $\ge 3.34\%$ or bdelloid rotifers at ~10%), animal loads rival or exceed those of any non-parasitic plant, and recent acquisitions in seed plants have dropped to near-animal levels (Ma et al. 2022).
* **Organellar Promiscuity:** **Plants lead overwhelmingly.** Plant mitochondria routinely fuse and capture foreign genomes (*Amborella*, *Lophophytum*), and plastids cross graft junctions; animal mitochondria show virtually zero HGT.
* **Trait Diversity & Behavioral/Developmental Sharpness:** **Animals lead.** While plant HGT clusters heavily in metabolism, stress tolerance, transport, and defense, animal HGT has been experimentally proven via CRISPR/knockouts to govern **mating behavior** (moth courtship), **sensory organ architecture** (vertebrate eye *IRBP*), **placentation** (*syncytins*), **sex determination** (pillbug W chromosome), and **reproductive incompatibility** (*C. briggsae* hybrid lethality).

### 4. Practical Implications for Agriculture, Medicine, and Regulation
1. **Crop Breeding & Pre-Validated Traits:** Because an ancient HGT gene surviving in a wild relative has already passed natural selection's compatibility and efficacy trials, transferring it into elite crops is a provenance shift rather than a functional gamble (Mariault et al. 2025). Fungal *Fhb7* (from *Epichloë*) is already deployed in commercial wheat breeding for *Fusarium* blight resistance (Wang et al. 2020), bacterial *TaCSP* improves wheat drought yield (Wang et al. 2025), bacterial *Tma12* from ferns protects transgenic cotton against whiteflies (Shukla et al. 2016), and graft-mobile junction biology is now used to generate transgene-free gene-edited crops (Yang et al. 2023).
2. **Targeted Pest & Parasite Control:** A gene an animal pest stole via HGT is an ideal selective target because the host plant or human patient lacks an ortholog. Engineering tomatoes to express double-stranded RNA silencing the whitefly's stolen *BtPMaT1* gene neutralizes the pest's detoxification shield without chemical sprays (Xia et al. 2021), and the 24 plant-derived gene clusters in *B. tabaci* offer a catalog of new RNAi targets (Gilbert & Maumus 2022). Similarly, horizontally acquired metabolic enzymes in parasitic worms (such as nematode ferrochelatase and *CobQ/CbiP*) represent selective drug-screening targets (Zhang et al. 2019), while bdelloid rotifer NRPS/PKS clusters represent an untapped animal source for antimicrobial bioprospecting (Nowell et al. 2024).
3. **GMO Regulatory Boundaries:** The discovery that every cultivated sweet potato carries expressed *Agrobacterium* T-DNA transgenes (Kyndt et al. 2015) proves that natural transgenesis predates human agriculture by millennia—challenging regulatory frameworks that define "transgenic" purely by the presence of cross-kingdom DNA rather than the specific trait's risk profile (Philips et al. 2022).

## 10. Open Questions and the Research Frontier

Despite the breakthroughs of 2021–2026, eight concrete gaps define the frontier of eukaryotic HGT research, each paired with the experimental test required to resolve it:

1. **Measuring the Eukaryotic Distribution of Fitness Effects (DFE):** The quantitative theoretical framework modeling whether incoming HGTs are deleterious, neutral, or beneficial (Good, Bhatt & McDonald 2025; Biozentrum 2025) has so far been parameterized only in bacteria. *The test:* Generation-resolved fitness assays of artificial insertions across ecological gradients in fast-cycling eukaryotes (e.g., whitefly cell lines, *Caenorhabditis*, or *Arabidopsis*).
2. **Generalizing the *Maverick* and *Starship* Vehicle Hypothesis:** *Mavericks* are proven cargo vectors in nematodes (Widen et al. 2023) and *Starships* in fungi (2025 preprint). Widen et al. explicitly predict that *Mavericks*—which are abundant in ray-finned fish—drove the 19 teleost-to-teleost gene transfers (Policarpo et al. 2025). *The test:* Reconstructing infectious *Maverick* virus-like particles in vitro and scanning flanking regions of the 19 teleost HGT loci for degraded *Maverick* terminal motifs and fusogen remnants.
3. **Explaining Extreme Recipient Lopsidedness:** Why do 135 of 156 reported insect plant-gene homologs sit in a single whitefly species complex (*B. tabaci*) while *Drosophila* has zero, and why do >95% of non-parasitic plant-to-plant transfers sit in grasses (*Poaceae*)? *The test:* Uniform, long-read, multi-lineage comparative screens that control for assembly cleaning and compare germline piRNA silencing efficiency and gut-to-oocyte viral trafficking between whiteflies and flies.
4. **Resolving the Leaf-Beetle Symbiont-to-Genome Re-Transfer Cycle:** Leaf beetles alternate between genomic bacterial pectinases and pectinase-producing gut symbionts (Kirsch et al. 2025). *The test:* Comparative genomics of beetle populations caught in the mid-transition state where a newly established symbiont's pectinase gene is actively replacing a decaying genomic copy (or transferring back into the chromosome as the symbiont is lost).
5. **Confirming Wasp-to-Caterpillar Germline Fixation via Polydnaviruses:** Bracovirus circles integrate into parasitized lepidopteran somatic cells in vivo (Herniou et al. 2013; Muller et al. 2021). *The test:* Systematic screening of lepidopteran germline assemblies for fixed wasp virulence genes (*ank*, *ptp*, sugar transporters) flanked by bracovirus host-integration motifs.
6. **Chemical Isolation of Bdelloid Rotifer Antibiotics:** Resistant bdelloids upregulate ~40 bacterial-derived NRPS/PKS clusters during fungal infection (Nowell et al. 2024). *The test:* Metabolomic purification and antifungal bioassay of the predicted tyrocidine-like cyclic depsipeptides from infected *Adineta ricciae*, paired with developing RNAi/CRISPR perturbation in bdelloids.
7. **Mechanical vs. Biological Vectoring of *BovB*:** Chigger mites and amphibian nematodes carry snake-identical *BovB* retrotransposons in the Madagascar transfer hotspot (Kambayashi et al. 2022). *The test:* Long-read sequencing of the mite and nematode genomes to determine whether snake *BovB* is chromosomally integrated into the parasite germline (biological transmission) or merely carried in ingested blood cells (mechanical transmission), followed by a controlled feeding-transmission assay.
8. **Stabilizing Ancient Interkingdom Catalogs Against Database Skew:** Because 70.7% of published plant interkingdom HGT candidates became inconclusive or failed upon broader homolog sampling (Aguirre-Carvajal et al. 2025), static candidate lists are obsolete. *The test:* Adopting automated phylogenetic network reconciliation (simultaneously modeling duplication, loss, incomplete lineage sorting, introgression, and transfer), ecological species-accumulation curves for homolog sampling, and protein-embedding clustering before declaring any ancient patchy gene an HGT (Armijos-Jaramillo & Aguirre-Carvajal 2026).
