# Academic Perspectives on Japanese Anime (Through 2018): Research Directions, Textual vs. Media-Ecological Paradigms, and a Comparative Analysis with Nordic Noir Television Drama

By 2018, academic research on Japanese animation—anime—had grown outside Japan from a nearly nonexistent area before the 1990s into *"an emerging field... attracting scholars from many different disciplines and subjects across the world"* (anime-manga-studies.com, 2015). Across this pre-2019 literature, a central methodological divide separates scholars treating anime as a **representational text** (looking *through* animation to read Japanese society, postwar memory, gender norms, and postcolonial power relations) from those treating it as a **media form** and ecological network (looking *at* layered celluloid images, limited/selective animation timing, performative conventions, character licensing databases, and cross-media franchises). These approaches overlap and complement one another rather than forming obsolete stages.

Compared alongside Scandinavian crime television drama—marketed internationally since 2010 as **Nordic Noir**—both demonstrate how non-Anglophone TV forms operate as "peripheral contra-flows" acquiring recognizable cultural difference across borders via contrasting institutional centers. Whereas Japanese TV anime relies on decentralized private-sector production committees, character merchandising, and a cottage industry with ~90% offshore frame outsourcing, flagship Nordic Noir drama is anchored by license-fee-funded public service broadcasters (PSBs) under statutory cultural mandates and European co-production alliances. Yet both achieved international reach by transculturating American genre templates, leveraging seriality and distinctive audiovisual aesthetics, bypassing mainstream gatekeepers via secondary broadcast windows, home video, and digital platforms, and converting linguistic or formal distance into cosmopolitan distinction within subcultural or educated "art TV" niches—while remaining constrained by "cultural discount" in Anglophone mass markets.

## Section 1: Main Approaches and Directions in Academic Research on Japanese Anime Outside Japan (Through 2018)

### 1.1 Field Emergence, Institutionalization, Scope, and Terminology: From "Japanimation" to "Anime"

Before the 1990s, Western academic research on Japanese animation was virtually nonexistent. In Marcel Koulikov’s *Online Bibliography of Anime and Manga Research* (2010), analyzed in Brian Ruh’s dissertation (2012), out of 814 English-language scholarly entries on anime and manga through 2010, **only 84 entries (10.3%) were published before 2000**, and just 1 entry predated 1990. This bibliography counts English-language academic monographs, peer-reviewed articles, and book chapters, excluding domestic Japanese criticism (*hyōron*, analytical commentary by Japanese journalists and subcultural critics for domestic magazines, vs. university peer-reviewed scholarship) and European catalogs like Franziska Brückner et al.’s German bibliography (2012), recording 2,000+ commercial anime titles and 28 overview essays through 2005.

Placed alongside Daisuke Miyao’s (2002) prewar history (the first animated film screened in Japan in **1909** came from France) and Thomas Lamarre’s (2009, pp. xxviii–xxix), Kaori Yoshida’s (2008, pp. 9–10), and Frederik L. Schodt’s (*The Astro Boy Essays*, 2007) domestic milestones—beginning in **1917** when Ōten Shimokawa, Jun’ichi Kōuchi, and Seitarō Kitayama independently produced Japan's earliest animated shorts, followed by Toei Animation Studio (Toei Dōga) in 1956, Japan's first feature-length color animation *Hakujaden* (*Panda and the Magic Serpent*) in 1958, and Osamu Tezuka’s Mushi Production TV series *Tetsuwan Atomu* (*Astro Boy*) in 1963—these figures show a seven-decade lag between Japanese animation's birth and English-language academic recognition. Due to this youth, early canonization was skewed: Godart (2013) noted the field initially overemphasized Tezuka's pioneering role.

The mid-1990s scholarship takeoff followed a four-link Western distribution chain (Ruh, 2012): (1) Katsuhiro Ōtomo’s cyberpunk feature *Akira* (1988; 1990 UK premiere at Bristol and London Film Festivals) proved to Western critics that anime addressed adult themes outside children's TV; (2) after the November 1975 US consumer VCR launch—which Sean Leonard documented accelerated fan-driven US anime penetration within three months—late-1980s desktop time-coding hardware enabled **fansubbing** (amateur fan clubs translating Japanese broadcasts and distributing subtitled VHS tapes by mail, vs. commercial dubs edited for children's syndication); (3) fan-entrepreneurs founded specialist North American distributors (AnimEigo, AD Vision) in the early 1990s, putting uncut anime in video stores; and (4) internet fan networks consolidated audiences, prompting scholars to explain Western youth consumption of Japanese pop culture.

Institutional anchors emerged in the early 2000s: the online peer-reviewed journal ***ejcjs*** (*Electronic Journal of Contemporary Japanese Studies*, Jan 9, 2001); the annual **Mechademia conference** in Minneapolis (from 2001); the annual peer-reviewed book series/journal ***Mechademia*** (ed. Frenchy Lunning, Univ. of Minnesota Press, launched 2006 as *Emerging Worlds of Anime and Manga*, including Therèsa Winge's early cosplay essay *"Costuming the Imagination"*, reaching Vol. 8 by 2013); and ***Animation: An Interdisciplinary Journal*** (launched 2006).

Terminology sparked foundational debates. Fred Patten (2004) traced how **"Japanimation"** (first used c. 1978 after the founding of the Cartoon/Fantasy Organization) was replaced in the early 1990s by **"anime,"** partly because its prefix resembled the slur "Jap." Citing Japanese critics Eiji Ōtsuka and Nobuaki Ōsawa (*Japanimēshon wa naze yabureru ka* [Why Japanimation Will Fail], 2005, p. 8), Yoshida (2008, pp. 9–10) adds that "Japanimation" designated pre-1980s exports routinely recut and dubbed to conceal national origin, whereas "anime" designated works distributed worldwide from the 1980s–1990s onward as an explicitly recognized global cultural, economic, and political form. Antonia Levi (1996) and Patrick Drazen (2003) equated "anime" with "Japanese animation" (accepted by Susan J. Napier, 2005), whereas Toshiya Ueno (1999) defended "Japanimation" to retain geographical/stylistic specificity. Conversely, Steven T. Brown (2006) asked *"Where is the anime screen?"*, proposing anime *"does not necessarily refer to animation from a specific country, but rather to ways of positioning images and information on a screen, and ways in which viewers interact with them."* Synthesizing these, Ruh (2012) defined anime as *"a flexible concept used as a shorthand to group together animated films and television programs that seem to share certain family resemblances,"* uniquely implying *"both form and nationality simultaneously."* In *Anime: A Critical Introduction* (2015), Rayna Denison rejects fixing anime to a timeless essence, studying it as a shifting collection of texts shaped by industry, critics, fans, and academia.

### 1.2 Structural Conditions, Disciplinary Entry Points, and Institutional Friction

Lacking standalone anime departments outside Japan, the field developed under **three structural conditions**:
1. **Fan-Culture Origins:** Early archives and methods (Henry Jenkins, 1992, 2006; Matt Hills, 2002; Leonard) were bound to fandom, explaining why reception and circulation preceded art-historical questions in Western scholarship.
2. **Area Studies vs. Media/Animation Studies Split:** Scholars divided between Japanese Studies (treating anime as a gateway to "contemporary Japan") and Film/Media/Animation Studies (Lamarre, Marc Steinberg, Denison, Stevie Suan, resisting purely national-cultural readings). Ywain Tomos (2013, pp. 21–22) noted early Western literature framed anime within Hollywood cel-animation film theory, whereas Japanese scholarship emphasized indigenous aesthetics and the Disney-to-Tezuka lineage.
3. **Uneven Cross-Linguistic Theory Absorption:** Foundational Japanese scholarship—Nobuyuki Tsugata's animation historiography, Fusanosuke Natsume's formal *hyōgenron* ("theory of expression") in manga studies, and Takayoshi Yamamura's regional development research—entered English late and unevenly. Most prominently absorbed were **Eiji Ōtsuka’s "narrative consumption"** (*monogatari shōhi*, 1989/2010) and **Hiroki Azuma’s "database consumption"** (*dētabēsu shōhi*, 2001/2009; Gerow, 2010; Sawai, 2014). Mature French, Italian, and German scholarship was also underrepresented in Anglo-American surveys.

Kukhee Choo (2013) demonstrated that institutionalizing anime research in North America generated deep **disciplinary friction between Japanese Studies (Area Studies) and Film, Media, and Cultural Studies** across four links:
1. **North American Japanese Studies Enrollment Crisis:** Concentrated in elite Ivy League and West Coast universities focused on US–Japan political/economic relations—teaching history, politics, religion, art history, and literature while excluding media studies for 50 years—Japanese Studies suffered severe enrollment drops during Japan's post-1990 recession (the University of Toronto proposed merging Area Studies programs; several smaller colleges abolished Japanese departments). Surging 2000s student interest in anime/manga revived language enrollments, prompting departments to add anime courses, rewrite job ads for *"Japanese literature, film studies, or anime and media studies,"* and bring anime to the Association for Asian Studies (AAS)—culminating at the March 2013 AAS San Diego conference, where Tamotsu Aoki lectured on "Cool Japan" and AAS President Ted Bestor (Harvard) addressed Japanese pop culture (Choo, 2013).
2. **Language Competence vs. Western Critical Theory:** A quantitatively larger stream emerged in Film, Media, and Comparative Literature departments. Choo (2013) notes many younger scholars entered via personal affection for anime without reading/speaking Japanese or studying Japanese history deeply, relying on Western critical theory, feminism, gender studies, aesthetics, and psychoanalysis. While yielding innovative formal readings, their work drew Area Studies skepticism when Western templates were applied without local context—e.g., psychoanalyzing Christian iconography in Mamoru Oshii’s films without recognizing that Christian motifs in Japanese pop media often function as exotic aesthetic ornament rather than theological confession. Because visiting Japanese scholars mostly interact on panels with Japanese-speaking Area Studies scholars, this dominant non-Area Studies cohort remains largely invisible to Japanese academia.
3. **The "Gaze of Conquest" and Native Informants in Area Studies:** Conversely, Choo (2013) critiques North American Japanese Studies for a Cold War mentality of "conquering and compressing Japan" (*Nihon no seifuku to asshuku*). Unlike scholars of American politics or literature—never expected to master all of American society because the US is not treated as a "conquerable territory"—Japanese Studies pressures scholars to possess totalizing mastery over Japan as a closed simulacrum, maintaining narrow gatekeeping that permits only one recognized authority per subfield (e.g., one scholar for the anime industry). Thus, Area Studies compresses Japanese intellectual life through a rotating canon of Japanese "native informants," shifting over a generation from political theorist Masao Maruyama to literary critic Kōjin Karatani and, in the 2000s–2010s, to subculture/media critics Hiroki Azuma, Shinji Miyadai, and Eiji Ōtsuka (Choo, 2013).
4. **The *Mechademia* Network and Trans-Asian Decentralization:** As Nobuhiro Masuda (2013) documented at the 3rd *Mechademia* Conference in Seoul (*"World Renewal: Counterfactual Histories, Parallel Universes, and Possible Worlds"*, 18 panels at the Korean Film Archive and Dongguk University), while early *Mechademia* volumes emphasized close narrative readings, the conference united non-Japanese-Studies scholars from film/literary theory, sociology, and cultural studies across North America, Europe, Oceania, and Asia around two poles: empirical fieldwork on fan practices, *media mix*, and *kyara* (character) settings, and theoretical synthesis rubbing contemporary philosophy against popular visual culture outside Tokyo and North America.

### 1.3 Core Scholarly Focus Areas and Methodological Precautions

Within these directions, pre-2019 scholarship concentrated on **eight thematic and empirical clusters**:

#### 1. Textual Themes, Cultural Representation, and "Cinema of De-assurance"
Early studies treated anime as an extension of Japanese national literature and cultural history: Helen McCarthy (first English overview, 1993), Ron Tanner (1994), Annalee Newitz (1995), Antonia Levi (*Samurai from Outer Space*, 1996, and 1998), Mitsuru Yoshimoto (1996), Shunya Yoshimi (2000, situating TV anime in Japan's Americanization and broadcast history), Sharon Kinsella (2000), Anne Allison (2000), and Patrick Drazen (*Anime Explosion!*, 2003, surveying gods/demons, heroes/villains, death, and female roles). Levi (1996, 1998, p. 70, in Ruh, 2012) argued that against morally binary American superheroes, anime heroes offered psychological complexity and moral ambiguity filling a deficit in US pop culture.

In *Anime from Akira to Princess Mononoke* (2000/2001; rev. 2005 as *Anime from Akira to Howl’s Moving Castle*), Susan J. Napier characterized anime as a **"cinema of de-assurance"**—confronting globalization's uncertainties via moral ambiguity and open-ended narratives, vs. Hollywood's "cinema of reassurance." Analyzing bodily transformation, gender, technology, wartime memory, and critiques of progress, Napier categorized anime into **three expressive modes**: *apocalyptic* (global catastrophe, nuclear anxiety, societal collapse), *festival* (carnivalesque play and temporary hierarchy inversion), and *elegiac* (mourning lost time, vanishing nature, and nostalgic rural pasts, often combined with apocalyptic themes; Coar, 2017; Mowbry, 2007). Early ideological readings sparked internal critique: Newitz (1995, p. 12, in *Film Quarterly*, cited in Ruh, 2012) claimed US fans consuming conservative gender/violent tropes in anime were *"willingly or unwillingly in an imperialist relationship in which Japan is the dominant culture"*—which Ruh (2012) criticized for lacking empirical audience data and reviving early-20th-century Hollywood "yellow peril" rhetoric. Similarly, Sato’s (1999) reading of anime faces as a symptom of Japanese cultural inferiority was rebutted by Mizuki Takahashi’s (2008) history of *shōjo* manga aesthetics, while Kinsella (2000) and Sato (2006) interrogated essentialist *Nihonjinron* discourse.

#### 2. History, War, and Collective Memory
Set by Roman Rosenbaum’s edited *Manga and the Representation of Japanese History* (2013, cited 55 times), this cluster examines wartime memory. Michele Mason (2012), citing Tessa Morris-Suzuki’s *The Past Within Us*, argued historical manga *"may be no less important than any textbook in shaping popular understandings of history."* Mason contrasted Keiji Nakazawa’s survivor-witness "manga memoir" *Barefoot Gen* (1973–1985) against right-wing revisionist works like Yoshinori Kobayashi’s *Sensoron* (*On War*) and Sharin Yamano’s *Manga Kenkanryū* (*Hating the Korean Wave*), which downplay A-bomb memories to attack postwar Japan's "culture of anti-militarism" (Thomas Berger) when revisionist textbooks omitted A-bomb casualty counts. Mason mobilized John Whittier Treat’s critique of the "documentary fallacy" (survivor literature works via metaphor and subjective memory, not objective chronicle), Holocaust representation ethics (Richardson, using Art Spiegelman’s *Maus*), and Michael Rothberg’s "multidirectional memory." Related studies include Suzuki (2011, on Shigeru Mizuki’s *yōkai* and war manga), Penney (2012, on *kyara* and historiography), Chua (2013), and Rumi Sakamoto (2016).

#### 3. Gender, *Shōjo* Culture, and Boys' Love (BL) / Queer Studies
* ***Shōjo* Culture, Magical Girls, and the Body:** Masami Toku—proposing *"shōjo manga is said to be a mirror of Japanese women's desires and expectations"*—edited *International Perspectives on Shōjo and Shōjo Manga* and authored *Shōjo Manga! Girls' Comics! A Mirror of Girls' Dreams* (in Woods, 2015). Deborah Shamoon published *Passionate Friendship: The Aesthetics of Girls' Culture in Japan* (2012), and Limniati (2017) surveyed feminist manga approaches (1970–2000). Kumiko Saito’s (2014) *"Magic, Shōjo, and Metamorphosis"* traced three waves of magical-girl (*mahō shōjo*) TV, relating heroines to *shōjo* culture, adulthood, domestic expectations, and male fan expansion, showing empowerment fantasies coexist with conventional gender norms. Kaori Yoshida (2008) analyzed gender, racial, and national "Otherness" via postcolonial feminism; Woods (2015) examined cross-dressing; and Rayna Denison’s *"Anime's Bodies"* (2015) showed Japanese and Western audiences interpret female anime bodies differently.
* **Boys' Love (BL / *Yaoi*):** Mark McLelland’s *"The Love Between 'Beautiful Boys' in Japanese Women's Comics"* (2000, in Bryce et al., 2004) preceded the 14-essay collection *Boys' Love Manga* (2010), *Boys Love Manga and Beyond* (2014), Dru Pagliassotti’s *"Reading Boys' Love in the West"*, Björn-Ole Kamm’s entertainment-theory study (2013), Turner (2016) on queer/affective fan practices, and Zsila et al.’s (2017) review. Citing Yano Research Institute data, Zsila (2018) reported the domestic Japanese BL market (commercial publications and fan *dōjinshi*) at **2.2 billion yen** (single-source industry estimate).

#### 4. Film, Authorship, and Animation Media Theory
Challenging Area Studies sociology and live-action film theory, Paul Wells (*Understanding Animation*, 1998; *Animation: Genre and Authorship*, 2002), Joanna Bouldin (2000), and Yoshida (2008, pp. 6–8) argued **animation must be studied as a distinctive medium rather than a film genre**, with its own codes, grammar, and "morphotic" capacity to construct a "metaphysical reality" (citing Thomas Hoffer, 1981, p. 3). Wells (2002) emphasized animation is *"the most auteurist of film practices"*—exemplified by Hayao Miyazaki reversing standard Hollywood workflow by drawing frames *before* recording voices (*prescoring* vs. *postscoring*). Aarnoud Rommens (2000) identified manga's "analytical montage" as anime's narrative foundation, while Lamarre (2009, 2018), Tze-Yue Hu (2010), Yvonne Spielmann (2012), Deborah Levitt (*The Animatic Apparatus*, 2018), Christopher Bolton (*Interpreting Anime*, 2018), Suan (2017), and Jose Andrés Santiago Iglesias (2018) developed formal/media-theoretical frameworks (Section 2). Valérie Cools (2014) showed that while cultural motifs evoking "Japaneseness" initially attract Western viewers across manga, anime, and Japanese video games, their shared medium-based phenomenology of flux and stasis binds them into a coherent transcultural experience. Marco Pellitteri (2018) documented that *kawaii* ("cute") traits (1:1 or 1:2 head-to-body proportions, oversized eyes, pastel tones) migrated into European children's spontaneous drawings, confirmed by Italian developmental psychology studies (Bertolini & Manini, 1988, 1993) and Pellitteri’s workshops (2000–2016) in Palermo, Rome, Bolzano, and Brixen.

#### 5. *Otaku* / Fan Studies, Affect, Participatory Labor, and Language Pedagogy
The largest English-language cluster was shaped by Matt Hills’s (*Fan Cultures*, 2002) **"suspensionist" position** (treating internal fan contradictions as essential negotiations rather than fixing definitions) and Henry Jenkins’s (1992; *Convergence Culture*, 2006) participatory culture, transmedia storytelling, and "pop cosmopolitanism." Drawing on Ōtsuka’s narrative consumption (1989/2010) and Azuma’s *Otaku: Japan’s Database Animals* (2001/2009, cited >1,300 times), Patrick W. Galbraith (2009) combined theory with Tokyo participant observation (2004–2009) to examine *moe* (fetishized attributes like *tsundere* personalities, twin-tails, or cat ears) as an affective response to fantasy characters among male and female fans. Shinji Miyadai (2011) analyzed *otaku* behavioral systems; Mizuko Ito, Daisuke Okabe, and Izumi Tsuji (*Fandom Unbound*, 2012) reframed *otaku* culture transnationally; Teri Silvio (2010) and Jason Bainbridge (2011) theorized cosplay as performance and posthuman masquerade; and Anne Allison (*Millennial Monsters*, 2006) and Joseph Tobin (*Pikachu's Global Adventure*, 2004) situated anime within youth toy/card consumerism.

Empirically, Napier’s Western fan survey (2005; 2016 rev. ed., in Tomos, 2013) found **70% of surveyed Western fans cited "animation quality" as what attracted them to anime, whereas ~30% did not understand or could not decode specific Japanese cultural imagery**. Pedagogical studies examined Japanese-language acquisition via fandom: Garza, Williams (2006), Abdallah (2018, subtitled vs. dubbed learning), and Allison (2015, on "J-wave" youth appeal).

On participatory fan labor, Steinberg (2013) noted publishers use the Comic Market (Comiket)—where amateur *dōjinshi* creators and indie game developers sell unauthorized derivative works—as a recruitment pool, readable as both creative symbiosis and **corporate appropriation of unpaid fan labor**. Conversely, in his Seoul *Mechademia* keynote *"The Era of Allegorical Dysfunction"* (*Gūwa kinō fuzen no jidai*), Ōtsuka used structuralist narrative theory (Vladimir Propp, Joseph Campbell) across *Mobile Suit Gundam*, *Star Wars*, Osamu Dazai, and Haruki Murakami to critique attributing subversive critical potential to *otaku* and *moe* consumption (Masuda, 2013). Outside Japan, Denison (2011), Hye-Kyung Lee (2011), Douglas Schules (2014), and Suan (2018, on *sakuga* fans cataloging expressive animation cuts) analyzed overseas fan translation, re-editing, and evaluation. In *The Soul of Anime* (2013, Ch. 6; reviewed by Shamoon, 2014), Ian Condry conceptualized unpaid overseas fansubbing as **"dark energy"** and civil disobedience aimed at improving the industry (*"the greater the circulation is, the more value is created"*), persisting even after Crunchyroll launched legal simulcasts in 2009–2010 (Ruh, 2012).

#### 6. Industrial Political Economy, Studio Ethnography, and Archival Benchmarks
Steinberg (*Anime’s Media Mix*, 2012), Bryan Hikari Hartzheim (2015, 2017), Manuel Hernández-Pérez (2018, on Netflix's European anime operations), and Jonathan Clements (*Anime: A History*, 2013) examined commercial franchises, crowdfunding (Loriguillo-López, 2017), and film policy (Kawashima, 2018). Externally sourced figures reflect differing measurement scopes rather than contradiction:
* **Export & Global Broadcast Shares:** By the 1980s, Japan was a net TV exporter, with **56% of Japanese TV exports consisting of animation** (Stronach, 1989, p. 128, in Ruh, 2012), while Toei Animation derived **~40%** of revenue overseas (Osaki, 2003, in Ruh, 2012). Yamazaki (2003, p. 14, in Ruh, 2012) reported anime accounted for **>50% of animated films shown worldwide**; JETRO (in Condry & Steinberg, 2013) and Brown (2006, in Tomos, 2013) reported anime accounted for **60% of the world's broadcast TV cartoons**; and Amy Shirong Lu (2008) cited a single-source estimate claiming anime accounted for **nearly one-third** of global media market revenue in 2003.
* **Annual Production Output:** Clements and McCarthy (*The Anime Encyclopedia*, 2015 rev. ed.) documented **135,530 minutes** of new animation produced annually in Japan, while Niracharapa Tongdhamachart (2015) reported **>160 new TV animated series and 40–50 feature films** annually, with *Sazae-san* holding the Guinness World Record as the longest-running TV animated series (**>2,250 episodes** since 1969; Davis, 2016).
* **Cross-Media Franchise & Convention Benchmarks (Davis, 2016):** By 2013, Eiichiro Oda’s *One Piece* reached a global print run of **345 million copies** (2013 single-volume sales were **~8.7 times** DC’s *Justice League* #1); at the peak of *Dragon Ball* and *Slam Dunk*, *Weekly Shōnen Jump*’s 1995 New Year issue sold **>6.53 million copies**; *Mobile Suit Gundam* net sales rose from **$560 million** (FY2013) to **~$688 million** (FY2014); US comic sales grew **+6%** in 2013 (ICv2 data, manga outpacing graphic novels); and Los Angeles’s Anime Expo drew **>80,000 unique visitors** (**220,000 turnstile attendance**) in 2014 before streaming bidding wars erupted in 2015.

#### 7. Religion, Space, and Contents Tourism (*Seichi Junrei*)
Jolyon Baraka Thomas’s *Drawing on Tradition: Manga, Anime, and Religion in Contemporary Japan* (2012) combined narrative/visual analysis, history, and ethnography to show how religious meanings are actively constructed via production and reception. In tourism geography, **"contents tourism"** (*kontentsu tsūrizumu*)—defined by Nelson Graburn as *"tourism stimulated by popular culture"*—was institutionalized in Japan in the 1990s alongside "multi-use content," gained state attention after the 2004 Korean drama *Winter Sonata* boom, and entered policy documents in 2005 (Sugawa-Shimada, 2015). Philip Seaton and Takayoshi Yamamura (2015 [online 2014]) emphasized contents tourism focuses *"not on the media form, but primarily on the contents—namely narratives, characters, and locations."* Its core anime practice is ***seichi junrei* ("pilgrimage to sacred sites")**, defined by Galbraith (2009) as fans *"making pilgrimages to places that appear in anime and taking photographs identical to the anime scenes."* Yamamura (2014) documented **Washimiya** (Saitama Prefecture, setting of *Lucky Star*), showing how the local community built a mutually beneficial relationship with fans and copyright holders until the community itself became a "medium" via museums, monuments, and websites; Yamamura (2018) also documented heritage revitalization in Shiroishi City (Miyagi Prefecture). Related studies include Takeshi Okamoto’s (2015, cited 218 times) overview of *otaku* tourism, Akiko Sugawa-Shimada’s (2015) analysis of *rekijo* (female history buffs) and "pop-spiritualism," and Mason and Rohe’s (2018) study of fan-scene management.

#### 8. Methodological Precautions and Decentering Anglo-American Reception
Three precautions govern pre-2019 scholarship: (1) **Topic vs. Method:** Studying a topic (e.g., gender or Japanese culture) does not identify which methodological operation a researcher performs (character representation, voice performance, workplace access, marketing demographics, or fan practice); (2) **Selectivity of the Export Archive:** Technologies and formal/informal distribution logics filter which anime travel abroad and which stay in Japan (Denison, 2018); and (3) **Decentering a Single "Western" or "American" Reception History:** Denison (2007) criticized equating anime's globalization with "Americanization." Rich regional literatures exist across **continental Europe** (Pellitteri, *Mazinga Nostalgia*, 1999/2008/2015, *The Dragon and the Dazzle*, 2010, and 2014b; Jean-Marie Bouissou, *Manga goes global*, 2000; Bouissou, Pellitteri, Dolle-Weinkauff, & Beldi, *Manga in Europe*, 2010), **East/Southeast Asia** (Koichi Iwabuchi, 2001, 2002; Nissim Otmazgin, 2008, 2013; Nam, 2013; Tongdhamachart, 2015), **Latin America** (Khattab & Tong, 2008; Tania L. Cobos, 2010; Pino, 2018 dissertation on Mexico City), and **North Africa and the Middle East** (Pellitteri, 2011 *Mangatopia* chapter; Otmazgin, 2013, on Israel and the Arab-world sweep of *Captain Tsubasa* as *Captain Majed*).

## Section 2: Viewing Anime as a "Representational Text" vs. a "Media Form"

### 2.1 Paradigm Divergence: Core Distinctions and Two Analytical Levels of "Media Form"

Treating anime as a **"representational text"** reflecting Japanese society and culture versus a **"media form"** with its own specific properties maps directly onto the central divide in pre-2019 anime studies. Ruh (2012) structured his review around *"readings of anime as a reflection of Japanese culture"* vs. *"studies that view anime as a medium/industrial/transnational hybrid form,"* while Suan (2017) explicitly opposed "media-form" analysis to national-cultural branding narratives.

In explanatory priority, **a representational-text approach asks *what* an anime makes meaningful about Japanese society, history, or identity (using anime as evidence to read Japan), whereas a media-form approach asks *how* its technical, formal, and industrial configuration operates, circulates, and shapes perception.** Across the pre-2019 literature, **"media form" operates across two distinct analytical levels that must not be conflated**:
* **Level B(i) — Formal-Technical Aesthetics, Perceptual Apparatus, and "Animesque" Grammar:** How cel layering, limited/selective animation timing, montage, color palettes, and repeatable performative conventions organize moving-image perception (Lamarre, 2002, 2009; Steinberg, 2006; Suan, 2017; Levitt, 2018; Santiago Iglesias, 2018).
* **Level B(ii) — Cross-Media Industrial Ecology (*Media Mix*) and Circulation Networks:** How those formal properties operate within character licensing franchises, production committees, TV schedules, and formal/informal distribution worlds (Steinberg, 2012; Condry, 2013; Denison, 2018; Lamarre, 2018). "Media form" is thus broader than the commercial *media mix* model alone.

As Yoshida (2008, pp. 6–10) observes, bridging these paradigms requires recognizing a double specificity: first, animation as a medium possesses distinctive visual codes, grammar, and a **"morphotic" form of expression (Wells, 1998; Lamarre, 2002; Bouldin, 2000) creating a "metaphysical reality" distanced from live-action photography (Hoffer, 1981, p. 3)**, giving creators flexibility to reinforce dominant ideologies (*myth-making*) or subvert them (*demythologizing*); second, anime as a globally distributed object since the 1980s–1990s operates as a historical site where Japanese identity is articulated in relation to the West and Asia.

### 2.2 Path A: The "Representational Text" Approach — Points of Focus and Methodological Critiques

Representational-text scholars investigate how fictional constructions articulate historical and social problems across **eight primary points of focus**:
1. **Postwar Identity Politics, Trauma, and Sociological Mirrors:** Napier (2001) read Tetsuo’s bodily mutation in *Akira* as *"coded on an ideological level as a reflection of Japan's own deep-seated ambivalence at this time—partly revelling in its new identity, but also terrified by it,"* with her core theme being *"the shifting nature of identity in a constantly changing society."* Allison (2000) read *Astro Boy* as *"a new kind of hero through which a generation of war-scarred Japanese could begin to re-envision their nation."* Bryce et al. (2004) read school anime as *"an important sociological window into the lives of Japanese children,"* while sci-fi themes of humanity and technology were read as a reflexive Japanese cultural dialogue (Tomos, 2013, pp. 19–20).
2. **Nation, War, and Collective Postwar Memory:** Napier (2001/2005), Mason (2012), Rosenbaum (2013), and Coar (2017) analyze urban obliteration, discrimination against *hibakusha* (including Korean A-bomb survivors), postwar poverty, the US "atomic bomb myth," and right-wing revisionist assaults on postwar anti-militarism as historical evidence of collective memory (Section 1.3).
3. **Indigenous Myths, Folklore, and Traditional Japanese Aesthetics:** Drazen (2003, in Tomos, 2013, p. 39) argued anime *"is never made for export, and therefore primarily reflects Japanese cultural myths and social behavior."* Tomos (2013) investigated whether traditional aesthetics—***wabi-sabi*** (imperfect transience), ***mono no aware*** (pathos of things), ***utsushi*** (projection/reflection), and Miyazaki’s ***ma*** (intentional emptiness/pause between actions)—remain *"determining factors in the development of anime."* Levi (2001) showed how Western localizations of *Sailor Moon* erased folklore: in Japan, Usagi Tsukino ("Moon Rabbit") evokes the rabbit in the moon, whereas the US version renamed her "Serena" (losing rabbit imagery) and German, French, and Italian versions named her "Bunny" (losing moon imagery), so *"the original meaning was lost in the transfer."* Similarly, Studio Ghibli director Isao Takahata’s *Jūni seki no animēshon* (*Twelfth-Century Animation*) located cinematic/animetic features in 12th-century illustrated handscrolls (*emaki*), specifically the *Chōjū jinbutsu giga* comic animal scrolls (Lamarre, 2009, pp. 12–15).
4. **Gender, Sexuality, and the *Shōjo* Archetype:** Scholars read passive male protagonists, battling heroines, magical-girl transformations, and the *shōjo* (a young girl in a liminal space of freedom before marriage and social reproduction) as negotiations of post-bubble stagnation, shifting domestic expectations, and tensions between empowerment and objectification (Napier, 2005; Levi, 1998; Yoshida, 2008; Shamoon, 2012; Saito, 2014; Toku, 2015).
5. **Technology and the Posthuman Body:** Studies examine cyborgs, AI, prosthesis, memory, and autonomy in *Ghost in the Shell* and *Akira* to interrogate boundaries between person, machine, animal, and environment (Napier, 2001/2005; Bolton, 2018).
6. **Religious and Environmental Imaginaries:** Studies highlight Shinto animism, sacred landscapes, ecological conflict, karmic cycles, and the soul in Ghibli films or *Fullmetal Alchemist*, distinguishing anime from US broadcast cartoons (Napier, 2001/2005; Thomas, 2012; Tongdhamachart, 2015; Study, 2015).
7. **Cross-Cultural "Cultural Learning" and National-Image Surveys:** Scholars test how much "Japaneseness" foreign viewers extract (Abdallah, 2018) or how manga/anime consumption shapes national stereotypes of Japan across six dimensions in France and Italy (Pellitteri, c. 2014–2015).
8. **Postcolonial "Otherness" (Western Orientalism, Self-Orientalism, Strategic Essentialism, and Japanese Occidentalism):** Yoshida (2008, pp. ii, 21–48) shows globally distributed anime operates across a three-way West–Japan–Asia power dynamic rooted in Japan's anomalous history as the only non-Western nation to have intensively experienced being both an imperial colonizer (Taiwan from 1895, Korea, and parts of China and Southeast Asia until 1945) and a colonized/subordinated subject (Meiji "cultural colonization" and the 1945–1952 Allied/US occupation).

Yoshida (2008) dissects this postcolonial matrix through **four mechanisms**:
* **Western Orientalism and "Yellowness":** Drawing on Edward Said’s *Orientalism* (1978)—figuring the West as rational, modern, and masculine and the East as emotional, traditional, enigmatic, and feminine, predicated on the Orient's "effective absence"—and Jun Xing’s three Hollywood Asian archetypes (*yellow peril*, *Madame Butterfly*, *Charlie Chan*), Yoshida (2008, pp. 21–33) shows how Western media simultaneously demonize and feminize East Asians, representing Japan as either a peril or a silent victim.
* **Self-Orientalism and the "Colonization of the Imagination":** Citing Said (1978, p. 322), Jessica Hagedorn, and Iwabuchi (2002), Yoshida (2008, pp. 23–24, 41–42) explains how non-Western subjects internalize and perform Western fantasies for global markets. As Iwabuchi notes, *"the complicity between Western Orientalism and Japan's self-Orientalism effectively works only when Japanese cultural power in Asia is subsumed under Japan's cultural subordination to the West"*—where the West is the modernized "Other" and Asia is Japan's backward past (though rapid East Asian modernization from the 1990s increasingly challenged this triad; Yoshida, 2008, p. 42, n. 54).
* **Strategic Essentialism Between West and Asia:** Drawing on Gayatri Spivak’s (1988, pp. 13–14) strategic essentialism (a marginalized group consciously defining itself in generalizing, positivist terms to reclaim political control over its identity narrative), Yoshida (2008, pp. 37–40) traces how modern Japan alternated between Meiji-era *datsua nyūō* ("escape from Asia, enter the West," stressing similarity to the white West against "backward" Asian neighbors, culminating in Nazi Germany and South Africa declaring Japanese "honorary Aryans") and wartime *daitōa kyōeiken* ("Greater East Asia Co-prosperity Sphere," strategically essentializing pan-Asian unity against Western imperialism).
* **Japanese Occidentalism, "Oriental Orientalism," and Homi Bhabha’s Mimicry in Miyazaki’s Anime:** Positioning Miyazaki via Bhabha’s *mimicry* and *hybridity* as Walt Disney’s postcolonial "double" (*"almost the same, but not quite"*), Yoshida (2008, pp. 13–14, 46–48) contrasts Disney features (*Aladdin*, 1992; *Pocahontas*, 1995; *Mulan*, 1998) against four Miyazaki films: (a) in *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* (1984) and *Porco Rosso* (1992), Miyazaki practices Japanese Occidentalism, constructing the Western "Other" as an exoticized fantasy space while rediscovering female agency; (b) in *Princess Mononoke* (1997), Miyazaki excavates marginalized internal "Others" in medieval Japan (San, the forest god Shishigami, and the cursed boar *tatarigami*) to subvert both Western Orientalism and Japanese self-Orientalism; and (c) in *Spirited Away* (2001), Miyazaki stages "Oriental Orientalism"—an Eastern culture "othering" another East—while opening a hybridized "Third Space" destabilizing fixed national hierarchies.

**Documented Blind Spots and Methodological Critiques of the Representational Paradigm:**
1. **Conflating Depiction, Endorsement, and Reception:** Research must distinguish three propositions requiring different evidence: (a) a work *depicts* a social norm/anxiety; (b) it *endorses* that norm; and (c) domestic or foreign viewers *accept* it.
2. **Essentialism, the *Nihonjinron* Trap, and Hybrid Origins:** Brian Moeran (1989) and Morris-Suzuki (1988) showed *Nihonjinron* is a constructed nationalist discourse. Ruh (2012) cautioned *"it is impossible to distill any essential 'Japaneseness' from it... Japanese animation itself is already a hybrid form,"* while Northrop Davis’s (*Manga and Anime Go to Hollywood*, 2016) transpacific "ping-pong" model confirmed modern anime was forged under deep Hollywood and Disney influence.
3. **Internal Disagreements over *Mukokuseki* ("Stateless/Racial Erasure"):** Against Iwabuchi’s (2002) argument that *mukokuseki* ("stateless") character designs erase Japanese racial traits (supported by Mamoru Oshii stating Japanese animators *"unconsciously choose not to draw 'realistic' Japanese characters"* and his own are *"modeled on Caucasian types"*), Marc Hairston (1999) and Michael Arnold (2004) objected that many characters retain black hair and dark eyes, while Ruh (2014, in Raes, 2018) argued anime characters are stylized symbolic abstractions rather than intentional depictions of Caucasian or specific racial phenotypes.
4. **Lamarre’s Critique of Three Commentaries That Erase the Medium (2009, pp. ix–xxxvii):** Lamarre cataloged three commentary types that erase animation as a medium: (a) *The "Book Report or Film Review" Model:* summarizing plot and themes (giant robots, cyborgs, techno-apocalypse) as if the work were a live-action movie or novel, akin to *"comparing conclusions without addressing arguments"* (p. xxx); (b) *"High Textual" Speculative Readings:* using plots as springboards for abstract philosophical questions about reality or the mind-body split while bypassing screen visual mechanics (p. ix); and (c) *Sociological Reflectionism and Cultural Determinism:* treating anime as a direct dataset or encrypted allegory of Japanese youth pathology, where scholars **"wind up with cultural determinism, endlessly pointing and proclaiming, 'This is Japan, this is Japanese'"** (p. xxvii), or fall back on the 19th-century nationalist slogan *wakon yōsai* ("Japanese spirit and Western technologies"; pp. xxxv, 14–15).

### 2.3 Path B: The "Media Form" Approach — Formal Grammar (B-i) and Ecological Mechanisms (B-ii)

#### Level B(i): Formal-Technical Specificity, Perceptual Apparatus, and "Animesque" Grammar

1. **The Animation Stand, Compositing, and the "Animetic Interval" (Lamarre, 2002, 2009, 2013, 2014):**
   In *"From Animation to Anime"* (2002), Lamarre argued limited animation *"fashions a new mode of viewing and consumption that involves scanning, rereading, searching for information... the spectator experience produced by anime is very much like the experience of informatization itself."* In *The Anime Machine* (2009, pp. xxiii–xxix, 15–17), Lamarre grounded cel animation in the **animation stand**—a physical rack developed after John Bray introduced transparent celluloid sheets in 1914, stacking painted cel layers under a fixed overhead rostrum camera. Because the camera is largely immobile, animators cannot easily move forward into 3D depth; instead, mechanical force is channeled into **the gaps between stacked celluloid layers—the *animetic interval***—managed via **compositing** (internal editing of stacked layers within a single frame, vs. film montage) and craft practices like Miyazaki and Yasuo Ōtsuka’s **"peg-hole technique"** (rotating key drawings on registration pegs for visual disequilibrium). Lamarre identified **three kinds of compositing** (2009, p. xxiv): (a) **Closed compositing:** suppresses gaps between layers so background and foreground move seamlessly, imitating live-action volumetric depth (Disney's multiplane camera); (b) **Open compositing:** leaves movement between layers loose and visible as layers slide at different speeds, creating a multiplanar image; and (c) **Flat (or superplanar) compositing:** brings stacked layers directly to the flat screen surface, eliminating depth cues for dense planar graphics (extended by Lamarre in 2013 as "radical perspectivism" and in *Animating Film Theory*, 2014, and by Levitt’s *The Animatic Apparatus*, 2018, on perceptual modulation).
2. **"Animetism" vs. "Cinematism" and Technical Underdetermination (Lamarre, 2009, pp. 3–11, xxv–xxxiii):**
   Lamarre distinguished **cinematism** (live-action cinema and multiplane animation using mobile cameras to plunge forward into depth, aligning the eye with a speeding bullet or locomotive to collapse distance) from **animetism** (cel animation separating the image into flat planes and organizing movement laterally across and between surfaces, like looking sideways out a train window as layered landscapes slide past, as in Ōtomo’s *Steamboy* and Hideaki Anno’s Gainax works). Drawing on Gilbert Simondon and Félix Guattari, Lamarre avoided technological determinism via **technical underdetermination**: because the apparatus contains a "center of indetermination," animators "think through technology" by aligning human thought with the sliding planes and intervals of the image.
3. **From "Limited Animation" to "Dynamic Immobility" and "Selective Animation":**
   Whereas Disney's full animation draws 18–24 unique frames/second, Japanese TV anime uses **limited animation** (8 frames/second or fewer; Villot, 2013), pioneered commercially by Tezuka’s Mushi Production on *Tetsuwan Atomu* in January 1963 to meet weekly TV deadlines on tiny budgets via minimal mouth movements, reused flying backgrounds, and held static poses (Condry, 2013). Although Miyazaki blamed Tezuka for locking the industry into low budgets, this constraint unexpectedly made the style attractive to Hollywood and foreign audiences (Davis, 2016). Recent formal scholarship replaces "limited animation" with Sheuo Hui Gan’s term **"selective animation"** (in Santiago Iglesias, 2018) to capture converting *"economic limitations into aesthetic resources"* (Maríaul Horno-López, 2014, pp. 89–90). In *"Immobile Sections and Trans-Series Movement"* (2006, pp. 190–206) and *Anime’s Media Mix* (2012), Steinberg conceptualized this as **"dynamic immobility"**: holding an expressive static pose while vibrating the frame, pulling the cel across a background, or adding speed lines creates dynamically immobile images matching the static form of manga panels, Meiji Seika chocolate stickers, and plastic toys.
4. **Performative "Media-Form" Identity (Suan, 2017) and Neighboring Concepts (*Anime-ic*, *Mangaesque*):**
   In *"Anime's Performativity: Diversity through Conventionality in a Global Media-Form"* (2017), Suan defines anime as a **"media-form"** rather than a "genre" to avoid genre-theory scale dilemmas (citing Denison’s 2015 "genres as sub-languages"): *"a globally prominent media-form that exhibits a variety of styles yet maintains a relative uniformity to sustain its recognizable identity as a specific media category."* Combining Judith Butler’s repetition of stylized acts, Azuma’s database consumption, and Donald Crafton’s **"figurative acting"** (characters performing as recognizable "types," vs. Disney's **"embodied acting"**), Suan argues anime's identity is **"performative—constructed through the performance of a series of anime-esque acts... citing a system/database of conventionalized models in every iteration."** Thus, *"what we recognize as 'anime proper' is not simply 'animation from Japan,' but animation that executes a large number of anime-esque acts"*—meaning **anime "does not rely on Japan as a defining attribute."** Neighboring concepts include Thomas Looser’s (2002) **"anime-ic"** (a multi-layered structure where mixed styles/media converge on a single plane without origin, expanded by Ruh, 2015) and **"mangaesque"** (Pellitteri, 2018, encompassing stylistic traits and prosumer attitudes toward production/distribution).
5. **The Six Operationalized Dimensions of the "Animesque" (Santiago Iglesias, 2018):**
   Santiago Iglesias (2018) operationalized this grammar into **six verifiable dimensions**: (1) **inter-episodic narrative continuity** (vs. episodic US cartoons); (2) **generalization of suspense and *eucatastrophe*** (sudden liberatory turns as narrative decompression); (3) **iconic character design** (*"neither cartoon nor realistic, but based on the suppression of superfluous elements,"* following Suan); (4) **distinctive color palettes** (*"scenes with an abundance of tertiary colors and lower saturation... narrating through color"*); (5) **volume expressed through movement** (moving the "camera" across static cel layers rather than moving the character); and (6) **montage grammar** (multi-shot openings—counting **8 opening shots** in *Future Boy Conan* and **5 opening shots** in *Heidi, Girl of the Alps* and *Mazinger Z*—plus rapid "isometric shot" editing and "pillow shots" derived from Japanese poetics; Shamoon, 2011). Because this grammar is formal rather than genetic, it migrates transnationally: 1979–1985 Euro-Japanese co-productions were hybrids *"neither completely Western nor completely Japanese"* (Santiago Iglesias, 2018), while US TV animation writers influenced by Tezuka, *Captain Harlock*, *Macross*, and *Evangelion* absorbed these conventions until US TV became *"filled with heavily anime-inspired animation to the point where it's almost the norm"* (writer Eugene Son in Davis, 2016).

#### Level B(ii): Infrastructure, Cross-Media Ecology (*Media Mix*), and Distribution Worlds

1. **The *Media Mix* (*Media Mikkusu*) vs. Transmedia Storytelling:**
   Steinberg (*Anime’s Media Mix*, 2012, p. viii; reviewed by Li, 2014; Monk-Payton, 2014; Hirano, 2014; Drew, 2014; Annett, 2014) defines the *media mix* as *"the cross-media serialization and circulation of entertainment franchises"* operating *"by overlapping the commodity image with the character"* (see also Lamarre, 2015, on regional geography, and *The Anime Ecology*, 2018). Calling it "anime's life support system," Steinberg (2013) traces it from Tezuka's 1963 *Astro Boy* toy/sticker licensing and Haruki Kadokawa’s 1970s cross-marketing of novels, films, and theme songs to late-1980s/early-1990s Kadokawa Books editors (Tsuguhiko Kadokawa, Eiji Ōtsuka on *MPD Psycho*, Ryō Mizuno on *Record of the Lodoss War*), who drew on North American tabletop RPGs (*Dungeons & Dragons*) to act as "game masters" building expandable worlds. Ruh (2012) and Steinberg and Condry (2013) contrast Jenkins’s (2006) US *transmedia storytelling*—where elements fuse into *"a single narrative whole"* with strict canonical consistency—against the Japanese *media mix*, where *"each medium tells the story in its own way, and they cannot be fully reconciled."* Anchored by the character (combining physical *materiality* with portable *immateriality* / *kyara*) rather than a unified plot, the *media mix* tolerates narrative contradiction across platforms.
2. **Narrative Consumption vs. Database Consumption and Character-First Production:**
   Scholars trace a shift from Ōtsuka’s (1989/2010) **narrative consumption** (1980s consumers buying fragments like stickers or episodes to reconstruct an overarching worldview / "grand narrative" / *sekaikan*) to Azuma’s (2001/2009) **database consumption** (postmodern *otaku* consuming a flat database of non-narrative *moe* character traits across plotless works like *Lucky Star* or Hatsune Miku). Condry (2013) confirmed this character-first mechanism ethnographically at a Tokyo production meeting with Bandai Visual producers planning *Code Geass*: producers spent an entire hour detailing characters and world rules without mentioning the plot, prompting Condry's Japanese colleague to explain, **"They probably haven't written the story yet."** Both Steinberg and Condry (2013) caution, however, that commercial character circulation only survives when it generates meaningful human **"social energy"** across collaborative networks.
3. **Formal/Informal Distribution Worlds and the Political Economy of Distribution Power:**
   Denison (2018) divides anime's transnational circulation into "formal" (licensed dubs, DVDs, legal streaming) and "informal" (fansubbing, piracy) worlds, driven by fan **"dark energy"** (Condry, 2013). At the macro-industrial level, Iwabuchi (2002), citing Nicholas Garnham’s (1990) law of political economy, emphasizes *"it is cultural distribution, not cultural production, that is the key locus of power and profit"*—explaining why Japanese hardware giants (Sony, Matsushita) acquired Hollywood studios in the late 1980s–1990s to secure global distribution networks, and why anime remained structurally dependent on Western distribution channels to reach global mass markets.

### 2.4 Concrete Illustration and Methodological Synthesis: Combining Both Paths Without Collapsing Them

Consider **a relatively still television image of a young robot (*Astro Boy*), accompanied by a moving mouth and voice soundtrack, followed by a burst of action, and the same robot figure printed on a Meiji Seika confectionery sticker or toy**:
* **Representational-Text Inquiry (Path A):** Asks whether the young robot dramatizes the social obligations of a postwar child, reconstruction of a war-scarred nation (Allison, 2000), or ethical perils of technology, via dialogue, narrative treatment, and historical intertexts.
* **Media-Form and Industrial Inquiry (Path B):** Examines *which* drawn elements move, how the held pose establishes "dynamic immobility" (Steinberg, 2006, pp. 190–192), how voice/sound supply continuity across static frames, and how that pose circulates into print and chocolate stickers to subsidize a broadcast fee (500,000 yen/episode) that did not cover Mushi Production's animation costs.
* **Audience Reception Inquiry:** Tests via surveys/observation which formal or narrative aspects viewers notice, value, or appropriate (e.g., Napier's finding that 70% of Western fans prioritize animation quality while 30% miss Japanese cultural references).

Leading scholars frequently bridge both poles: (1) **Cross-Paradigm Integration:** Iwabuchi (2002) combined media economics (Hoskins & Mirus's cultural discount) with cultural semiotics; Denison (2007, 2015) and Laz Carter (2018) combined textual representations of "Japaneseness" in Studio Ghibli's *Spirited Away* with transnational distribution packaging ("glocalization"); Nicholas Raes (2018) showed Western *Pokémon* localizers performed a "balancing act" between representational odor removal and exotic formal appeal; and Bolton (*Interpreting Anime*, 2018) argued anime spectatorship oscillates between emotional involvement in represented characters and self-reflexive awareness of their technological construction as moving drawings. (2) **Suan’s *"Consuming Production"* (2018, sections 2.4–3):** Suan analyzes the TV anime *Shirobako* (2014–2015) first as a *representational text* depicting everyday workplace struggles at a fictional Tokyo studio, and then compares that narrative against the *media-form and industrial reality* documented in the series' own end credits and *sakuga* fan practices. While *Shirobako* represents anime production as a purely domestic Japanese workplace endeavor, the actual episode credits reveal vital transnational labor from outside Japan—including South Korean episode direction—exposing precisely what the narrative representation omits.

## Section 3: Comparative Analysis of Japanese TV Anime and "Nordic Noir" TV Dramas (Through 2018)

### 3.1 Analytical Framing: State of the Pre-2019 Literature and Three Structural Asymmetries

Comparing Japanese TV anime and Scandinavian crime TV drama (**Nordic Noir**) across five dimensions rests on **three foundational premises**:
1. **State of the Pre-2019 Comparative Literature:** Before 2019, no standalone academic study systematically compared Japanese TV anime and Nordic Noir across all five dimensions. The closest juxtaposition was Roy Stafford’s *The Global Film Book* (Routledge, 2014, Ch. 9, *"Global Television"*, pp. 235–263), which under *"Contra-flows in the international TV market"* briefly mentioned trade in *"Japanese anime and Korean drama"* alongside the UK/US vogue for *"'Nordic Noir' or 'Scandi-drama'"* and European niche subtitled drama (focusing Case Study 9.2 on Danish serials, *Borgen*, and Yellow Bird's Swedish/German *Wallander*, while treating anime separately in Ch. 5). Below synthesizes both independent bodies of pre-2019 scholarship.
2. **Asymmetry of Category Levels (Media-Form vs. Television Genre):** "Anime" is a broad animation **media-form** spanning all narrative genres, and its macro-statistics often bundle TV anime, features, and merchandise. "Nordic Noir" is a specific **live-action crime-fiction and regional branding formation** crossing literature, film, and TV (originating in Scandinavian crime novels, a 2010 BBC documentary *Nordic Noir*, Arrow Films' UK DVD label, and Barry Forshaw’s *Death in a Cold Climate*, 2012, and *Nordic Noir*, 2013; Creeber, 2015; Hansen & Waade, *Locating Nordic Noir*, 2017; Hansen, Peacock, & Turnbull, 2018). While "Scandinavia" strictly refers to Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, "Nordic" includes Iceland and Finland.
3. **Asymmetry of Geographic Footprints and Measurement Metrics:** Nordic Noir's primary overseas strongholds are Western Europe (especially Germany, the UK, and Benelux) and Anglophone prestige markets, where scholars possess granular per-series measurements (UK BARB broadcast ratings and European LUMIERE cinema admissions). Japanese TV anime penetrated mass children's TV across East/Southeast Asia, Latin America, the Middle East, and Western Europe from the 1970s–1980s, but is primarily measured via macro-industrial turnover, export revenue, or broadcast shares rather than per-series rating sequences.

#### Key Historical Nuances in Both Timelines:
* **European First Boom and the Hollywood Live-Action Remake Branch in Anime:** Following 1975–1978 premieres in Spain, Italy, and France, Italy's 1978–1984 boom made it *"the Western market with the highest number of broadcast television anime and theatrically released anime films"* (Pellitteri, 2014b), while France had two dedicated anime channels by 1993 and Spain's boom peaked 5–10 years later in the early 1990s with TV liberalization and *Dragon Ball* (Pellitteri, 2018; Santiago Iglesias, 2018). In North America, Hollywood pursued sporadic live-action adaptations (Davis, 2016): the Wachowskis’ *The Matrix* (1999, pitched to investors by producer Joel Silver using *Ghost in the Shell*) and *Speed Racer* (2008, judged by Davis as most faithful in spirit/style to anime); Fox’s commercial/critical failure *Dragonball Evolution* (2009); and projects in development as of 2016 including *Akira* (Leonardo DiCaprio’s Appian Way), DreamWorks’ *Ghost in the Shell* (Scarlett Johansson, cast 2015 for April 14, 2017 release), James Cameron/Fox’s *Alita: Battle Angel*, Guillermo del Toro and Naoki Urasawa’s HBO pitch for *Monster*, and *Death Note*. Unlike Nordic Noir's hit English remakes, Hollywood live-action anime remakes remained sporadic and largely unsuccessful.
* **Three-Step Trajectory of Nordic Noir and Territory Count Reconciliation:** Pia Majbritt Jensen and Ushma Chauhan Jacobsen (2017, pp. 13–14) traced a three-step trajectory: series moved first across Nordic sister countries and Germany (largest single foreign market; ZDF, 2013), entered **the UK's BBC Four in January 2011 as the decisive international "shop window" and "stepping stone"**—where *"in the space of just five years, Denmark went from being a relatively insignificant exporter of television... to an international player punching well above its weight"* (Jensen, Nielsen, & Waade, 2015; Turnbull, 2015; Waade, 2017)—and cascaded globally (Australia’s SBS was an early exception importing *Rejseholdet* a decade prior). Reported sales counts vary slightly by date and territory vs. regional footprint definitions: Bondebjerg (2016) and Stougaard-Nielsen (2016) report **100+ countries** for *Forbrydelsen* and **130+ countries** for *Bron/Broen* (also Schröder, 2015; *The Guardian*, Jan 2014); Jensen and Jacobsen (2017) report up to **120 territories** for DR exports (*Arvingerne*, *1864*, *Bedrag*); and Degn, Jensen, and Krogager (2015) report **150+ countries and territories** each for *Forbrydelsen* and *Bron/Broen* and **~80 territories** (vs. ~70 countries in Redvall, 2013) for *Borgen*.
* **Granular Audience Measurements for Nordic Noir (Static Baseline vs. Dynamic 10x Expansion):**
  * *Cinema Admissions Breakdown (Hedling, 2014):* Oplev’s 2009 Swedish film *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* sold **>1,217,000 tickets** in Sweden, **1,500,000 tickets** combined in Denmark and Norway, and released on **554 prints** in France. By 2011, the European Audiovisual Observatory’s LUMIERE database recorded **9,392,228 admissions across 38 European countries and the US** (Hedling estimating **>10 million** global paid viewers across 50+ countries). Using the 9,392,228 LUMIERE total as denominator, domestic Swedish admissions accounted for **13.0%** ($1,217,000 \div 9,392,228$), Denmark and Norway combined for **16.0%** ($1,500,000 \div 9,392,228$), and the other 36 non-Scandinavian markets for **71.1%** ($6,675,228$ admissions). Yet at the UK box office, Oplev’s subtitled original grossed **$2,342,433** vs. **$18,796,728** for David Fincher’s 2011 English remake (an **8.0 : 1** ratio; Mazdon in Hedling, 2014).
  * *UK Television Ratings — Static Comparison vs. Dynamic Growth (Hedling, 2014; Esser, 2017):* On BBC One, the first three episodes of Kenneth Branagh’s English *Wallander* drew **5.72M to 6.63M** viewers, whereas the first 13 episodes of subtitled Swedish *Wallander* on BBC Four averaged **<500,000** (<1/10th of the remake; Hedling, 2014). However, Andrea Esser (2017, Table 3) tracked the **dynamic tenfold expansion** of BBC Four’s Saturday-night subtitled slot: from French *Spiral* in 2006 (**62,000** avg) and Swedish *Wallander* in 2009 (**295,000**, +31% above slot avg) and 2010 (**418,000**, +66%), to Danish *Forbrydelsen* Season 1 in early 2011 (**534,000**, +65%), **Season 2 (1,102,000, +240% above slot avg)**, and Season 3 (**1,095,000**, +136%); *Borgen* Season 1 (**744,000**, +60%); *Bron/Broen* Season 1 (**1,112,000**, +139%); and ***Bron/Broen* Season 3, reaching a single-episode peak of 1.78 million viewers—a 7.6% market share for a niche channel whose all-day average share was 1.1%** (Aarhus University, 2014, noted a cumulative cult reach of >4M viewers for *Forbrydelsen*, plus **~300,000 UK DVD box sets** sold by Arrow Films; Esser, 2017).
  * *Socio-Demographic Profile of UK Subtitled Drama Viewers (Esser, 2017):* 2012 BARB data showed UK viewers were overwhelmingly upper-middle/middle class (ABC1) and university-educated: *Borgen* was **79% ABC1**, **55% higher-educated**, and **52% female**; *Forbrydelsen* was **70% ABC1**; and *Bron/Broen* was **71% ABC1**. In a multiple-choice platform question (400+ respondents), **331** selected "TV channels," **225** "catch-up," **172** "DVD," and **147** "standalone subscription services," while **<50** used online streaming or P2P sharing.

#### 1. Japanese TV Anime: Production Committees, Loss-Leader Late-Night Economics, and 90% Offshore Labor
* **The Production Committee (*Seisaku Iinkai*) and Studio Exclusion:** Regarded as unique to Japan (Okeda & Koike, 2011; Kawashima, 2018), the production committee is a temporary joint-venture consortium pooling capital and dividing IP rights per title. Originating in theatrical features (*Nausicaä* and the eight-company committee for *Akira*), it was first applied to TV on *The Irresponsible Captain Tylor* (1992) and generalized after *Neon Genesis Evangelion* ("Project Eva") in 1995 (Steinberg, 2012, 2013; Sakuga Blog, 2017). Daliot-Bul (2014), Suan (2018, section 2.1), and industry analyses emphasize **the commissioned animation studio is usually excluded from the production committee** (even when the studio created the original IP, as with Manglobe on *Samurai Flamenco*); only a few major studios (Toei, TMS, Pierrot, Sunrise, Production I.G, Kyoto Animation) regularly sit on or lead committees. Thus, even when overseas streaming licenses recoup production costs upfront, downstream profits flow to committee investors rather than contracted studios or animators. Because manga/light-novel magazines act as low-cost R&D labs—with **~60% of anime series based on pre-existing popular characters** (Condry & Steinberg, 2013) and **up to 90% adapted from manga** under a broader definition (MacWilliams, 2008, in Tomos, 2013)—broadcast entry barriers are much lower in Japan than in Hollywood or HBO (Steinberg, 2013).
* **Loss-Leader Broadcasts, *Shin'ya Anime* Ratings, and Merchandise Recoupment:** In 1963, Tezuka sold *Tetsuwan Atomu* to TV and advertisers for **500,000 yen/episode**—far below cost, acting as *"a loss-leader for a whole new industry"* (McCarthy)—recouping deficits via Meiji Seika chocolate stickers and toys (Steinberg, 2012; Hartzheim, 2015). Hartzheim (2015) shows this system persists: TV Tokyo producer Fukashi Azuma (Sept 2011 interview, in Hartzheim, 2015) stated *"program ratings have become less important than the sales of various goods related to the program."* Daytime children's series like *Pretty Cure* act as *"thirty-minute toy commercials,"* while the 2006 live-action drama and 2007 TV anime of *Nodame Cantabile* generated a net reprint boost of **600,000 copies** of the original manga, proving TV anime operates *"like a megaphone broadcasting on a weekly schedule into Japanese homes"* (Hartzheim, 2015). For teen/adult *otaku*, **late-night anime (*shin'ya anime*, 22:00–04:00 JST)** was pioneered by TV Tokyo’s *Those Who Hunt Elves* (1996, growing out of late-night voice-actor radio, *aniraji*), expanded in 1997 after *Evangelion*, and spread to satellite broadcaster WOWOW and independent UHF stations from 1998 (Condry, 2013, Ch. 5 on Studio Gonzo's 2006 *Red Garden*; Wikipedia *"Late-night anime"*). Because total households viewing at midnight is ~33% (~12% at 2:00 a.m.), around 2009 a late-night rating of **2.0% was "passing" and 4.0% was "high"**—viable only because costs are recouped by selling **two-episode domestic DVDs/Blu-rays for upwards of $60 in Japan** (Condry, 2013).
* **Studio Geography, 90% Offshore Outsourcing, and Animator Wage Precarity:** Citing Dentsu data, Condry (2013, Ch. 1) documents ~700 "anime-related" firms—**400 (57.1%)** in direct studio production and **300 (42.9%)** auxiliary firms—while 2011 government statistics (in Tomos, 2013) record **430 animation production companies, 61.4% concentrated in Tokyo's 23 wards** (especially along the Chūō line west from Shinjuku, like Madhouse near Ogikubo Station, and the Seibu Ikebukuro line northwest; Condry, 2013). Studios employ 15 to a few hundred staff and face acute volatility (Madhouse and Gonzo both vacated corporate floors between 2006 and 2010). While scripts, 300-page storyboards (*ekonte*), character designs, and key frames (*genga*) are generally made in Japan, **~90% of in-between frames (*dōga*) are outsourced to lower-wage studios in South Korea, the Philippines, and China** (Condry, 2013; Tomos, 2013), alongside international episode direction (Suan, 2018)—prompting Madhouse producer Yūichirō Saitō to remark, *"We just want to make good animation; we are not so concerned about national origins."* Inside Japan, **9 out of 10 animators (90.0%) leave within three years** (10.0% retention; Hayashi, 2009 *WSJ* data in Condry, 2013), managed under scheduling pressure via the emotional labor of low-ranking production operators (*seisaku shinkō*; Morisawa, 2015). Reported animator earnings vary by cohort, year, and currency: Condry (2013, citing Hayashi, 2009) reports **$11,000/year** for animators in their twenties and **~$22,000/year** in their thirties; a 2018 labor study reports a 2013 industry average of **$28,000/year** and **1.1 million yen (~$10,000/year)** for new entrants; and Tomos (2013) records a 2009 figure of **£6,700** for animators/in-betweeners (referred to in one passage as annual and in another as monthly).

#### 2. Nordic Noir: Public Service Broadcasters, DR’s "15 Dogmas," Five External Financing Routes, and the *Lilyhammer* Streaming Lesson
* **PSB Dominance and DR’s "15 Dogmas":** Analyzing Eurodata TV / Mediamétrie data from the MeCETES project across 13 European countries, **2,645 TV-drama productions, and 6,093 distribution events** (2011–2014), Ib Bondebjerg (2016) proved **PSBs carry 70% of all European TV series broadcasts across Europe, vs. 30% on commercial channels**. In Denmark (pop. 5.4M–5.6M), DR1’s Sunday 20:00–21:00 drama slot regularly captures a **60% to 85% (peaking near 90%) national audience share** (**1.5M to 2.5M viewers** per episode, with nearly half the Danish population watching the *Forbrydelsen* Season 1 finale; Bondebjerg, 2016; Degn et al., 2015; Stougaard-Nielsen, 2016). As Eva Novrup Redvall documented (*Writing and Producing Television Drama in Denmark*, 2013, pp. 31, 69, 103, 130; Schröder, 2015; Dunleavy, 2016), after 1990s DR audience research showed negative reactions to domestic comedy pilots (Jensen & Jacobsen, 2017) and Lars von Trier’s *Riget* (1994–1997) sparked an aesthetic shift (Nielsen, 2012), DR producers visited the US set of *NYPD Blue* to study the showrunner model and codified DR’s **"Screen Idea System"** and **"15 Dogmas"** in **2003**: (1) **One Vision** (head writer authority permeating production, p. 103); (2) **Compact 3-Person Writers' Rooms** (on *Borgen*, p. 130); (3) **Double Storytelling** (weaving ethical/social/political themes into genre plots, p. 210); (4) **Crossover** (rotating film talent between cinema and TV with *Den Danske Filmskole*); (5) **The "Production Hotel"** (co-locating creative, technical, and administrative staff); and (6) **Producer's Choice** (handpicking freelancers). Unlike Tokyo anime studios—where directors like Mamoru Hosoda avoided pre-imagining audience reactions and rarely used surveys (Condry, 2013)—DR’s Media Research Department systematically tested dramas (mid-1990s to 2016) using **focus groups, surveys, and biometric skin-conductance arousal measurements** (Redvall, 2017). DR's model was exported to Norway’s NRK and emulated in Australia (Hansen et al., 2018).
* **Five External Financing Routes and Three Co-Production Types:** Because small domestic markets cannot fund high-end drama via license fees alone (Raats et al., 2016), Jensen, Nielsen, and Waade (2015/2016) identified **five external funding routes** from interviews with DR Drama, DR Sales, ZDF Enterprises, Nimbus Film, and Miso Film: (1) co-funding with foreign broadcasters/distributors; (2) finished-program sales; (3) presales; (4) format/remake sales; and (5) international/regional funds (Nordvision, Nordisk Film & TV Fund, Sweden's Film i Skåne and Ystad-Österlen funds, Copenhagen Film Fund; Hansen et al., 2018, detail *Bron/Broen* Season 4's budget). Bondebjerg (2016) classifies European co-productions into **three models**: (1) *Financial/Technical Co-Productions with Single National Creative Control:* *Forbrydelsen* (co-funded by DR, SVT, NRK, ZDF, and Nordisk Film & TV Fund, creatively controlled by DR) and Swedish *Wallander* (co-funded by German ARD Degeto under Swedish creative control); (2) *Bilateral Creative Co-Productions:* *Bron/Broen*, produced by **Filmlance** (Sweden) and **Nimbus Film** (Denmark) for SVT and DR (Waade & Jensen, 2013, pp. 195–198; classified by Hjort & Agger, 2016, p. 148, as a "natural co-production" prompted by shared border geography); and (3) *Multilateral Creative Co-Productions:* EBU-promoted *The Team* (2015), combining a Danish directing team with a Danish, German, and Flemish cast, criticized as an artificial pan-European "Euro-pudding" (Bondebjerg, 2016).
* **Two Structural Crossovers — Inverse Co-Production Motives (1979–1985 vs. 2000s) and the *Lilyhammer* Streaming Convergence:**
  1. *Buying Cheap Capacity vs. Buying External Capital:* Both industries engaged in European co-productions from opposite ends of the value chain. Santiago Iglesias (2018) documented the **1979–1985 Euro-Japanese animation co-production wave**—West Germany’s ZDF and Nippon Animation on *Bannertail* (1979), Italy’s RAI and Tokyo Movie Shinsha on *Sherlock Hound* (1981, 6 episodes directed by Miyazaki), and Spain’s TVE/BRB International with Japanese studios on *Ruy the Little Cid* (1980), *Dogtanian and the Three Musketeers* (1981), *Around the World with Willy Fog* (1983), and with Taiwanese studios on *David the Gnome* (1985). This wave was **initiated by European broadcasters to buy cheap, rapid Japanese animation labor**: BRB sales director Maria Aragon explained in *Variety* (1984, p. 206) that character creation, scripts, storyboards, music, and post-production were done in Spain, *"but the actual animation work is handled in Japan... over there we can finish a whole series in six months, whereas in Spain it might take four years."* By the late 1980s, European TV privatization made buying finished Japanese anime cheaper than co-producing, ending the wave. Conversely, Nordic Noir co-productions were **initiated by Nordic producers to attract foreign capital** to offset high domestic labor/production costs.
  2. *Loss of Control Under Global Streaming Capital (Sundet, 2017):* From 12 interviews with 13 informants at Norway’s NRK and Rubicon TV, Vilde Schanke Sundet (2017) showed how partnering with Netflix pushed Nordic public broadcasting toward the structural plight of Japanese anime studios. Originating in 2009 under NRK External (meeting a 10% independent commissioning quota), *Lilyhammer* brought Netflix on board mid-Season 1, nearly quadrupling the budget across three seasons—from **33M NOK (Season 1) to 62M NOK (Season 2) and 120M NOK (Season 3)**. By Season 3, Netflix invested more than twice NRK's share and **captured global copyright**, compressing NRK’s exclusive domestic window to 6 months. Sundet documented the three-fold *"cost of transnational 'success'"*: (a) *Compliance and Scheduling Costs:* Because Rubicon TV violated Norwegian PSB rules by including product placement in Season 1, NRK had to delay the premiere and demote the series from its Sunday prime-time "maximum possible window" (>30% share) to late Wednesday; (b) *Legal and Distribution Conflict:* When NRK tried to release all Season 3 episodes online at once, Netflix threatened legal action, prompting an NRK executive to remark, *"We have gone from a world where NRK didn't know what Netflix was, to a world where Netflix is NRK's number one enemy"*; and (c) *Creative Friction:* NRK wanted to satirize the Norwegian Labour and Welfare Administration (NAV) and local stereotypes, whereas star Steven Van Zandt and Netflix pushed for American-style violence and sex. Here, the Nordic PSB model converged with the Japanese Production Committee model: **top-tier capital controlling global distribution (Production Committee / Netflix) captured global IP rights while squeezing the autonomy and windows of the producing entity.**

### 3.4 Narrative Content and Audiovisual Characteristics: Open-Ended Manga Serials and "From Cool to Kawaii" Filtering vs. Finite Novelistic Seasons, "Double Storytelling," and Threefold Melancholy

#### 1. Japanese TV Anime: Manga Serialization, *Mukokuseki* Aesthetics, and Europe’s "From Cool to Kawaii" Gatekeeper Filter
Beyond *animetism*, *dynamic immobility*, and *mukokuseki* design (Section 2), Pellitteri (2018) showed TV anime's overseas narrative profile was reshaped by receiving-country gatekeepers. In 1980s continental Europe, moral panic among parents, educators, and politicians over perceived "violence and foreignness" in Japanese robot, sci-fi, and adventure anime forced European TV networks to curtail action genres and buy female-led magical-girl (*majokko*), romance, and *World Masterpiece Theater* literary adaptations. Pellitteri terms this shift **"from cool to kawaii"**, documenting three consequences by the late 1990s–early 2000s: (1) female-oriented titles dominated European TV anime broadcasts; (2) European theatrical releases shrank almost exclusively to Studio Ghibli films while sci-fi/action features vanished from cinemas; and (3) a massive European female manga readership emerged alongside female theme-song vocalists, resulting in a broad "feminization" of anime's European reputation.

#### 2. Nordic Noir: "Double Storytelling," Sven Clausen’s "Theory of Interdependence," Strong Female Detectives, and Threefold Melancholy
* **Mediatized Politics, "Double Storytelling," and the "Theory of Interdependence":** Ib Bondebjerg (2015) and Glen Creeber (2015) show how *Forbrydelsen* weaves three parallel storylines—criminal investigation (the murder of Nanna Birk Larsen), municipal political institutions, and private family grief—capped each episode by a cross-cut "ending montage." This embodies DR producer Sven Clausen’s **"theory of interdependence"** (in Redvall, 2013b, p. 166—*"no man is an island and that every individual action will inevitably influence the whole community"*) and head writer Søren Sveistrup’s "two-dimensional storytelling" structured around mathematician Edward Lorenz’s (2001) **"butterfly effect."** Against Margaret Thatcher’s (1987) neoliberal dictum that *"there is no such thing as society,"* Nordic Noir uses murder investigations to diagnose "dark contradictions of the welfare state" (Nestingen, 2008/2011; Brodén, 2011; Bergman, 2011, p. 35) while proving society exists as intimately connected individuals (Creeber, 2015). In *Bron/Broen* Season 1, the "Truth Terrorist" stages crimes exposing **five welfare-state failures**—(1) inequality before the law, (2) homelessness, (3) disregard for the mentally ill, (4) failure to integrate second-generation immigrants, and (5) Western hypocrisy regarding child labor—before the finale reveals Danish detective Martin Rohde’s own past affair inadvertently triggered the killer's vendetta (Creeber, 2015; García Avis, 2015).
* **The "Missing/Dead Girl" Motif, Equal-Rank Twin Detectives, and Quantitative Proof of Female Protagonist Appeal:** Revolving around a missing or murdered young woman (Hansen et al., 2018), Nordic Noir pairs emotionally dysfunctional male observers (Kurt Wallander, Martin Beck, Harry Hole) with **unconventional, powerful female detectives** (McCabe, 2015; Jermyn, 2016) in an equal-rank, diametrically opposed twin-detective dynamic inspired by *The X-Files* rather than hierarchical Holmes-and-Watson pairings (Creeber, 2015). In *Forbrydelsen*, methodical, uncommunicative Sarah Lund (Sofie Gråbøl)—wearing her Faroese jumper, never smiling, failing as a mother and fiancée, and given almost no backstory (unlike the US remake, criticized for over-explaining her; Povlsen, 2011, p. 97; Agger, 2013)—is paired with macho, impulsive Jan Meyer (Søren Malling). In *Bron/Broen*, after a bisected corpse is placed precisely on the Øresund Bridge border, logical, socially awkward Swedish detective Saga Norén (Sofia Helin, displaying Asperger’s traits, driving a spotless sports car, living in a cold city apartment) is paired with warm, diplomatic Danish detective Martin Rohde (Kim Bodnia, driving a messy car, living in a rustic family home), turning bi-national policing into an allegory that *"bridges have to be built"* (Creeber, 2015; García Avis, 2015; Alexi, 2017). Crucially, a **quantitative content analysis of 32 Danish TV dramas (2005–2014) by Helles and Lai** (in Jensen & Jacobsen, 2017) proved **strong female leads correlated significantly and positively with international export success**, whereas low production values and simple narrative structures correlated with export failure.
* **Threefold "Nordic Melancholy," Anti-Televisuality, and "Guilty Landscapes":** Drawing on Karin Johannisson (2009) and Kierkegaard’s (1843) melancholic police observer, Anne Marit Waade (2017) systematized Nordic Noir's aesthetic into **three levels of melancholy**: (1) *atmospheric mood*, (2) *character emotion* (repressed longing/loneliness), and (3) *societal or existential diagnosis* (rooted in Munch, Hammershøi, Sibelius, Ibsen, Strindberg, and Ingmar Bergman). Rejecting John Caldwell’s (*Televisuality*, 1995, pp. 3–11) hyperactive broadcast editing, Nordic Noir practices "Slow TV" (Holdsworth, 2006; Peacock, 2011): *Forbrydelsen* Episode 1 takes over 21 minutes to establish its three plotlines, grouping two to four consecutive scenes with Sarah Lund to create what Sveistrup called *"visually more epic. Larger pictures. Spaghetti Western stuff"* (Kingsley, 2012, p. 151; Creeber, 2015) across a dim, rainy Copenhagen inspired by Danish poet Henrik Nordbrandt’s line *"November, November, November…"* Conversely, Swedish *Wallander* juxtaposes sunny Scanian pastoral landscapes with brutal crime to create what Martin Lefebvre (2006) and Stijn Reijnders (2009/2011) call **"guilty landscapes"** (whereas the BBC English remake touristified Ystad's summer beauty; Forshaw, 2012, p. 6), while *Bron/Broen* uses homogeneous grey-green-brown lighting across Marc Augé’s centrifugal "non-places" (Hernández Pérez, 2014; Alexi, 2017). This aesthetic reshaped international TV, inspiring UK *Broadchurch* (2013–), Welsh *Y Gwyll / Hinterland* (2013–, bought by DR for Danish broadcast), Scottish *Shetland* (2013–), *The Fall* (2013–, opening with 3 minutes 50 seconds of silence; Steenberg, 2016), *The Missing* (2014), French *Disparue* (2015), Czech *Pustina* (2016), German *Dark* (2017), and HBO’s *True Detective* (2014–; Creeber, 2015).

#### 3. Deep Commonalities and Structural Divergence: Transculturated American Genealogies and "Form vs. Place"
* **Hidden Shared Genealogy (Transculturating American Popular Culture):** Both are products of **transculturation**—defined by Dimitris Eleftheriotis (*Popular Cinemas of Europe*, 2001, pp. 101, 108, in Hedling, 2014) as marginal groups selecting and inventing from materials transmitted by a metropolitan culture. Hedling (2014) showed Sjöwall and Wahlöö were Swedish translators of Ed McBain’s *87th Precinct* novels who politicized McBain’s procedural template for Swedish welfare society (as Italian Spaghetti Westerns adapted US Westerns). In parallel, Davis (2016) showed Japanese anime emerged from a transpacific "ping-pong" exchange: Tezuka borrowed from Disney and Hanna-Barbera; magical-girl anime adapted *Bewitched* and *I Dream of Jeannie*; 1967’s *Mach GoGoGo* (*Speed Racer*) blended Elvis Presley’s *Viva Las Vegas* (1964) and James Bond’s *Goldfinger* (1964); and *Battle Angel Alita* creator Yukito Kishiro declared, *"For me, Mad Max was more important than Blade Runner."*
* **Form vs. Place Mapping onto Section 2:** Japanese TV anime’s distinctiveness is deposited in a **deterritorialized formal grammar and character-database economy** (matching **Path B: Media Form** in Section 2), whereas Nordic Noir’s distinctiveness is bound to **specific Nordic geography and welfare-state social critique** (matching **Path A: Representational Text** in Section 2).

### 3.5 Common Factors Behind Their Globalization Success—and Internal Weighting Differences

Despite divergent institutional models, Japanese TV anime and Nordic Noir succeeded transnationally before 2018 via **six shared structural mechanisms**:

1. **"Peripheral Contra-Flows" and the Refutation of Traditional Media Trade Theories:**
   Both represent "contra-flows" from the global audiovisual periphery to the Anglo-American core (Stafford, 2014; Jensen, 2016). Esser (2017) established the baseline Danish drama overcame in Britain: in the mid-2000s, **~75% of all imported programming across the UK's eight main TV channels came from the US, while imports from all of continental Europe combined averaged <1%** (2% on BBC Two). Both refuted Joseph Straubhaar’s (2007) "cultural proximity" hypothesis, operating instead via Antonio La Pastina and Straubhaar’s (2005, p. 275) "multiple proximities" (genre and subcultural affinity across distance; Ruh, 2012; Tongdhamachart, 2015; Waade, 2014). Even in East Asia, Otmazgin (2008) proved natural cultural proximity could not explain Japanese anime's dominance—where Japanese titles accounted for **80%–90%** of stock across 23 Hong Kong anime/manga shops in 2004, **>50%** of the 474 hours of foreign animation on South Korea's three main terrestrial networks in 2004, **93.5%** on top Korean cable channel Tooniverse, and **3.5 hours/week** on Thai Channel 9 since 1981 (plus illicit street piracy in South Korea before President Kim Dae-jung's 1998 cultural opening, when Japanese music still held ~10% of the Korean market; METI, 2002)—proposing a **"regional paradigm"** driven by urban middle-class consumer spaces linked by Pony Canyon, Avex, Fuji TV, and JET TV. Similarly, Iwabuchi (2001, 2002) showed that in Taiwan—Japan's largest, most receptive market—cultural proximity was actively reconstructed under shared modern temporality and postcolonial "yearning for Asia."
2. **Secondary Gatekeepers, Institutional/Scheduling Niches, and Western Distribution Infrastructure ("The Three-Leaf Clover"):**
   Neither broke through via mainstream US/UK prime-time network slots; both exploited secondary windows and Jensen and Jacobsen’s (2017, pp. 2, 7–9) **"three-leaf clover" model** aligning (1) *market gatekeepers/buyers*, (2) *cultural intermediaries* (critics, journalists, fansubbers), and (3) *audiences*:
   * *Japanese TV Anime ("Americanization of Japanization" & Layered Branding):* Anime entered the West via 1980s US cable time-slot gaps, 1990s adult VHS label Manga Video, Cartoon Network's late-night Toonami block (Complex, 2017), and fansubs. Yet for mass commercial scale, Iwabuchi (2002) emphasized Japan's *"most serious weakness is the lack of international distribution channels"*—forcing *Ghost in the Shell* to rely on PolyGram's Manga Entertainment, Studio Ghibli on its 1995 global pact with Disney, and *Pokémon* on Warner Bros. and Nintendo of America, a dependence Iwabuchi termed the **"Americanization of Japanization."** In theatrical marketing, Denison (2007, 2008a, 2008b, 2015, pp. 120, 127–131) and Carter (2018) showed Ghibli producer Toshio Suzuki’s domestic *daihitto* ("big hit") strategy merged with Disney into a **"layered brand"** combining mainstream "deliberate Disneyfication" (star voice dubbing; Ogihara-Schuck, 2014, p. 88) with "transnational auteur" prestige.
   * *Nordic Noir (BBC Austerity, the 2010 Service Licence Rewrite, and Arrow Films):* Esser (2017) uncovered the institutional conditions behind BBC Four's Saturday 9:00 p.m. subtitled slot: facing an overall 25% BBC budget cut, BBC Four had its £50M budget cut by 10%, and **its revised 2010 Channel Service Licence explicitly stipulated that BBC Four *"should no longer broadcast original UK drama... [but] will continue to showcase the best foreign-language drama series and films from around the world."*** Channel Controller Richard Klein acquired Danish series at very low cost and packaged them as **"upmarket soap for people who want a glass of red wine,"** filling a Saturday-night vacuum left by mainstream channels avoiding ITV's *The X Factor* and BBC One's *Strictly Come Dancing*—during a "lucky time window" when UK binge-watching was rising **just before Netflix entered the UK** (Esser, 2017). Simultaneously, independent distributor **Arrow Films** invested early in UK DVD rights and "Nordicana" festivals, while crime genre conventions acted as a familiar cross-cultural **"known contract"** (like *Inspector Morse*; Bondebjerg & Redvall, 2015).
3. **Seriality, Cross-Media/Format Economies, and European Reverse Imitation:**
   Both leveraged seriality for ancillary revenue: anime used open-ended serials to drive global toy, card, and game ecosystems (inspiring European transmedia imitations modeled on anime, such as Italy’s *W.i.t.c.h.*, selling **>16 million magazine copies**, and *Winx Club*, sold to **130 countries** with hundreds of millions of euros in merchandise turnover; Pellitteri, 2018), whereas Nordic Noir used 10-episode novelistic arcs (prepared by translated crime fiction; Stougaard-Nielsen, 2017) to drive "must-see" viewing, ~300,000 UK DVD box sets, Frans Bak soundtrack CDs, and lucrative English remake rights.
4. **Mediated Taste Communities and Two-Tier Translation Dynamics (Stigmatized Subculture vs. Elite Bourdieusian Distinction):**
   Both relied on passionate taste communities and evolved a **two-tier translation structure** (mass-market dubs/remakes vs. core subtitled appreciation, e.g., simultaneous dubbed mass releases and subtitled *otaku* screenings of *Spirited Away* in France; Denison, 2007), while engaging in producer–audience "push–pull" interactions (Annette Hill, 2016). Yet their **social class positions were inverted**: early Western anime fans and Harajuku *kawaii* dressers suffered social **stigmatization** as "weird Others" (analyzed via Erving Goffman’s stigma theory by Bouissou, 2008, and Pellitteri, 2010, 2018), whereas Nordic Noir's UK audience (70%–79% ABC1, 74.9% aged 45+, higher-educated) spread recommendations via **interpersonal word-of-mouth** among family, friends, and colleagues (Esser, 2017), turning subtitled viewing into Bourdieusian class **"distinction"** and *"the pleasure of watching foreigners speak a foreign language"* (Stougaard-Nielsen, 2016). Even so, empirical reception limits remained sharp: Jacobsen (2018) and Jensen and Jacobsen (2017, pp. 13–15) showed from fieldwork in **Japan** (where Super!Drama aired *Forbrydelsen* in 2012) and **Brazil** that Nordic Noir's reception outside proximate Western markets was "lukewarm" and fragile—generating almost zero Japanese press coverage, tiny audiences, and no Sarah Lund sweater sales (though Brazilian political activists appropriated Danish plots on blogs to critique local corruption)—while in the US, the 1998–2008 TV/DVD anime boom contracted and normalized after 2008 (Daliot-Bul, 2014; Daliot-Bul & Otmazgin, 2017).
5. **Screen Geography to Offline Tourism and Intergenerational Consumer Loops:**
   Both converted screen viewing into physical tourism: Nordic Noir drove location tourism to Ystad (Wallander trails), Stockholm (*Millennium* tours), and the Øresund region (Reijnders, 2009/2011; Waade, 2013; Askanius, 2017), while anime drove *seichi junrei* contents tourism (Yamamura, 2014; Okamoto, 2015; Type 4, inbound foreign tourism, in Seaton & Yamamura’s 2015 six-part typology). Uniquely, anime established an **intergenerational consumer loop** in Europe: Pellitteri (2018) documented that first-generation "Italian *otaku*" women who grew up watching 1980s–1990s anime (*Cutie Honey*, *Creamy Mami*, *Sailor Moon*, Hello Kitty) dressed their own daughters in these characters and sustained *kawaii* fashion brands (Tokidoki), *gashapon* capsule toys, and *Gyaru* / *Gothic Lolita* subcultures across generations.
6. **Global SVoD Platform Incorporation:**
   In the 2010s, global streaming platforms absorbed both as subscriber-acquisition assets: Crunchyroll's legalization (2009) and Netflix's 2017 slate of 12 original anime series on one side, and Netflix's co-production/acquisition of *Lilyhammer* (2012), German *Dark* (2017), and Danish *The Rain* (2018) on the other.

### 3.6 How "Cultural Uniqueness" Is Constructed and Negotiated in Transnational Dissemination

In both Japanese TV anime and Nordic Noir, **what foreign audiences consume as "cultural uniqueness" is co-constructed across three levels—the text/form, production/policy institutions, and the receiving culture's marketing and discourse** (Eichner & Waade, 2015). Crucially, Iwabuchi (2002) and Nordic TV scholars (Jensen & Waade, 2013; Hedling, 2014; Esser, 2017) independently leveled **the exact same two-part critique against Hoskins and Mirus’s (1988, p. 500) classical theory of "cultural discount"**: (1) cultural foreignness and "strangeness" (Davis, 2016) can become an added value or **"cultural mark-up"** rather than a liability; and (2) cultural discount theory is overly Western-centric and ignores the split between mass markets and transnational niche markets. Yet from this shared starting point, the two forms followed **mirror-opposite historical trajectories**:

* **Pathway 1 — Japanese TV Anime (Absence / Odorlessness $\rightarrow$ Re-Nationalization):** Stage 1: "Three Odorless Cs" & *mukokuseki* design (Iwabuchi, 2002) + *Nihonjinbanare* production politics (Lu, 2008) $\rightarrow$ Stage 2: Localization "Balancing Act" (1963 *Astro Boy* & 1998–2017 *Pokémon* 4Kids edits erasing Japanese text while keeping exotic visuals, except in Asia/Italy; Raes, 2018) $\rightarrow$ Stage 3: Post-2002 state "Cool Japan" re-nationalization & Ghibli glocalization vs. deterritorialized "animesque" media-form brand (Suan, 2017).
* **Pathway 2 — Nordic Noir TV Drama (Place Mark-Up $\rightarrow$ Brand Inflation/Stripping):** Stage 1: Danish climate/language marketed "from within" + UK Arrow Films ("Nordicana") & BBC Four branding "from without" inverting cultural discount for ABC1 elites (Waade, 2013/2014) $\rightarrow$ Stage 2: UK austerity nostalgia for welfare state ("accessible difference"), *hygge*/design, and safe "consuming whiteness" compared by *The New Yorker* (2013) to 19th-century French *Japonisme* (Stougaard-Nielsen, 2016) $\rightarrow$ Stage 3: Mass-market cultural discount (10:1 / 8:1 ratios) solved via de-Nordified remakes (*The Bridge*, *The Tunnel*), while Celtic/European Noir imitations inflate the label until it is "no longer unique" (Esser, 2017; Hansen & Waade, 2017).

#### 1. Japanese TV Anime: From "Cultural Odorlessness" (*Mukokuseki*) and Localization "Balancing Acts" to Top-Down "Cool Japan" Re-Nationalization
1. **Stage 1 — The "Three Odorless Cs" and Production-Side *Nihonjinbanare*:**
   Iwabuchi (*Recentering Globalization*, 2002, pp. 33–35, 71) argued Japan's successful global exports consisted of the **"culturally odorless three Cs"**—*Consumer technologies* (Walkman, VCRs, karaoke), *Comics and cartoons (anime)*, and *Computer/video games*—lacking **"cultural odor"** (features evoking the country of origin's contemporary way of life). Critic Eiji Ōtsuka (1993) warned against celebrating anime's global popularity because it rested on a *"stateless, odorless nature,"* while Toshiya Ueno (1996) noted *"the 'Japaneseness' of Japanimation is recognized only when it actively becomes a stateless visual culture."* Furthermore, Amy Shirong Lu (2008) showed **de-Japanization (*Nihonjinbanare***, as in *Record of the Lodoss War*, *The Wizard of Oz*, or *Anne of Green Gables*) was practiced by Japanese creators at production through **three cultural politics of internationalization**: (a) *depoliticized internationalization* (*Yu-Gi-Oh!* blurring character race for global markets); (b) *Westernized internationalization* (late-1970s to mid-1990s martial-arts anime like *Fist of the North Star* and *Saint Seiya* projecting Japanese nationalism by having Japanese heroes defeat arrogant blond Western villains); and (c) *self-Orientalized internationalization* (*R.O.D -Read or Die-* depicting stereotypical China and postcolonial Hong Kong to position Japan as a "surrogate West" in Asia).
2. **Stage 2 — Western Localization Cleansing and the *Pokémon* "Balancing Act":**
   Erase-and-replace localization had deep US roots: after the 1954 US import of *Godzilla*, US distributors recut *tokusatsu* films, replacing Akira Ifukube’s scores with canned music so they sounded *"less Oriental"* and cutting Japanese actors out of *King Kong vs. Godzilla* (Ragone in Davis, 2016). In September 1963 on *Astro Boy*, Tezuka removed Japanese signage and US producer Fred Ladd renamed characters (Tobio to "Astor Boynton"), altered the Episode 1 moral lesson, excised violence/nudity, and visited Tokyo in October 1964 to compel Season 2 violence reductions (Ruh, 2012; Schodt, 2007, pp. 83–84); in 1984 on *Voltron*, US producer Peter Keefe fabricated English dialogue without a Japanese script (Davis, 2016). In the late 1990s, *Pokémon* producer Masakazu Kubo stated it was *"easy to produce international versions by erasing Japanese language signs as much as possible"* (Iwabuchi, 2004, p. 68), and US licensor 4Kids localized the series *"to hide its Japaneseness"* (*Time*, 1999)—with **every region outside Asia importing the US remade version of *Pokémon*** (Iwabuchi, 2002). Tracking 20 years of *Pokémon* localization, Raes (2018) showed 4Kids scrubbed Japanese text and rewrote rice balls (*onigiri*) as **"jelly donuts"** in Season 1 (1998), and by Season 20 (*Sun & Moon*, 2017) replaced Japanese markers with Hawaiian ones—yet practiced a **"balancing act"** retaining enough embedded Japanese visual aesthetics to sell the show as exotic (Katsuno & Maret, 2004). Similarly, in Disney's US release of Studio Ghibli’s *Spirited Away* (2001), untranslated Japanese ideograms (*Sen*, *me*) left inside the film were transcribed for Anglophone viewers into exoticized *"evidence of the film's Oriental origins"* (Denison, 2007, p. 318; Carter, 2018).
3. **Stage 3 — Regional Exceptions (Asia and Italy/France) and Top-Down "Cool Japan" Re-Nationalization:**
   Empirically, the "odorless" thesis was refuted in East Asia—where Otmazgin (2008) showed consumers always knew they were buying Japanese products (also Tsutsui, 2012; Daliot-Bul, 2009; Koma, 2013b)—and in Italy, where Pellitteri (2018) documented *"the Japanese origin of anime was never hidden; on the contrary, it was openly displayed as coming from Japan."* In his survey of France and Italy, Pellitteri (c. 2014–2015) found three reader generations divided by a generational gap (older readers driven by a childhood "nostalgia effect" from 1970s/1980s TV anime vs. younger readers practicing Bourdieusian "distinction") and national image differences: **"traditional and exotic Japan" dominated among Italian respondents (45.4% vs. 19.0% for pop culture), whereas "Japanese pop culture" dominated among French respondents (47.3% vs. 36.2% for exotic imagery)**—yet manga readers and non-readers held the *exact same* stereotype structure based on an "imagined/fictional Japan" (Rafoni, 2004; Sabre, 2006).
   From 2002 onward (following Douglas McGray’s *"Japan's Gross National Cool"*, *Spirited Away*'s Oscar win, and the state's "Intellectual Property Nation" declaration), the Japanese government launched the "Cool Japan" slogan (2003), appointed Doraemon "Anime Cultural Ambassador" (2008; Iwabuchi, 2018), established the Creative Industries Promotion Office (2010), published METI’s *Cool Japan Strategy* (2012, aiming to grow related industries from **2.3 trillion yen to 8–11 trillion yen** while branding Japan as the "sacred land" of anime; Seaton & Yamamura, 2015), and invested >$1.3B via the Cool Japan Fund (2012–2013; Norris, 2010; Report, 2011; Wentz, 2016). However, Iwabuchi (2002, Ch. 2; 2015, pp. 424–426), Choo (2012), Bouissou (2013, testing manga soft power across four European countries), Condry (2013), and Brienza (2016) proved that state "nationalization of cool" projects a narcissistic, self-Orientalizing image failing to convert pop-culture enjoyment into foreign political persuasion, while Suan (2017) and Santiago Iglesias (2018) showed what sells globally is the deterritorialized **"animesque" media-form**.

#### 2. Nordic Noir: From "Place-Saturated Cultural Mark-Up" and "Accessible Difference" to Mainstream "Cultural Discount," Remake De-Nordification, and Brand Inflation
1. **Stage 1 — Manufacturing "Cultural Mark-Up" From Within and Without:**
   In *"Nordic Noir challenging 'the language of advantage'"* (2013) and *What Makes Danish TV Series Travel?* (2014), Jensen and Waade showed that on the Danish production side, *"the exoticism of Danish settings, landscapes, light, climate, language and everyday life is used as a promotional tool in international marketing, thereby internationalizing Danish television drama production **'from within'**,"* while on the UK reception side, subtitled drama represented a **"cultural mark-up"** among British viewers and critics, *"internationalizing British television **'from without'**"* and adopting a **"meta travelling style"** (Waade, 2014). As Broomé (in Stougaard-Nielsen, 2016) observed, the genre *"is perhaps only truly 'Nordic' when read or watched from abroad,"* consolidated externally by **Arrow Films** (DVD box sets and "Nordicana" events; Esser, 2017), art-status "noir" labeling (Steenberg, 2016), regional European labels (English *Nordic Noir / Scando-noir*, French *le polar polaire*, German *Schwedenkrimi*; Hedling, 2014), and lifestyle bundling with New Nordic cuisine, modernist design, architecture, Sarah Lund's Faroese jumper, and *hygge* (Löfgren & Ehn, 2007, p. 10; Eichner & Waade, 2015; Waade, 2017).
2. **Stage 2 — Receiving-Culture Projections ("Accessible Difference," "Consuming Whiteness," and the *Japonisme* Analogy):**
   In *"Nordic noir in the UK: The allure of accessible difference"* (2016), Stougaard-Nielsen showed British critical/lifestyle discourse projected austerity-era middle-class **nostalgia for a pre-Thatcherite welfare state** onto Scandinavia as **"accessible difference."** Drawing on Ben Pitcher’s *Consuming Race* (2014), Stougaard-Nielsen argued that among BBC Four's middle-class demographic, fascination with Scandinavian drama also operated as a politically and racially safe form of **"consuming whiteness"**—satirized in the 2011 Christmas special of BBC's *Absolutely Fabulous* and by Black British comedian Lenny Henry. Strikingly, *The New Yorker* (Jan 7, 2013) directly compared Britain's infatuation with Danish TV and lifestyle to 19th-century French elite **"Japonisme"**—forging an explicit historical rhyme between Nordic Noir and Japanese visual culture.
3. **Stage 3 — Persistent Mainstream "Cultural Discount," Remake De-Nordification, and Brand Inflation:**
   Empirically, Hedling (2014) and Bondebjerg (2016) proved that while "cultural mark-up" operated within the educated BBC Four / university-town "art TV" niche (where subtitled Swedish *Wallander* ranked third behind two seasons of *Downton Abbey* at an independent Midwestern US university-town video store in Spring 2014, yet was **completely absent from the mainstream chain Family Video**, and where a corpus study in Bondebjerg, 2016, counted **14,072 UK press articles** vs. **192 Danish articles** on *Downton Abbey*), severe **"cultural discount"** governed the Anglophone mass market:

When mainstream networks bought format rights to overcome cultural discount, they engaged in what Louise Brix Jacobsen Gemzøe (2013, p. 283) and García Avis (2015, pp. 127–138) identified as **active "de-Nordification"**—*"a remake trend that, on the one hand, is inspired by all things Nordic, but on the other hand, actively engages in removing the Nordic feel from the remade productions"* (even as remakes stimulated comparative viewing across versions; Richard Berger, 2016). While AMC’s *The Killing* (2011–2014) preserved rainy greyness by relocating Copenhagen to Seattle, FX’s *The Bridge* (2013–2014, relocated from an initially considered US–Canada border to the militarized El Paso–Ciudad Juárez border because co-executive producer Meredith Stiehm noted *"there's not a lot of cultural or political conflict going on between Canada and the US"*; Sepinwall, 2013) replaced Nordic darkness with bright yellow desert heat (*"constant light is very nearly as oppressive and depressing as constant dark"*; Wollaston, 2014) and replaced the Scandinavian "five welfare-state truths" with cartel tunnels, Mexican police corruption, and Juárez femicides, while Sky/Canal+’s *The Tunnel* (2013–) replaced the open Øresund Bridge with the subterranean claustrophobia of the Eurotunnel and linked troubled youth to the 2011 London riots (García Avis, 2015). Meanwhile, within the prestige niche itself, proliferation of "Celtic Noir" (*Tartannoir*) and European imitations inflated the "Nordic Noir" label until it no longer required Nordic languages or creators (Hansen & Waade, 2017)—leading UK viewers surveyed by Esser (2017) to conclude that *"Danish TV drama, while currently still a guarantee of quality, is no longer (or no longer perceived as) unique."*

#### 3. Asymmetric Tourism Monetization: Place-Saturated Location Tourism vs. Fictional-Coordinate *Seichi Junrei*
Finally, this inverse construction of cultural uniqueness maps directly onto offline tourism monetization: because Nordic Noir is **place-saturated live-action realism**, its physical filming locations automatically convert into location tourism in Ystad or Stockholm; because Japanese TV anime is a **2D database of stylized, *mukokuseki* signs**, fans and municipal authorities must actively collaborate (as in Washimiya with *Lucky Star*; Yamamura, 2014) to anchor 2D fictional coordinates onto real-world maps to generate *seichi junrei* contents tourism.

**Comparative Synthesis and Polarity-Flip Conditions:**
Ultimately, Japanese TV anime and Nordic Noir form an exact mirror image in negotiating "cultural discount" and "cultural uniqueness": **Japanese TV anime neutralized mass-market cultural discount by altering the product itself** (early odorless dubbing, 4Kids localization cleansing, and *mukokuseki* character design) before being retroactively re-nationalized by subcultural fans, Ghibli glocalization, and state "Cool Japan" policy, while its formal grammar detached from Japan as the global "animesque" media-form. **Nordic Noir neutralized cultural discount by filtering the audience tier**—turning subtitles, Nordic climate, and welfare-state melancholy into an elite "cultural mark-up" for educated ABC1 audiences on BBC Four, while leaving mass-market cultural discount to be solved by de-Nordified English-language remakes, until its mise-en-scène grammar likewise detached from Scandinavia across global prestige TV. In both cases, the variables that flip cultural foreignness from a commercial discount into a cultural mark-up are the **social-class and subcultural positioning of the target audience (mass broadcast/multiplex vs. *otaku*/ABC1 "art TV" niche)** and the **historical-regional reception sphere (Anglophone West vs. continental Europe and East/Southeast Asia)**.
