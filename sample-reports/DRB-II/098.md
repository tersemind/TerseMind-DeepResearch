# Mary Lou Williams as Jazz Educator and Jazz Historian (c. 1970–1981)

Mary Lou Williams (May 8 or May 10, 1910 – May 28, 1981) dedicated her final decade to a systematic pedagogical, archival, and historiographical campaign—summarized by her watchword **"Save Jazz"**—aimed at rescuing an African American spiritual art form from twin threats: conservatory book-learning and unrooted avant-garde (free, atonal improvisation divorced from traditional harmony and rhythm) or commercial rock crossover. Turning her status as a living participant across every major jazz era into a second career, she articulated a unified theory of American music across inner-city Catholic classrooms, Saturday studio coaching in New York, Yale University's "Conservatory without Walls" (1972), touring three-to-five-day collegiate residencies (such as UW–Madison in 1976), faculty appointments at the University of Massachusetts Amherst (1975–1977) and Duke University (1977–1981), solo and duo lecture-recitals, typewritten polemical essays, the 1980 Mary Lou Williams Foundation for children aged 6 to 12, and her visual "Jazz Tree" diagram created with illustrator David Stone Martin. Because her 1970s pedagogy often restated positions articulated in earlier decades (her 1954 *Melody Maker* memoir serial, 1962–1964 *Jazz for the Soul* program sheet, and 1964/1966 interviews), those earlier statements are integrated below with explicit dates attached.

## 1. Educational Activities and Teaching Philosophy

### 1.1 Proto-Pedagogical Groundwork and Sequence of Teaching Venues

Williams brought decades of informal mentorship and institutional pioneering into the university classroom. She was the first African American woman to host a weekly radio show, run her own record label (Mary Records—also described per *Encyclopedia of World Biography* as the first record company founded by a woman) and publishing company (Cecilia Music), and join ASCAP ([Wisconsin Academy](https://www.wisconsinacademy.org/magazine/fall-2021/essay/looking-mary-lou-williams); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)).

During the 1940s, her Harlem apartment at 63 Hamilton Terrace served as *"a kind of salon"* and nightly after-hours school where younger modernists—Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell, Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Tadd Dameron, Kenny Dorham, Art Blakey, Sarah Vaughan, Miles Davis, Mel Tormé, and critic Leonard Feather—*"gathered there nightly"* to test harmonic and rhythmic ideas on her Hamilton (Baldwin) upright piano through dawn into the afternoon ([Folkways FJ 2860 liner notes](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf); [O'Neill, Dec 7, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html); [Fledderus, UNT](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf); Daphne Brooks, [NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)). She pioneered broadcast instruction in 1945 via her weekly WNEW radio program, *The Mary Lou Williams Piano Workshop*, premiering the twelve movements of her astrological *Zodiac Suite*—creating the later movements spontaneously at the keyboard on air, which she called ***"real jazz composing"*** ([MLWbio.html](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MLWbio.html))—before scoring it for chamber orchestra at Town Hall (December 31, 1945) and performing three movements with the New York Philharmonic at Carnegie Hall (June 22, 1946) ([Rutgers IJS-0060](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/resources/1215)). By 1955, she presented a chronological history of jazz piano in concerts and on her LP *A Keyboard History* ([Cunniffe, JazzHistoryOnline](https://jazzhistoryonline.com/mary-lou-williams/)).

Following a European stay (1952–1954), a sudden departure from the concert stage in Paris in 1954 when pianist Hazel Scott encouraged her to pray and read the Psalms ([The Duke Chronicle, Jan 28, 2025](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-mary-lou-williams-centennial-center-jazz-icon-musician-religious-inspiration-stylistic-jazz-variations-20250128)), Charlie Parker's 1955 death, and her May 1957 Roman Catholic baptism alongside Lorraine Gillespie under Fathers Anthony Woods and John Crowley, Williams founded the **Bel Canto Foundation** (1957–1963, Rutgers IJS Subseries 4.12) ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)). Chartered to assist musicians *"suffering from or exposed to alcohol or drugs,"* it operated a thrift shop, rummage sales, expense books, and aid cases. Vaughn A. Booker ([*Religions*, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)) contests older portraits (in Linda Dahl's and O'Brien's recollections) of the foundation as *"insufficient, amateurish folly,"* reframing it as *"daily religious labor"* and an *"institutionalization of personal accountability"* aimed at producing *"good sounds"* from rehabilitated musicians. Per Carsyn Shirk (2022, citing Rutgers IJS archives; [LSU Repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2338&context=honors_etd)), Williams experienced a prophetic vision of the **"death and corruption of jazz music,"** prompting her return with an explicitly pedagogical vocation. In the 1960s, *"because of the lack of musical knowledge she found in some younger players, Williams made it her mission to teach jazz history to New York City street kids,"* convinced that *"if the younger generation understood the past, they could add to the future"* ([Fledderus, UNT](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)).

Between 1966 and 1981, Williams conducted her educational work across seven major institutional and community arenas:

1. **Seton High School, Pittsburgh, and Inner-City School Assemblies (1966–1967 / early 1970s):**
   In 1966–1967, Bishop (later Cardinal) John J. Wright assigned and hired Williams to teach jazz history and music at Seton High School on Pittsburgh's north side—described in Father Peter O'Brien's *Mary Lou's Mass* liner notes ([Smithsonian Folkways SFW 40815](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40815.pdf); [Mary Blengino](https://maryblengino.it/en/mary-lou-williams-jazz-and-sacred-music-the-final-years/)) and Tammy L. Kernodle's *Soul on Soul* ([Dokumen.pub](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)) as an all-white Catholic high school, and by Deanna Witkowski ([DeannaWitkowski.com](https://www.deannawitkowski.com/blog/the-city-where-jazz-is-love-pittsburgh-and-mary-lou-williams)) and [UrbanFaith](https://urbanfaith.com/mary-lous-sacred-jazz/) as an all-girls Catholic school, where she also worked with Fr. Michael Williams of the Catholic Youth Organization. Kernodle notes she took the Seton job *"but never received her promised salary,"* while petitioning Wright for Catholic-school jazz funding: *"Jazz! We're on the verge of losing a great spiritual music"* (Gayle Murchison, 2002, citing D. Antoinette Handy and Dahl; [Bard PDF](https://tools.bard.edu/wwwmedia/fisher-files/Murchison%20Mary%20Lou%20Williams.pdf)). Facing conventional students and skeptical nuns, she bypassed textbooks by composing her first liturgical work, *The Pittsburgh Mass* (progenitor of *Mary Lou's Mass*), directly in class over one week. Per Fledderus (citing Dahl), she wrote eight bars at a time while giving students a break, taught them to sing the freshly inked phrases by ear, and shifted abruptly to conventional music theory whenever nuns entered. On July 24, 1967, she *"led thirteen girls from Seton in singing her first Mass in 1967 at the mammoth Saint Paul Cathedral in Oakland"* (Pittsburgh) ([Witkowski](https://www.deannawitkowski.com/blog/the-city-where-jazz-is-love-pittsburgh-and-mary-lou-williams)). She later extended assembly outreach to Harlem street youth and grade/high schools nationwide ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)).

2. **Saturday Private Coaching in New York, The Cookery, and Correspondence Mentorship (1970s):**
   In 1970, Williams booked herself at Barney Josephson's Greenwich Village restaurant The Cookery so young audiences would rediscover acoustic jazz—noting *"kids coming from all over New York… were going home and telling their parents that they'd discovered a new kind of music"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)). Throughout the 1970s, she coached privately on Saturdays in her New York apartment (and later her Durham home) for nominal fees or gratis. In her early 1977 hotel-room interview with critic Len Lyons during her ten-day Keystone Korner engagement in San Francisco (in *The Great Jazz Pianists*, 1983; [CerraJazz Substack](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)) and a separate between-sets Keystone Korner interview (headed 1977, page-titled 1978; [MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html) / [ItsAllGreat.html](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/ItsAllGreat.html)), she explained: *"I teach Saturdays in New York… I helped Hilton Ruiz, who is with Rahsaan [Roland Kirk], by taking him back to Fats Waller for four months. I had him swinging the left hand. He can play anything."* In March–April 1979, when Berklee-trained pianist Dave Ratcliffe mailed her handwritten transcriptions of her solos on *"Rosa Mae"* and *"Dirge Blues"*, she invited him to register at Duke and study privately for **$20.00 per hour** instead of her regular **$35.00** rate ([StudyingWithMLW.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)).

3. **Yale University's "Conservatory without Walls" / Duke Ellington Fellowship Program (October 1972):**
   On October 7, 1972, in Yale's Woolsey Hall, Yale President Kingman Brewster presented Duke Ellington with an honorary medallion, and Yale professor Willie Ruff—founding director of the Duke Ellington Fellowship Program—awarded the Duke Ellington Fellows medal to Ellington and fifty African American musical pioneers, including Williams, Eubie Blake, Willie "The Lion" Smith, Jo Jones, Max Roach, Charles Mingus, Dizzy Gillespie, and Ruff ([Yale University Library, Oct 11, 2022](https://library.yale.edu/news/library-celebrates-willie-ruff-53-and-conservatory-without-walls); [*8495Jazz*](https://8495jazz.substack.com/p/the-circle-is-unbroken)). The concert opened with Gillespie, Williams, Slam Stewart, and Kenny Clarke playing *"with the easy command of the masters"* (*New York Times*, Oct 9, 1972); the Yale Film Archive preserves two episodes of the 1972 TV program *"What's Happening"* with concert footage and Fellow interviews. Conceived by Ruff as a **"Conservatory without Walls"** for direct oral transmission, the program brought musicians to mentor and perform at Yale and across New Haven. By 2002, Ellington Fellows as a whole had worked with an estimated 180,000 New Haven public school students (a program-level statistic reported by *Yale News* in 2002; Yale Library records do not separately itemize Williams's individual classroom visits).

4. **Touring Three-to-Five-Day Collegiate Residencies, UW–Madison (1976), Philadelphia (1978), and Honorary Doctorates (1970s–1980):**
   Supported by two John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation Fellowships in Music Composition—awarded in **1972** at age 62 (reporting in 1973 she was busy *"trying to bring back an important art called 'jazz.' It's happening"*) and **1977** ([Guggenheim Foundation](https://www.gf.org/stories/the-first-lady-of-the-jazz-keyboard); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams); another source lists 1972–73)—plus grants from the NEA, Meet the Composer (1975–1980), and Consortium of Jazz Organizations (1975–1978) in Rutgers IJS Subseries 4.10 ([Rutgers IJS](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/archival_objects/516568)), Williams toured college campuses throughout the 1970s. Per Scott Yanow ([*Syncopated Times*](https://syncopatedtimes.com/mary-lou-williams-profiles-in-jazz/)), she preferred college venues to *"smoky night clubs,"* though no complete master itinerary survives.
   - **University of Wisconsin–Madison (1976):** Invited for a three-day residency by Prof. James Cheatham (Director of the Black Music Ensemble) *"to help infuse authentic jazz into the school's instructional program,"* she *"shared the history of jazz with the UW–Madison community and performed at the Wisconsin Union Theater, showing her mastery of jazz styles, from Stride to Modern,"* including *Mary Lou's Mass* ([Wisconsin Academy](https://www.wisconsinacademy.org/magazine/fall-2021/essay/looking-mary-lou-williams)).
   - **St. Paul's College & Richmond, VA (April 1978):** Musicologist D. Antoinette Handy documented her April 8, 1978 concert at St. Paul's College in Lawrenceville, VA, and Richmond visit (*The Black Perspective in Music* 8, no. 2, Autumn 1980, pp. 195–214; [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1214051)).
   - **Philadelphia, PA (November 10, 1978):** A flyer documents a 2:00 p.m. lecture-demonstration in Hopkinson Hall at International House, Philadelphia, on relationships among blues, spirituals, gospel, and jazz, hosted by an unspecified university's Afro-American Studies Program ([Auger Down Books catalog, item 9, p. 11](https://augerdownbooks.cdn.bibliopolis.com/images/upload/augerdownbooksspring2025.pdf)).
   - **Broadcasts, White House, and Honorary Doctorates:** On October 8, 1978, she recorded the inaugural guest episode of Marian McPartland's NPR *Piano Jazz* ([NPR, 2010](https://www.npr.org/2010/05/06/126537497/mary-lou-williams-on-piano-jazz)), alongside late-1970s performances at international festivals, television programs, and the White House (exact date unspecified; [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)). Six institutions—**Fordham, Boston, and Loyola Universities, and Manhattan, Bates, and Rockhurst Colleges**—awarded her honorary doctorates ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)).

5. **University of Massachusetts Amherst (1975–1977):**
   Prior to Duke, Williams held her first multi-year faculty appointment as Artist-in-Residence at UMass Amherst, dated 1975–1977 by *Contemporary Black Biography* ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)), [*Britannica*](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mary-Lou-Williams), [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf), and the [NYPL Schomburg finding aid, pp. i–ii](https://nyplorg-data-archives.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/collection/generated_finding_aids/scm23917.pdf). (Some popular biographies claim ~1972 ["five years after 1970"] and an unretrievable Oxford snippet says "from 1976"; no original appointment document or specific UMass course numbers/syllabi were located in accessible records.)

6. **Duke University, Durham, North Carolina (September 1, 1977 – May 28, 1981):**
   Effective **September 1, 1977**, at age 67, Williams relocated to Durham after accepting Music Department Chairman Frank P. Tirro's offer to become **"Artist in Residence in Jazz Studies"** ([Down Beat, Oct 6, 1977, p. 11](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/70s/77/DB-1977-10-06.pdf); [Billboard, Sept 17, 1977, p. 73](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1977/Billboard%201977-09-17.pdf); [Duke Music, Feb 1, 2022](https://music.duke.edu/news/black-history-month-showcasing-contributions-six-faculty-and-staff)). She was the **first Artist-in-Residence in the Music Department's and Duke's history** ([Duke Centennial](https://100.duke.edu/story/mary-lou-williams/); [The Duke Chronicle, Jan 28, 2025](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-mary-lou-williams-centennial-center-jazz-icon-musician-religious-inspiration-stylistic-jazz-variations-20250128); [NWHM](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/mary-lou-williams)). While Duke already had a campus jazz performance tradition ([Duke Jazz Archive](https://library.duke.edu/rubenstein/collections/jazz)), her appointment came *"only fourteen years after the first black undergraduate students were admitted"* ([Kernodle, *Soul on Soul*](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)), crowning *"her crusade of the past decade to educate others about the cultural roots of jazz"* and making the jazz program *"one of the focal points for Black students and culture on campus"* ([Duke Centennial](https://100.duke.edu/story/mary-lou-williams/)). Though a non-tenured residency, Duke provided **"the first regular paycheck of her life"** ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)). From 1977 onward, she performed publicly *"only during college vacations"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html); [Down Beat, Oct 6, 1977](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/70s/77/DB-1977-10-06.pdf)).

   Her Duke instructional duties encompassed:
   - Teaching an introductory 16-week undergraduate lecture-demonstration course for non-musicians, *History of Jazz* (two sections, Tuesdays and Thursdays in the music building band room), covering jazz music, aesthetics, sociology, and history (*"teaching nonmusicians a course introducing them to Jazz in all its forms"*) ([Down Beat, Oct 6, 1977](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/70s/77/DB-1977-10-06.pdf); [Billboard, Sept 17, 1977](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/Billboard/70s/1977/Billboard%201977-09-17.pdf); [FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf); [BlackPast](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/williams-mary-lou-1910-1981/)).
   - Teaching a separate jazz improvisation course and giving weekly private piano lessons on the spinet piano in the front hall of her two-story Durham house and at Duke ([Down Beat, Oct 6, 1977](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/70s/77/DB-1977-10-06.pdf); [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html); [Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)).
   - Writing arrangements for and directing the **Duke Jazz Ensemble** (1977–1981) ([Down Beat, Oct 6, 1977](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/70s/77/DB-1977-10-06.pdf); [FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)).
   - Connecting classroom instruction to public sacred performance: on **February 25, 1978** (6:30 p.m. service in Duke Chapel), she was pianist and conductor for a chorus of **135 students from her Duke Jazz History classes** performing *Mary Lou's Mass* ([Duke Archives poster](https://exhibits.library.duke.edu/items/show/13424)). Duke Archives also preserve Department of Music audiovisual recordings from her residency, including an October 11, 1977 lecture.

   Her *History of Jazz* courses drew massive waiting lists. A May 14, 1979 *New Yorker* report ([Whitaker, "Mass Meetings"](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1979/05/14/mass-meetings-3)) and Kernodle (*Soul on Soul*) state that **more than 700 Duke students** applied when the 16-week course launched, forcing an initial cap of **150 students across two sections** (averaging 75.0 per section, accommodating at most **21.4%**, $150 / 700$, while leaving **78.6%**, or $550 / 700$, unaccommodated); Williams's April 1979 letter to Ratcliffe ([StudyingWithMLW.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)) states, ***"We had over 750 kids to register for my class – now have 196,"*** reflecting seat expansion between the 1977 launch (150 seats) and 1979 registration (196 seats, or **26.1%** of 750 registrants). Former student Hamida Jackson-Little recalled: *"It wasn't just a class where you listen to music. … She was a treasure"* ([Duke Centennial](https://100.duke.edu/story/mary-lou-williams/)). Her courses also sparked a **40.0% increase in jazz album sales** at Durham-area record stores (an aggregate local retail observation in [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)).

   Williams's Duke classroom relied on an uncompensated institutional partnership with her personal manager and religious confidant, **Father Peter F. O'Brien, S.J.** (b. 1940), who first wrote to her after her February 1964 *Time* profile, met her at Manhattan's Hickory House in 1964, began working for her in 1967, and served as her official manager from fall 1970 until 1981 ([Rutgers IJS-0060](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/resources/1215); [O'Brien, Fall 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)). O'Brien took an unpaid leave from St. Ignatius Loyola in New York to move to Durham and co-teach *History of Jazz*—described by O'Neill (1979) as *"her teaching colleague at Duke, and… a veritable encyclopedia on both jazz and the history of Miss Williams's work"*—even though Duke hired only Williams and gave O'Brien **no salary or medical benefits** ([Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)), giving the university two full-time instructors and a concert-management office for one faculty line.

   When Williams was diagnosed with bladder cancer (dated in most primary accounts to 1979, though one secondary source says *"the next year"* after 1977 at age 69), requiring surgery and hospitalization at Duke Medical Center from December 1979 through mid-March 1980—where a piano was rolled into her room before she returned to class in late March 1980 ([Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html))—O'Brien's presence enabled her to maintain teaching and ensemble directing through fall 1980, with a final public performance in Tallahassee, Florida, in 1980. Classroom, concert, and student records are preserved in Rutgers IJS Subseries 4.11/4.13 (*Duke University*, numbered 4.11 in the arrangement hierarchy and 4.13 in Series 4 scope prose) and Series 3 (*Personal correspondence*) ([Rutgers IJS](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/archival_objects/516568)). During her final bedridden months in early 1981 (with footage showing her listening in hospital to a Duke Wind Symphony rehearsal), she left incomplete manuscript sketches of ***History…*** (*The History of Jazz*) for 55 symphonic winds, piano trio, and chamber orchestra, with movements for ***Suffering***, ***Spiritual***, ***Ragtime***, and ***Gospel***—later realized by Duke Prof. Anthony Kelley and premiered by the Duke Wind Symphony in April 2024 ([Kelley, Apr 8, 2024](https://music.duke.edu/news/journey-through-musical-world-mary-lou-williams); [O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [The Duke Chronicle, Jan 28, 2025](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-mary-lou-williams-centennial-center-jazz-icon-musician-religious-inspiration-stylistic-jazz-variations-20250128)).

   In 1981, by direct student vote, Williams received Duke's **Trinity Award** ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)), and those students attended her funeral after her death on May 28, 1981 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)). Tenor saxophonist **Paul Jeffrey** *"first came to Duke in 1981 from Rutgers University at the request of Mary Lou"* ([*Philadelphia Tribune*](https://www.phillytrib.com/obituaries/paul-jeffrey-81-noted-tenor-saxophonist/article_5f2c4b86-c337-5374-88ea-403248902208.html)) to continue the Duke Jazz Ensemble (other notices list 1982 or 1983 as his formal permanent start). Fulfilling a promise to build a Black cultural center, Duke established an annual spring **Mary Lou Williams Day** and founded the **Mary Lou Williams Center for Black Culture**, which opened September 24, 1983 ([Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html); [Duke Archives](https://archives.lib.duke.edu/catalog/uamarylou); [*Duke Today*, 2023](https://today.duke.edu/2023/09/legacy-40-years-mary-lou-williams-center-black-culture-duke)). Secondary sources list her tenure end as "1977–1980" ([Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf), reflecting active classroom teaching) or **"1977–1981"** (O'Brien 2010, Kernodle, Rutgers IJS-0060, and *Encyclopedia.com*, reflecting her formal appointment until her death).

7. **The Mary Lou Williams Foundation (Founded 1980):**
   In 1980, Williams, O'Brien, and vocalist/educator Joyce Breach incorporated the **Mary Lou Williams Foundation** ([Rutgers IJS-0060](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/resources/1215); [Cunniffe](https://jazzhistoryonline.com/mary-lou-williams/)). Its charter targeted *"jazz training for gifted youth"* ([NWHM](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/mary-lou-williams)) and *"advanc[ing] public knowledge of the art of jazz music by teaching the same in all its forms to children between the ages of 6 and 12…."* O'Brien served as Executive Director until his death in 2015 ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)).

### 1.2 Format of Her Lecture-Recitals

Williams's lecture-recitals operated through a **two-person team classroom structure with Father Peter O'Brien**, a **multi-day campus residency model**, and **solo or trio spoken-and-played concert and recorded formats**.

#### 1. The Team Classroom Presentation Format at Duke
In her Tuesday/Thursday *History of Jazz* course at Duke, O'Brien and Williams split exposition and live demonstration into two halves. As Williams explained in 1980:
> *"At Duke we work as a team, both of us teach, he plays the records and does some talking. Peter is very dedicated. He knows jazz history and he certainly knows the history of Mary Lou Williams. Then too, he is an excellent researcher."* (Williams 1980, citing Holmes 1986, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf))

Dave Ratcliffe, sitting in the front row in fall 1979, recorded the sequence ([StudyingWithMLW.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)): in the first half, O'Brien lectured on the social and musical history of the era and played phonograph records; in the second half, Williams entered, demonstrated the style live at the piano, and required the entire class of non-musicians to sing her songs and idiomatic phrases of each jazz era from memory and ear.

#### 2. The Multi-Day Campus Residency Model
In his January 8, 1978 liner notes to *The History of Jazz*, O'Brien outlined her typical touring residency:
> *"A three or five day residency on a Campus will find her on stage in concert with her trio, in a music or black history class, in **lecture-demonstrations in large halls detailing, on the piano and in question and answer periods, the roots and history and course of Black American Music and Jazz**, with the college archivist taping oral history for the future, in rehearsing the college band, or the college Glee Club for a performance with her of Mary Lou's Mass."* ([FJ 2860 liner notes, pp. 3–4](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf))

Traveling only with Williams and a bassist, Williams rewrote and adapted choral parts for *Mary Lou's Mass* on sight to fit local student or church singers' vocal abilities ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)). O'Brien noted her campus narrations *"have been preserved on NewsFilm and in the archives of many radio stations"* ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)).

#### 3. Specimen 1: *The History of Jazz* (Smithsonian Folkways FJ 2860 / FW02860, Recorded Late 1970, Released 1978)
Described as *"a landmark work of combined scholarly and musical virtuosity"* ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/music-popular-and-jazz-biographies/mary-lou-williams)), *"a comprehensive performance-lecture"* ([*Britannica*](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mary-Lou-Williams)), and *"the only recording that I know of on which she speaks for any great length"* (O'Brien), ***The History of Jazz*** ([Smithsonian Folkways FW02860](https://folkways.si.edu/mary-lou-williams/the-history-of-jazz/ragtime/music/album/smithsonian)) is the definitive recorded solo lecture-recital. #### 4. Specimen 2: Live Concert History Medleys (Keystone Korner 1977, Carnegie Hall 1977, Montreux 1978)

#### 5. Specimen 3: The *"Jazz for the Soul"* Mimeographed Handout (1962–1964 Onward)
Written in 1962–1963 (during her *Saint Martin de Porres* sessions) and distributed *"everywhere she played"* starting in 1964 ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)), this one-page handout comprises three sections—(1) **"Origin"**, (2) **"Creative Process of Improvisation"**, and (3) **"The Spiritual Feeling: The Characteristic of Good Jazz"**—closing in all-capitals:
> *"YOUR ATTENTIVE PARTICIPATION, THRU LISTENING WITH YOUR EARS AND YOUR HEART, WILL ALLOW YOU TO FULLY ENJOY THIS EXCHANGE OF IDEAS, TO SENSE THESE VARIOUS MOODS, AND TO REAP THE FULL THERAPEUTIC REWARDS THAT GOOD MUSIC ALWAYS BRINGS TO A TIRED, DISTURBED SOUL."* ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian))

On *The History of Jazz* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)), she explained why she preferred campus lecture halls to clubs: **"Most musicians dislike playing in nightclubs. Often noises break the concentration, the purpose is lost and yet the performance must continue."** She used participatory rehearsals everywhere: before her June 1946 *Zodiac Suite* Carnegie Hall concert with the New York Philharmonic, she wrote an unannounced boogie-woogie chart the day prior, passed out parts while the conductor was at the telephone, and **"had 36 violins standing up, playing something like Charlie Parker would play"**—prompting violinists to stop her on the subway asking when she would write for them again ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)).

### 1.3 Core Teaching Philosophy and Methods

Williams combined a causal critique of post-1950 conservatory schooling with oral-tradition coaching. In her April 1979 letter to Dave Ratcliffe ([StudyingWithMLW.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)), she stated:
> ***"If you attended Berkeley [Berklee] & other schools – You should drop all schools 'cept to study composition etc. Jazz cannot be taught out of books (even Avant Garde)."***

#### 1. Why Formal Schooling and Exercise Books Block Natural Jazz Feeling
Williams diagnosed a four-step mechanism by which conservatory training undermined creativity:
- **Mechanical Pattern Conditioning ("Playing Like a Typewriter"):** *"We're living in a technical society, and people think that if they go to school and learn it, they'll be able to play it right. Sure, you can play like a typewriter, but it'll make your audience nervous to listen to it. It's nothing that's written down in a book"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)). At the Keystone Korner (1977/1978), she added: *"Now if I went to school and studied, I'd be able to write for NBC and the movies. And I don't like that, I create too much. See it blocks you... Often I'm driving the car and hear a horn go Toot! and start a tune. I was on my way downtown on the subway and just from the noise of the wheels I arranged something"* ([MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html)).
- **Short-Circuiting the "Mind-to-Heart-to-Fingertips" Channel:** *"The moment a soloist's hands touch the instrument, ideas start to flow from the mind, through the heart, and out the fingertips, faster than lightning. Therefore, if the mind stops, there are no ideas, just mechanical patterns. If the heart doesn't fulfill its role, there will be very little feeling, or none… at all"* ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)).
- **Intrusion of "Fancy Runs" into Improvisation:** *"The exercises in classical music that they give you in school destroy the natural feelings. When you study too much of the classics, you have a tendency to put runs and fancy things into jazz. But that's wrong... After bop they began going to school. This destroyed that healthy feeling in jazz"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)).
- **Selfish Individualism vs. Ensemble Accompaniment:** *"Each individual is for himself. He saves himself and he could care less and I don't understand it because if I don't accompany you well then I won't even feel like playing a solo myself because you will not be able to play anything if I don't accompany you right"* ([MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html)).

#### 2. Five Oral-Tradition Pedagogical Methods
Integrating *"concepts of music-making attributed to enslaved African-Americans into her teaching"* ([Kernodle, Duke Black Music Lab, 2021](https://sites.duke.edu/blackmusiclab/events/mary-lou-williams/)), Williams relied on five methods:

1. **Singing Every Era and Moving Backward When the Blues Sounded "Cold":**
   Insisting *"the only way to learn about music was to have it come from inside and to sing it out"* ([Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)) and telling students to listen with eyes closed (*"See what you feel in your heart"*; [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)), she required non-instrumentalists to sing every era before advancing:
   > ***"I have them sing all the eras of jazz. When we get to the bop era they sing, 'shoo-be-doo-be-doo.' My current students are doing quite well at getting the feeling. It has to go in the ears and saturate the entire mind before they're ready to move on to the next era. Last semester, I gave the kids a blues to sing, but it sounded cold. So we moved backward and did all the spirituals again. After that, they had a better feeling."*** ([O'Neill, Dec 7, 1979, pp. 30–31](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html))
   
   To teach phrasing and note attack, she drummed her fingers and sang standards like *"I Cover the Waterfront"* with elastic vocal timing, explaining, *"It's in how you attack the notes"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)).

2. **Direct Observation and Aural Apprenticeship ("I Watch" / "Shut-Up, Think Music"):**
   Having learned at age two or three on her mother's lap at a pump organ without formal lessons—her mother strictly forbade lessons lest reading sheet music ruin her improvisation ([Handy, 1980](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1214051); [O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian))—Williams summarized her learning theory as **"I watch"** ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)). As a child in Pittsburgh, she watched blues pianist Lovie Austin play with one hand while writing music with the other (*"Seeing her, challenged me into doing difficult things"*; [MLWbio.html](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MLWbio.html); [Brooks](https://dokumen.pub/liner-notes-for-the-revolution-the-intellectual-life-of-black-feminist-sound-0674052811-9780674258808-9780674052819.html)); after playing *"Milenburg Joys"* with her elbows under a sheet as a novelty act, an older musician heard one special chord and scolded her for clowning, prompting her to play seriously ([Kernodle, Chs. 1–2](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)). She learned notation and chord names in 15 minutes from Andy Kirk ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)) and recalled how older players in Kirk's band trained her: *"You see I was not allowed to talk until I was about twenty-five or thirty years old—the band I was with they'd tell me, 'Shut-up, think music, stop talking.'… there was a mental telepathy kind of a thing going forth… A guy would play something and you could answer him immediately then. And the minds were kept flowing like that. Fast minds"* ([MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html)).

   While encouraging transcription and permitting composition study, she corrected students by live keyboard demonstration. When Ratcliffe played his transcription of her eight-bar introduction to *"Roll 'Em"* (*Live at the Cookery*), she interrupted before he finished, stepped to the keys, and said, *"That's not right. Let me show you how it's supposed to go"* ([Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)). In her final letter to him (September 4, 1980), she wrote: *"Practice & do a great piano—your timing is better. I'll always tell you what's good or bad, Only way to teach it."*

3. **Custom Hand-Written Exercises, Relaxation, and Teaching "Love":**
   Banning published exercise books (*"invariably he'll play those exercises in jazz. It's better for him to write his own exercises. What you're hearing today is those exercise books from pianists who went to school"*), she hand-wrote custom exercises for each student ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)). In the first lesson, she lowered tense wrists (*"If their wrists are up, I have to bring them down. I have to get them to lay back and relax on each note"*), taught flat-fingered control (observed in Art Tatum and Bud Powell), closed her eyes while playing ([Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)), and massaged students' shoulders:
   > *"I do a lot of writing for each student, while other teachers are using books. I guess I'm more of a coach… While the student's doing exercises, I massage the shoulders and tell him, 'Relax, I love you, baby.' **I have to teach them love. The technique alone doesn't mean a thing.** We've got to save the jazz heritage."* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may))

4. **Mandatory Historical Apprenticeship ("Go Back to Fats Waller"):**
   Insisting *"The young have to learn from the old, and not the other way around"* (Holmes 1986, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)) and lamenting that youth *"no longer have heroes — like Babe Ruth"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)), she assigned pianists a canonical study list—**Jelly Roll Morton, Earl Hines, James P. Johnson, Fats Waller, Willie "The Lion" Smith, and Teddy Wilson**—telling them: *"Go back to Fats Waller. I'm not telling anyone to play like him, but go back and learn the records. Copy them. That's the metronome. It isn't corny"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)). She sent horn players back to **Lester Young** and **Ben Webster** *"to get the feeling. Then let them play what they want"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)).

5. **Practical Keyboard and Ensemble Discipline:**
   Because contemporary bassists specialized in single sub-genres (*"You can't find a man to play all four eras of jazz"*), she had to hire two or three bassists on an album and relied on solo piano when touring her *History of Jazz* sequence without multiple sidemen; while favoring **5-foot-2-inch** or **5-foot-7-inch Baldwin** grands over Steinways (noting a Steinway *"is too sensitive, and it'll tighten up on you if you pound on it,"* though Ratcliffe noted her perfect pitch was unsettled by out-of-tune pianos), she trained students to play any piano without excuses and modulate to a new key after every tune (e.g., *"I Can't Get Started"* in **C-sharp** rather than **C**) to avoid monotony ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may); [Ratcliffe](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html)).

#### 3. Student Uptake and Practical Boundaries
Dave Ratcliffe's 1979–1980 Duke apprenticeship ([StudyingWithMLW.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/StudyingWithMLW.html); [Ratical Index](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/)) illustrates her impact and limits. Offering him private lessons at **$20.00 per hour** instead of her **$35.00 per hour** standard rate—a **42.9% fee reduction** ($15.00 / $35.00 = 42.857%), with the student paying **57.1%** ($20.00 / $35.00 = 57.143%)—alongside twice-weekly classroom singing and intensive transcription (including *"No Title Blues"* and *"J.B.'s Waltz"* from *My Mama Pinned a Rose on Me*, among seven transcribed works in his archive) coincided with Ratcliffe raising his daily practice to at least **4 hours/day in October 1979** and **5 hours/day in November 1979** (a **25.0% month-over-month increase**, or +1.0 hour over the 4.0-hour baseline). Yet Ratcliffe noted that in the large lecture class, **"some kids signed up simply because it was supposed to be an easy grade and they would sit up in the back mumbling"**; and when Williams was hospitalized (December 1979–mid-March 1980) while Ratcliffe worked a weekend graveyard shift and fractured his right hand in February 1980, his momentum collapsed, leading him to conclude he **"simply wasn't obsessive enough to want to practice all day every day and play all night every night"** and leave Durham in April 1980.

## 2. Jazz Tree Analysis

### 2.1 Provenance, Attribution, and Dating Reconciliation (1971–1980)

The diagram is titled **History of Jazz**, bears Mary Lou Williams's name, and carries a Jazzmobile public-service credit ([Color reproduction, HistoryTree.jpg](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/HistoryTree.jpg); [Monochrome reproduction, PhillyJazz](https://phillyjazz.us/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/mary-lou-williams-tree-of-jazz.jpg)). Kernodle calls it *"perhaps the most tangible representation of Mary's rhetorical perspective on jazz in the 1970s"* ([Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)), while *Grove Music Online* ([Oxford Music Online](https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/grovemusic/view/10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.001.0001/omo-9781561592630-e-90000358106)) notes that *"Mary Lou Williams, Duke Ellington, and Art Blakey, among others, likened jazz to a tree."*

Three provenance and dating details require reconciliation:
1. **Illustrator Attribution (David Stone Martin vs. "David Sloan Wilson"):** O'Brien, Smithsonian Folkways, Smithsonian Music, Kernodle, Benjamin Givan, Carsyn Shirk, and Ethan Iverson confirm the pen-and-ink poster was drawn to Williams's exact specifications by **David Stone Martin** (1913–1992)—whose album-cover career (400+ covers for Asch, Disc, Folkways, and Clef/Verve) Williams launched in the early 1940s by introducing him to Moses Asch, and whose signature (`"Martin"`) appears at the bottom right of the root bed ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [Smithsonian Music](https://music.si.edu/story/mary-lou-williams-musical-and-social-change-agent); [Givan, *Journal of Musicology*, 2018](https://online.ucpress.edu/jm/article/35/3/397/63589/The-Fools-Don-t-Think-I-Play-Jazz-Cecil-Taylor); [MusicAficionado](https://musicaficionado.blog/2021/11/03/jazz-album-covers-by-david-stone-martin/)). The online [Rutgers IJS-0060 finding aid](https://archives.libraries.rutgers.edu/repositories/6/resources/1215) Biographical/Historical note and one secondary biography contain a clerical error misnaming him "David Sloan Wilson."
2. **Relation to the *History of Jazz* Folkways LP:** Gayle Murchison (2002; [Bard PDF](https://tools.bard.edu/wwwmedia/fisher-files/Murchison%20Mary%20Lou%20Williams.pdf)) refers to *"her published Jazz Tree included in her History of Jazz album."* However, the Folkways FW02860 booklet PDF ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)) contains no tree diagram and credits the cover artwork to Joan Miró; whether a print was inserted into certain LP pressings or reissues remains unresolved from the booklet PDF.
3. **Reconciling the 1971, 1973, 1977, and 1979–1980 Dates:**
   - **1970–1971 (Conceptual Genesis):** Williams recorded her *History of Jazz* tape (late 1970), wrote to Fr. Mario Hancock in 1971 that *"After the era of bop, the Negro began losing his heritage, in jazz"* ([Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)), and collaborated with Martin on Mary Records artwork (reproduced in SFW 40815).
   - **1973 (Early Jazzmobile Poster Reference):** *Jazz Makers: Vanguards of Sound* states "History of Jazz" was designed by Williams for a **1973 Jazzmobile poster** ([Archive.org text](https://archive.org/stream/Musical_DropBooks/Jazz.Makers.-.Vanguards.of.Sound_DropBooks_App_djvu.txt)), and a McGill University post notes *"another variation of the 'Jazz Tree'… drawn by the amazing Mary Lou Williams,"* indicating pre-1977 versions.
   - **1977 (Primary Pen-and-Ink Drawing by David Stone Martin):** O'Brien (2010; *Nite Life* notes, SFW 40816), Smithsonian Music, and Givan (2018) date Martin's drawing to **1977**. Shirk (2022; [LSU Repository](https://repository.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2338&context=honors_etd)) confirms the original drawing is in the **Rutgers IJS Mary Lou Williams Collection, Series 5, Box 2, unnumbered folder labeled *"History of Jazz Tree 1977"*** (also noted under Series 9: *Artwork and graphics* in 2025 reprocessing).
   - **1979–1980 (Published Offset Prints, Classroom Handouts, and Journal Publication):** Ephemera Press listings, Ratcliffe's archive (`HistoryTree.jpg`), Ethan Iverson ([*Do The Math*](https://ethaniverson.com/theory-of-harmony/)), Viktor Lillard ([*Hoppers in Athens*](https://hoppersinathens.com/en/2026/06/20/jazz-is-a-tree-lessons-from-duke-ellington-and-mary-lou-williams/)), and Kathryn R. Felt ([Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/p/CCqx9KIpOCb/)) date the published print to **1979**. An October 10, 1979 Williams letter enclosed printed copies ([Schulson Autographs Catalog 168, item 4188, p. 15](https://www.schulsonautographs.com/images/upload/schulson-autographs-catalog168.pdf)), O'Neill received it as a classroom handout in December 1979 ([Mission.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)), and Handy reproduced it in Autumn 1980 ([JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1214051)).

#### Key Analytical Takeaways from the Jazz Tree
1. **Non-Epochal Placement of the Blues as Continuous Circulating Sap:** Unlike textbooks placing the Blues only as a pre-1920 folk precursor, Williams placed **BLUES** in the roots and ran it vertically up both exterior sides of the trunk alongside Spirituals, Ragtime, K.C. Swing, and Bop. Where the blues stop flowing (on the left), branches wither into barren sticks ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [Givan, 2018](https://online.ucpress.edu/jm/article/35/3/397/63589/The-Fools-Don-t-Think-I-Play-Jazz-Cecil-Taylor)).
2. **Replacement of "New Orleans / Dixieland" on the Trunk with "Ragtime" and "K.C. Swing":** While New Orleans pioneers (King Oliver, Louis Armstrong, Sidney Bechet, Kid Ory, Jelly Roll Morton) appear on the leaves, the central trunk bypasses Dixieland and commercial East Coast swing labels for **Spirituals → Ragtime → K.C. Swing → Bop**.
3. **Corroboration of Dead-Branch Vocabulary and Context of "Cults":** The dead-branch labels (**Commercial Rock**, **Avant Guard**, **Black Magic**, **Cults**, **Exercises**, **Classical Books**) match Williams's prose verbatim: in a 1977 letter to journalist Phyl Garland, she fretted over youngsters who had *"lost the heritage playing rock, avant garde (sic), black magic exercises…"* ([Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)). While no primary document gives a single proper-noun gloss for *"cults"*, Shirk (2022) documents a 1973 Williams letter beginning *"in the direction of cults,"* and Ralph Dumain ([*Autodidact Project*](http://www.autodidactproject.org/my/jazzmystic1.html), citing Graham Lock) notes the era's discourse on avant-garde mysticism—such as Sun Ra's *"cult following"* and esoteric cosmology. Notably, the Tree does **not** label cool jazz, modal jazz, hard bop, or fusion as dead branches.
4. **Individual Creators on Leaves Rather Than Post-Bop Sub-Genre Labels:** Above **Bop**, the trunk bursts directly into leaves naming individual musicians—including post-bop icons **John Coltrane**, **Miles Davis**, **Sonny Rollins**, **Charles Mingus**, and **Buster Williams**—showing that while no fifth collective era had superseded bop, artists attached to the blues-and-bop trunk remained vital creators.
5. **Origin vs. Racial Eligibility and Women in the Canon:** Though the roots are grounded in Black American suffering, the canopy includes white musicians (Jack Teagarden, Zoot Sims, Red Norvo), showing Black cultural authorship did not operate as an exclusively racial performer rule. Placing herself (**Mary Lou Williams**) and **Lovie Austin** on the leaves inscribed women directly into the canon. (At her 1977 Carnegie Hall concert with Cecil Taylor, she drew a *different* diagram: *"three concentric circles, symbolizing, as she saw it, her music, his and the music of their interplay"* [[Dahl, *JazzTimes*, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)].)

## 3. Jazz Historiography

### 3.1 Origin, Core Spirit, and the "Soul" vs. "Jazz" Definition

Williams shifted the origin question from a single inventor to the collective historical experience of enslaved Black Americans, anchoring her 1970s lectures in the mantra ([Kernodle, *Soul on Soul*](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)):
> ***"Jazz is Black Heritage and a true American art form, which is spiritual and healing to the soul."***

1. **Collective Racial Suffering and the Spiritual as the Main Origin:**
   In *Jazz for the Soul* (1962–1963) and her 1970/1978 *History of Jazz* narration, she stated:
   > *"From suffering came the Negro spirituals, songs of joy, and songs of sorrow. **The main origin of American Jazz is the spiritual.** Because of the deeply religious background of the American Negro [Black American], he was able to mix this strong influence with rhythms that reached deep enough into the inner self to give expression to outcries of sincere [censored] joy, which became known as Jazz."* ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf))

   In her ca. 1970–1971 archival essay *"Has the Black American Musician Lost His Creativeness and Heritage in Jazz?"* (Rutgers IJS, cited in [Booker, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)), she wrote: *"Through our suffering God took pity on us and created the world's greatest true art: the 'Negro Spirituals' and from the Spirituals: Jazz was born in all its creative and progressive forms… Jazz is also a healer of the mind and soul."* She told Arnold Shaw in 1971 ([Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)): *"That's how the only American art was born — through the suffering of black American slaves. Soul came through the church and suffering"*; and wrote ([Aquilina, 2019](https://angelusnews.com/arts-culture/mary-lou-williams-pioneer-of-sacred-jazz)): *"Only out of suffering is a true thing born."* Closing Side 1 of *The History of Jazz*, she grounded her authority in lived struggle: *"to bring this history to you I had to go through muck and mud"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)).

2. **Objection to the Word "Jazz" in Favor of "Soul":**
   Sharing Duke Ellington's view in *Music Is My Mistress* (1973, p. 309, which she urged students like Ratcliffe to read: *"We stopped using the word ['jazz'] in 1943, and we much prefer to call it the American Idiom, or the Music of Freedom of Expression"*), Williams critiqued the commercial degradation of the label "jazz" on *The History of Jazz*:
   > *"I'd like to define two titles used constantly to describe people or music namely Soul and Jazz. **Soul is a musician with love, charity and sacrifice for his fellow man in his music. The presence of God through suffering.** It is also a substitute title for a Black American. Other definitions for Jazz are shameful and dishonorable for such soulful and religious music. It has been called other names down through the years. Ragtime, Swing, Bop and Avante Garde, but whenever there's a strong beat people always want to degrade the music by calling it Jazz. And when it is called Jazz other soulful, creative and artistic aspects in the music are lost."* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf))

3. **Mental Telepathy and Sacred Legitimacy:**
   She codified the permanent test of valid jazz:
   > ***"The spiritual feeling is characteristic of good Jazz. This feeling, the deep conversation and the mental telepathy going on between bass, drums and a number of other soloists are the permanent characteristics of good Jazz."*** ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf))

   Because jazz originated in the spiritual, she rejected any secular/sacred divide. Leveraging Vatican II liturgical reforms ([Booker, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)) and inverting religious critics like William Banks (who wrote *"the jazz tree has wormy, rotten fruit, legal scrapes, divorces, alcoholism, dope and nicotine addiction"*; [Aquilina, 2019](https://angelusnews.com/arts-culture/mary-lou-williams-pioneer-of-sacred-jazz)), she declared in *Ebony* (1966):
   > *"The ability to play good jazz is a gift from God… **This music is based on the spirituals — it's our only original American art form — and should be played everywhere, including church.** Those who say it shouldn't be played in church do not understand they are blocking the manifestations of God's will."* ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian); [Aquilina, 2019](https://angelusnews.com/arts-culture/mary-lou-williams-pioneer-of-sacred-jazz))

   She echoed this in 1973 (*"Jazz is the greatest religion of them all… It's a healer of the mind and the soul"*; [Murchison, 2002](https://tools.bard.edu/wwwmedia/fisher-files/Murchison%20Mary%20Lou%20Williams.pdf)), 1975 (*"It should be played everywhere — in churches, nightclubs, everywhere"*; *New York Post* via [NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/11/758076879/mary-lou-williams-missionary-of-jazz)), and 1978 (*"God did blacks a favor by creating jazz especially for them. God helps people through jazz; people have been healed through it. It has happened to me"*; [Booker, 2016, p. 36](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)). Her liturgical works—*St. Martin de Porres / Black Christ of the Andes* (rec. 1962, rel. 1964, Grand Prix du Disque Français), *The Pittsburgh Mass* (July 24, 1967), *Mass for the Lenten Season* (performed April 1968 at Harlem's St. Thomas the Apostle Church as a memorial for Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and in Rome in 1969; [Witkowski, 2025](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/jac/article/8/2/55/405608/A-Glorious-Celebration-Mary-Lou-Williams-s-Mass)), and *Music for Peace / Mary Lou's Mass* (commissioned by the Vatican 1969, rec. 1970, choreographed by Alvin Ailey 1971, and performed at St. Patrick's Cathedral on January 18, 1975, before 3,000+ people as *"the first jazz Mass ever celebrated there"*—where protesters *"went away ravin'"*—and again April 22, 1979)—embodied this historiography ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf); [Guggenheim Foundation](https://www.gf.org/stories/the-first-lady-of-the-jazz-keyboard); [MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html)).

### 3.2 Understanding of Spirituals and the Blues

Williams taught that **"spirituals, coupled with the blues, were at the root of all jazz styles"** ([Murchison, 2002](https://tools.bard.edu/wwwmedia/fisher-files/Murchison%20Mary%20Lou%20Williams.pdf); [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)). Whereas Langston Hughes called the blues "hymns to the secular region of man's soul," O'Brien (2010) observed that *"Williams went a step further, and called the blues 'the spiritual feeling' in jazz. In one deft phrase, she dispensed with any false division in thinking about human life — or about black American music."* As she stated: *"The blues was really important — this is your healing and love in the music… It's all spiritual music and healing to the soul"* ([O'Brien, 2010](https://folkways.si.edu/magazine-fall-2010-mary-lou-williams-jazz-soul/ragtime/music/article/smithsonian)), and *"I can play the old-fashioned spiritual and I can 'bop' on top of it"* (Handy, via [Murchison, 2002](https://tools.bard.edu/wwwmedia/fisher-files/Murchison%20Mary%20Lou%20Williams.pdf)).

Having learned *My Mama Pinned a Rose on Me* from stepfather Fletcher Burley and played earthy blues as a child in Pittsburgh house-rent parties ("chitterlin' struts") for $1/hour and gambling joints where Burley snuck her in under his topcoat ([*Melody Maker*, 1954](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)), she rejected the notion that the blues expressed defeat. In her December 1977 *My Mama Pinned a Rose on Me* (Pablo 2310 819) liner notes ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)), she wrote:
> ***"All my life I have felt that 'the Blues' was a special kind of music, expressing everyday experience.... It's music above racism and bitterness, and it shows heroism."***

On *The History of Jazz*, she also paid tribute to Folkways founder **Moses "Moe" Asch** for preserving this heritage: *"Without his sacrifices the world would never have known such great artists as Leadbelly, Muddy Waters, Pete Seeger, Woody Guthrie, and a great many other artists including a great concert pianist, Bella Bartop [Béla Bartók]... And to tell the truth there would be very little, if anything to tell about our only American art born through the suffering of slaves if it were not for him and his great company, Folkways Records"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)).

### 3.3 The Ten-Year Innovation Cycle and Evaluations of Historical Styles

Williams viewed jazz history as organic, collective transformations occurring roughly **every ten years** through the 1940s:
> *"Something has to come out of bop that you've never heard in your life before. Spirituals were new. You never heard anything like ragtime before when it was new. These **came out of pure suffering of a race of people**. Not the suffering of one person, but of a race. You get to Kansas City in the thirties, and you never heard anything like stride before. In the forties bebop was new. See, **it's about every ten years.** But what you're hearing now is what somebody studied in school."* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may))

She grounded her authority in having evolved across every era: *"I'm the only living artist who has changed her style with each period… I was there when it began"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html); [Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)), a claim endorsed by Nat Shapiro, Barry Ulanov ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)), and Billy Taylor ([Playbill](https://playbill.com/article/swing-time)).

| Historical Era & Cycle | Key Stylistic & Technical Characteristics | Left-Hand vs. Right-Hand Keyboard Evolution | Representative Figures Cited by Williams |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Spirituals & Gospel (19th C. Origin)** | Sacred songs of joy/sorrow born of slavery and racial suffering; vocal cry and call-and-response; included gospel in live demonstrations. | Organ-like devotional chordal voicings and slow cadence (*Lord Have Mercy*, *The Lord Is Heavy*). | Enslaved African American creators; passed to Williams by mother Virginia Riser. |
| **2. Ragtime & Early Stride (1890s–1920s)** | Syncopated vernacular piano; favored swinging, "ragged" East Coast stride over Scott Joplin's formal, Europeanized sheet-music idiom. | **Left hand is supreme** ("the metronome"): powerful alternating bass notes and chords; *"The right hand didn't mean a thing back then. If you didn't have a strong left hand, you weren't considered a good pianist"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)). | Jelly Roll Morton, James P. Johnson, Willie "The Lion" Smith, Fats Waller, Lovie Austin, Jack Howard. |
| **3. Kansas City Swing & Boogie-Woogie (1929–1930s)** | *"The greatest Jazz era of them all"* and *"the last era of the well trained musicians"*; 4/4 riff charts built in all-night jams; 8-to-the-bar boogie-woogie; walking bass; 6th chords. | *"A pianist had to have two strong hands. Especially a good swinging left hand"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)); *"The right hand became important around the mid-thirties with Teddy Wilson"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)). | Andy Kirk & Clouds of Joy, Bennie & Bus Moten, Walter Page, Count Basie, Jo Jones, Pete Johnson, Jimmy Rushing, Hot Lips Page, Lester Young, Ben Webster, Herschel Evans, Coleman Hawkins, George & Julia Lee, Earl Hines, Teddy Wilson, Art Tatum. |
| **4. Bebop / Modern Jazz (1940s–1950s)** | Spontaneous Black modernist revolution (*"the only real modern jazz"*); extended harmonies (*"The old blues took on a new look"*); phrasing accents **every other note** rather than smooth swing eighth notes. | **Left hand imitates the bop drummer**: sparse, syncopated chordal punctuation mirroring Kenny Clarke and Art Blakey *"dropping bombs"* (*"bop! baba-bop! bop!"*), while right hand runs advanced chord extensions. | Monk, Charlie Christian, Kenny Clarke, Art Blakey, Idrees Sulieman; Parker, Gillespie, Clyde Hart; Bud Powell, Dameron, Milt Jackson, Al Haig, J.J. Johnson, Max Roach, Kenny Dorham, Oscar Pettiford, Sarah Vaughan, Miles Davis. |

1. **Ragtime and Harlem Stride:**
   Honored as her personal starting point (*Who Stole the Lock Off the Henhouse Door*, learned at age three; [MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)). Per O'Brien, she favored the loose, swinging, "ragged" East Coast stride style leading toward **James P. Johnson** (whom John Hammond initially thought had recorded Williams's 1930 Brunswick solos under a pseudonym; Armitage in `MLW-TheHistory.pdf`) and **Fats Waller**, contrasting it with **Scott Joplin's** more formal, Europeanized idiom ([O'Brien, FJ 2968, p. 3](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02968.pdf); [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html)). Her **powerful Waller-style stride left hand** won instant respect on the TOBA circuit, sitting in with Ellington's Washingtonians at age 13–14, and in Kirk's band ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)).

2. **Boogie-Woogie:**
   Regularly demonstrated (*"Hesitation Boogie"*, *"Baby Bear Boogie"*, *"Roll 'Em"*), though subsumed within Swing rather than given a separate trunk label, and accompanied by a modest self-downgrade: *"I was never considered a top boogie woogie pianist but was trained to play all styles"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)).

3. **Kansas City Swing ("The Greatest Jazz Era of Them All"):**
   Having arrived in Kansas City in 1929 and served through 1940/1942 as solo pianist and chief composer-arranger for Andy Kirk and His Twelve Clouds of Joy—creating *Mess-A-Stomp*, *Walkin' and Swingin'*, *Froggy Bottom*, *Mary's Idea*, *Cloudy*, *Little Joe from Chicago*, *Roll 'Em* and *Camel Hop* (for Benny Goodman), *What's Your Story, Morning Glory* (for Jimmie Lunceford), and *Trumpets No End* (*Blue Skies* for Ellington)—Williams elevated Kansas City Swing to the pinnacle of jazz history:
   > ***"In the year of 1929 — the greatest Jazz era of them all — The Kansas City Swing era. What a great time for creative musicians and the last era of the well trained musicians. And out of this era great musicians such as Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell, Art Blakey, Max Roach and Charlie Parker. And many of the bop era musicians were born."*** ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf))

   She praised Kansas City across four dimensions:
   - *Communal ethics and mentorship:* *"There was much love and charity among musicians in Kansas City. To develop new techniques and ideas younger musicians listened to older musicians... Famous musicians from all over the world came to Kansas City to sit in on jam sessions to find to their surprise they had tackled such great giants as Lester Young, Joe [Jo] Jones, Count Basie, Ben Webster on tenor, Herschel Evans on tenor, Charlie Parker on alto, James Russian [Jimmy Rushing] a great singer, Buzz Mouton [Bus Moten], Benny Mouton [Bennie Moten], Pete Johnson, a great boogie woogie pianist, Hot Lips Page, Walter Page, George and Jr. [Julia] Lee and several great women pianists and countless other high school musicians"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)).
   - *Two-handed physical craft:* *"If a pianist didn't have a strong left hand, well, he was not considered very good at all. Nobody would play with him"* (Pearson 1987, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)).
   - *Harmonic experimentation anchored by beat and feeling:* *"back in Kansas City I found chords they're just beginning to use now"* (Berendt 1975, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)), and *"When I was arranging in the early thirties, I started using the sixth of the chord, and Andy Kirk told me it was against the rules. They only used triads, but we did it because it sounded good. Harmonies were very limited then. It was the beat and the feeling that mattered"* (Lyons 1983, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)).
   - *Institutional cause of decline:* Explained via talent extraction rather than stylistic exhaustion: *"John Hammond came to town… He took all the good musicians out, and it hasn't been good since"* (Pearson 1987, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)).

4. **Bebop:**
   Championing bop from its inception (noting to Lyons in 1977 that *"Charlie Parker was boppin' when he was in knee pants"* with an oompah high school rhythm section), she defended bop in her May 22, 1954 *Melody Maker* article (reprinted 1978; [MMiview1954.html](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html)) and 1970/1978 narration along three lines:
   - *Organic racial spontaneity and protection against theft:* Bop arose *"spontaneously in the same way as our blues and classic jazz, or any other music that a race of people produces. And I contend that bop is the only real modern jazz, despite the contentions of the copyists of Stravinsky, Hindemith and Schoenberg."* At Minton's Playhouse (*"the house that built bop"*), Monk and his peers were fed up with commercial "leeches" (scribbling down phrases on shirt cuffs and tablecloths), prompting Monk to declare: ***"We're going to create something that they can't steal, because they can't play it."***
   - *Technical definition via phrasing and accentuation:* *"Bop is the phrasing and accenting of the notes, as well as the harmonies used. Every other note is accented. Never in the history of jazz has the phrasing been like it is in bop,"* while the left hand mirrored drummers Clarke and Blakey *"dropping bombs"*.
   - *Continuity with the Blues and Kansas City love:* *"They were more or less like the Kansas City musicians. So much love flowing from them... The old blues took on a new look. The bop era blues chords added a great richness and more technique"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)). Regardless of style, music *"will have to have the jazz foundation — a beat!"* ([1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html)).

### 3.4 Criticized Post-Bop Styles, Written Polemics, and Cultural Nationalism

Williams diagnosed a profound rupture **after bebop** (specifically after Charlie Parker and Bud Powell):
- ***"'I'm sad in my heart,' she told me, 'because after Bud Powell and Charlie Parker, they cut off the growth of something beautiful.' … The music is spiritual. It came from spirituals, ragtime, Kansas City swing, and bop, which is when we lost our creative artists. After bop they began going to school. This destroyed that healthy feeling in jazz."*** ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may); [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf))
- *"After the bop era it seemed that the creation and the heritage was a little bit lost. If it continues in this direction we'll never have another great Art Tatum, Bud Powell, Dizzy Gillespie, Louie Armstrong... Now we come to a period of the music wherein the disturbance and unrest of the world has crept in to destroy the roots and heritage. Titles are misused and confusing and much kissing and hugging but very little charity and love."* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf))
- *"After the bop era it just all went to pot; musicians started playing commercial rock and what they call 'free music,' and **all the feeling went out of it**. If a creative musician listens only to music like that, he can't ever get off the ground."* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html))

In an October 10, 1979 private letter, she even accused younger Black musicians of losing their ancestors' spiritual feeling and claimed some white players were now expressing that feeling better ([Schulson Autographs Catalog 168, item 4188, p. 15](https://www.schulsonautographs.com/images/upload/schulson-autographs-catalog168.pdf)).

#### Williams as Archival Essayist, Black Feminist Theorist, and Cultural Nationalist
Scholarship by Daphne A. Brooks (*Liner Notes for the Revolution*, Harvard UP, 2021; [NPR, Sept 14, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams); [De Gruyter PDF](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/9780674258808-012/pdf)), Tammy L. Kernodle ([PBS Wisconsin, 2010](https://pbswisconsin.org/watch/university-place/university-place-the-legacy-of-mary-lou-williams-ep-492/); [Duke Black Music Lab, Sept 23, 2021](https://sites.duke.edu/blackmusiclab/events/this-music-is-black-music-mary-lou-williams-and-the-identity-politics-of-jazz-in-black-power-era-america/); [NPR, Sept 11, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/11/758076879/mary-lou-williams-missionary-of-jazz)), and Tracy McMullen (*American Music*, 2023; [UI Press](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/am/article/41/3/417/392012/The-New-Jazz-Studies-Continues)) contextualizes her historiography:
- **Typewritten Archival Essays:** Preserved in **Rutgers IJS (MC 60), Series 5, Box 2**, including ***"Don't Destroy the Roots"*** (unpublished, p. 6), ***"Has the Black American Musician Lost His Creativeness and Heritage in Jazz?"*** (ca. 1970–1971), ***"Jazz Is Our Heritage,"*** and ***"Music and Progress"*** (*Jazz Record*, Nov., year unspecified). She defined a genius as *"one who does everything without being taught,"* attacked *"frustrated never-made-it critics who once aspired to be a musician but had no ears so decided to write about it"* whose *"burning jealousy"* threatened the *"fellowship of jazz,"* and praised the ideal critic *"who listens to a record broadminded… as if he was playing it,"* open to all *"the historical genres running through it."* In letters to Leonard Feather, she protested industry racism: *"the black musician has been badly treated as far as grammys & awards… We black musicians became embittered because"* the Black artist received insufficient *"credit for what was created thru his suffering"* ([Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)).
- **Cultural Nationalism and Black Studies:** Brooks reads Williams as *"the black woman musician as theorist of her own forms,"* while Kernodle interprets her curriculum as an African diasporic reclamation aligning conceptually with Black cultural nationalism and the birth of academic Black Studies (without formal allegiance to a specific Black Power organization, emphasizing what Black people created under American conditions rather than generic appeals to continental Africa): *"At the very same time that black studies and women's studies and gender studies… in the academy are being crafted and constructed, she is constructing some very similar narratives in jazz history… It's very progressive"* ([NPR, Sept 11, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/11/758076879/mary-lou-williams-missionary-of-jazz)). Kernodle compares her works to *"the protest songs of Odetta, Fannie Lou Hamer, and Nina Simone in accentuating and reclaiming the themes of resistance, resilience, and spirituality"* ([Duke Black Music Lab, 2021](https://sites.duke.edu/blackmusiclab/events/this-music-is-black-music-mary-lou-williams-and-the-identity-politics-of-jazz-in-black-power-era-america/)) and notes *"the public articulation of such ideas began with Mary Lou Williams in the 1970s"* (p. 247, in [McMullen, 2023](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/am/article/41/3/417/392012/The-New-Jazz-Studies-Continues)).

## 4. Evaluations of Specific Styles and Musicians

Williams applied a consistent evaluative standard: **historical rootedness in the blues/swing tradition + genuine emotional "feeling" and communication + organic harmonic and aural invention**. As she wrote in 1954:
> *"Regardless of what technique he may have, a jazzman must be able also to tell a story. **I can never admire a robot pianist whose runs flow straight from his studies instead of his feelings.**"* ([1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html))

Separating style-level polemics from personal malice (*"I'm not putting anyone down personally"*; [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)), her specific verdicts are detailed below.

### 4.1 Avant-Garde and Free Jazz (Ornette Coleman, Eric Dolphy, and Style-Level Verdicts vs. Her Own Practice)

#### 1. Style-Level Condemnations
- **1964 (*Time*):** On the New York free-jazz vanguard (identified in *Time*'s footnote as **Ornette Coleman, Eric Dolphy, and Sonny Rollins's** chordless experiments), she asked: *"Have you heard these 'freedom' players? **They're making people sick all over town.**"* ([Time, Feb 20/21, 1964](https://time.com/archive/6808446/jazz-the-prayerful-one/))
- **1964 (On Ornette Coleman and "Evil" Self-Absorption):** Quoting her nephew's rock 'n' roll friends: *"These rock 'n' roll kids say their music is modern and that music like Ornette Coleman's is corny… Why I think theirs may be the music of the next era is because jazz musicians became too evil. **Before jazz was a matter of love. There's a lot of music now that is played for the musicians themselves, not for the people who are listening**"* (Williams 1989, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf); no other direct personal verdict by Williams on Coleman or Sun Ra was located in accessible texts).
- **1964 / 1987 (Inability to Hear Chords):**
  > *"When I had my foundation in New York, a couple of the 'free' musicians came to me to be helped. And I discovered that **they couldn't hear chords**. Quite a few of them haven't learned to play basic things, because the 'free' music is: play anything you want, but make sure you don't play a true chord. Although that can be exciting, I don't really call that music."* (McPartland 1964 / Williams 1987, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf))
- **1977 (Len Lyons Interview):** *"If you play rock and avant-garde, **you're corny for jazz. You're stiff because there's love in the jazz feeling. The avant-garde is cold.** … **The avant-garde has many frustrated classical pianists, and they'll disappear.** … What you're hearing these days is **black magic, exercises, foreign sounds, rock, and free. You're not hearing jazz.**"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may))
- **1977/1978 (Keystone Korner Interview):** *"Some of the avant-garde guys think they're so far out until they're greater than the other cats but they're not… I see **great havoc on earth if jazz doesn't come back on radio and TV soon** because the other music is making people too nervous."* ([MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html))
- **1977/1978 (*Embraced* Liner Essay):** Wrestling with *"a perverted force… coming into the music"* from *"foreign composers"* and *"commercial rock,"* she saw the avant-garde scene filled with ***"hate, bitterness, hysteria, black magic, confusion, discontent, empty studies, musical exercises by various European composers, sounds of the earth, no ears, not even relative pitch and Afro galore"*** (adding, *"I'm crazy about African styles in dress"*) ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/); [Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)).

#### 2. Documented Counterexamples and Nuances in Her Own Practice
1. **Priority via 1930s/1940s Kansas City "Zombie Music":** She, Monk, Lester Young, and Dick Wilson had already improvised chordless *"outre"* music in Kansas City (*Melody Maker*, 1954; [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)): *"I used to improvise without chords in the thirties in Kansas City, and they called it zombie music... On a tape twenty-five years ago I played what music sounds like today. I had a premonition of what it would sound like and played like Cecil Taylor. Melba Liston laughed and laughed. I played it for Dizzy's wife [Lorraine Gillespie], and she said, 'Why don't you call that \"a fungus among us\"?'"* ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may)).
2. **Recording *"A Fungus Amungus"* on *The History of Jazz* (1970/1978):** Though introduced as *"predicting new sounds in the music that would almost destroy the roots and heritage of the Black man"* ([MLW-TheHistory.pdf](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MLW-TheHistory.pdf)), O'Brien praised her performance as *"impressionistically beautiful and shaped with feeling… Avant Garde or Free Jazz replete with the history of Jazz that preceded that form"* ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)).
3. **Performing Free and Complex Meter Works (*Zoning*, 1974; *Free Spirits*, 1975; Montreux, 1978):** The Jazz Tree's dead branches *"did not stop Williams from using rock beats or 'free…'"* improvisation ([Stoll, 2021](https://www.scribd.com/document/744275540/Stoll-2021-the-Technique-Of)). On *Zoning* ([SFW40811.pdf, pp. 1–2, 8–9](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40811.pdf)), she placed *"Zoning Fungus II"* alongside sacred tracks; Bob Blumenthal notes her harmonic/rhythmic notions *"bordered on the avant-garde; yet her ear for melody and her deep immersion in the blues idiom remained,"* with *"Medi II"* shifting between 8/8 and 12/8 and *"Intermission"* in 11/4 (5+6 bass pattern). On *Free Spirits* (1975), she wrote of *"combining the past, the present, and the future — the experience and the experiment"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)), sounding *"like a young pianist influenced a bit by McCoy Tyner"* ([Yanow](https://syncopatedtimes.com/mary-lou-williams-profiles-in-jazz/)); in June 1978 she shared a Carnegie Hall bill with Herbie Hancock and McCoy Tyner ([FW02860.pdf](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02860.pdf)); and at Montreux (1978) she played the free improvisation *"Concerto Alone at Montreux"* ([Cunniffe](https://jazzhistoryonline.com/mary-lou-williams/)).
4. **Employing Avant-Garde Drummer Andrew Cyrille:** Williams's 1960s checkbooks ([Booker, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)) show avant-garde drummer **Andrew Cyrille** (later Cecil Taylor's percussionist) on her payroll, even as his free style served as *"a comical foe for Williams in her normative articulation of jazz as music"* (Kernodle).
5. **The *Embraced* Resolution:** Insisting in her diaries she had *"changed with the times"* ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)) and telling Billy Taylor *"we must be willing and able to open our minds to new ideas and developments"* ([Smithsonian Music](https://music.si.edu/story/mary-lou-williams-musical-and-social-change-agent)), she resolved in the 1978 *Embraced* liner notes that ***"one should play all forms of music"*** ([Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams)).

### 4.2 Cecil Taylor and the April 17, 1977 *Embraced* Concert

Taylor had admired Williams since hearing her at Boston's Savoy Club in 1951 (*"She was playing like Erroll Garner, but her music had a lot of range"*). After she heard Taylor in London and he visited her at The Cookery—telling her, *"No one's playing anything but you"*—Williams proposed, organized, and personally financed a two-piano concert at **Carnegie Hall on April 17, 1977** (released 1978 on Pablo Live as *Mary Lou Williams & Cecil Taylor: Embraced*) *"in the spirit of reconciliation between the two 'camps' of jazz"* ([Balliett, *The New Yorker*, June 6, 1977](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1977/06/06/new-york-notes-21); [Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)). Appealing for funds (*"Help save this precious music and keep me out of Bellevue! Smile!"*), she barely broke even in a *"no more than half-filled"* hall ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)).

The collaboration clashed over structure: Williams wrote dual-piano spiritual arrangements for the first half using her "history of jazz" sequence with her rhythm section (Bob Cranshaw, bass; Mickey Roker, drums), expecting historical grounding to lead into freer interaction (*"I'll play free and then I'll jump back to swinging"*); Taylor resisted her framework, complaining she *"wanted him to play her music but [she] refused to perform his music the way he wanted it heard"* ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)). On stage, Taylor unleashed marathon percussive clusters, turning the concert into ***"a heavyweight fight rather than a peaceful resolution"*** (Kernodle), *"at best a tug of war in which Mr. Taylor managed to remain dominant"* (*NYT*), with Taylor as *"the predatory avant gardist"* against her *"spare, bluesy ministrations"* (Gary Giddins) ([Balliett, 1977](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1977/06/06/new-york-notes-21); [Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)). Backstage, saxophonist **Paul Jeffrey** had to be *"physically restrained from punching him"*; Baroness Pannonica "Nica" de Koenigswarter wrote to Williams comparing the concert to *"a confrontation between heaven and hell, with you (heaven) emerging gloriously triumphant!!!"* ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)—a remark by Koenigswarter, not Williams).

Williams's assessments of Taylor combined genre critique, recognition of his bebop roots, and affection:
- **On His Playing and Genre Status (1977):**
  > ***"He's very creative and I like a lot of things he does, although some of it I don't care for, personally. But he's from bebop. He's in his mid-forties, and all the beboppers are now in their forties or fifties. But he's not playing jazz. There's no jazz about it at all. It's a feeling that's been lost in the music. Everything now is either technical or electronic, nothing deep and sincere that will touch the hearts of people and make them feel better."*** ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may))
- **Her Personal Letter to Taylor (c. 1979):** ***"…being angry you created monotony, corruption, and noise… Why destroy your great talent clowning, etc.? … I still love you, Mary."*** ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/))
- **Subsequent Encore Performance (1978):** Williams performed with Taylor a second time at Duke University in a recorded 1978 concert ([Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf)). After following the April 1977 Taylor concert by sharing Carnegie Hall with Benny Goodman in January 1978, she remarked: *"Now I can really say I played all of it"* ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)).

### 4.3 John Coltrane

Williams held **John Coltrane** in high esteem, placing him on the living leaves of the Jazz Tree ([HistoryTree.jpg](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/HistoryTree.jpg); [O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)):
1. **Spiritual Feeling vs. New Era Creation (1977):** Measuring him against her ten-year era trunk:
   > ***"Coltrane had that spiritual feeling, too, but he didn't introduce anything new. He was just a great giant."*** ([Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may))
2. **Mastery of the Tradition and Ear-First Apprenticeship (1979):** Dahl notes Williams accepted late Coltrane (and Taylor) because *"they could still, if they wanted to, play 'within the tradition'"* ([Dahl, 2000](https://www.jazztimes.com/features/profiles/mary-lou-williams-cecil-taylor-embraceable-you/)). In 1979, Williams cited Coltrane and Gillespie as proof masters play by ear before formal study: *"People like Dizzy Gillespie and John Coltrane were playing before they ever studied. You just have to play it first. Jazz doesn't come out of school"* ([O'Neill, 1979](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/Mission.html)).
3. **Exempting Coltrane While Criticizing Unskilled Followers (1964):**
   > ***"Take a guy like Coltrane, he knows what he's doing, but these people without a knowledge of music, it's like — well, it's a very neurotic world."*** (McPartland 1964, in [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf))
4. **The Famous "Basie" Quote on Coltrane Imitators (1977/1978):**
   > *"I take them back to Fats Waller and bring them up to date. They've got to know about the older music in order to play avant-garde. See the guys that they're patterned after like Coltrane and all that, **they would never be a Coltrane because they don't know anything about the Basie.** It's just like you going to school starting in kindergarten, first, second grade."* ([MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html))

### 4.4 Dave Brubeck and Cool / Eurocentric "Theoreticians"

Accessible records contain no 1970s condemnation of **Dave Brubeck** by name (he is absent from the 1977 Lyons interview, 1979 O'Neill profile, and the 57:43 audio of her October 8, 1978 *Piano Jazz* broadcast; [NPR, 2010](https://www.npr.org/2010/05/06/126537497/mary-lou-williams-on-piano-jazz)). Her documented record dates from the 1950s:
1. **1954 Genre Differentiation from Bebop and Critique of Classical Copyists (*Melody Maker*, May 22, 1954, reprinted 1978):** In 1954—when Brubeck appeared on *Time*'s cover (examined alongside Kernodle's work in Kelsey Klotz's *Dave Brubeck and the Performance of Whiteness*, 2023, reviewed by [McMullen, 2023](https://scholarlypublishingcollective.org/uip/am/article/41/3/417/392012/The-New-Jazz-Studies-Continues))—she distinguished Brubeck from bop while rejecting Eurocentric "theoreticians":
   > *"Bop is the phrasing and accenting of the notes, as well as the harmonies used. Every other note is accented... **Musicians like Dave Brubeck come up with different styles which may be interesting. But they are not bop.** … And I contend that bop is the only real modern jazz, despite the contentions of the copyists of Stravinsky, Hindemith and Schoenberg. The swing era produced smooth eighth notes which many of our theoreticians are still playing."* ([1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html))
   
   Williams herself had studied Schoenberg, Stravinsky, Hindemith, and Bartók in the 1940s with arranger Milton Orent (heard on *Zodiac Suite*); for her, modern harmonies were valid only when subordinated to a swinging beat and blues storytelling rather than smooth conservatory eighth notes ([Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html); [Shirk, 2022](https://repository.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2338&context=honors_etd)).
2. **1957 Praise (*Down Beat*):** Quoted praising Brubeck: ***"He brought something fresh into jazz."*** ([Down Beat, July 25, 1957, p. 16 / PDF p. 18](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/50s/57/Down-Beat-1957-07-25-24-15.pdf))
3. **1958 Bel Canto Benefit Donation:** Unable to attend her 1958 Carnegie Hall benefit, **Brubeck contributed $10** alongside Erroll Garner and Baroness Nica ([Booker, 2016](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/7/8/108)).

### 4.5 Evaluations of Other Musicians, Rock, and Electronic Instruments

| Musician / Subject | Williams's Specific Evaluation and Reasoning | Source & Date |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Keith Jarrett, Chick Corea, & Bill Evans ("Schools of Exercise")** | Praised technical command while linking them to *"exercises"* and *"classical books"* dead branches for losing warm feeling: ***"Great musician! Great technician!*** *But they're not what I'm trying to save… they lost their feeling — period. Commercial rock and avant-garde are frigid. I'm not putting anyone down personally."* Granted Jarrett and Corea *"are playing good avant-garde. Maybe it's exciting,"* and said of Evans: *"They went to schools of exercise... some kids have to be taught to play like Bill Evans because they've studied so much they can't play any other way. They can't play with jazz feeling, like [saxophonist] Zoot Sims."* | [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Cedar Walton & Bobby Timmons** | Singled out among contemporary post-bop pianists for retaining blues-rooted feeling: *"Who's the fellow who plays like Bobby Timmons did? Oh, yeah, it's Cedar Walton. He's got the feeling I'd listen to."* | [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Zoot Sims** | Praised as *"the greatest of the white players for jazz feeling"* (and placed on the Jazz Tree leaves), proving her criterion was oral/musical rather than racial essentialism. | [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may); [HistoryTree.jpg](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/HistoryTree.jpg) |
| **Dizzy Gillespie** | Revered friend and bop pioneer on Jazz Tree leaves: *"Dizzy is the most copied musician in the world. He's modern yet stuck to the heritage."* | 1971 letter in [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html); [HistoryTree.jpg](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/HistoryTree.jpg) |
| **Thelonious Monk** | Close friend, protégé, and original creator (*"Most of us admire him for this"*). Noted his youthful technique exceeded his later style, recalled his early harmonies being called *"Zombie music"* (*"the screwy chords reminded us of music from Frankenstein"*), and noted Teddy Hill dubbed him *"the High Priest of Beebop."* | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [Kernodle](https://dokumen.pub/soul-on-soul-the-life-and-music-of-mary-lou-williams-music-in-american-life-2020019223-2020019224-9780252043604-9780252085536-025204360x.html) |
| **Bud Powell** | Revered: ***"No one could play like Bud***… *he is the only pianist who makes every note ring. The strength in his fingers must be unequalled"* (noting Monk's influence). Observed he was *"double-jointed,"* playing with flattened middle-finger sections. With Parker, marked the end of her creative trunk. | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Duke Ellington** | Called *"a genius who gets the best results out of musicians,"* noting only Ellington's and Basie's bands spontaneously rearranged old numbers on the bandstand. On Jazz Tree leaves. | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [HistoryTree.jpg](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/HistoryTree.jpg) |
| **Fats Waller & Willie "The Lion" Smith** | Waller revered as the pedagogical *"metronome"* for left-hand swing; admired how he *"composed his number while the girls were dancing."* Recalled Smith advising her: *"The first note you play creates the mood. Don't be shy. Play that note, get the mood going, hold it, and you've got the audience."* | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Art Tatum** | Praised for unmatched mental speed, control, and harmonic foresight (*"Chords he was throwing in then, the boppers are using now"*), though warning students that copying his runs mechanically *"can be like exercises you'd get at school."* | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Erroll Garner** | Called *"the Billie Holiday of the piano… original and has more feeling than almost any pianist I can think of,"* defending him against critics who looked down on him for not reading music. | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html) |
| **Teddy Wilson** | Praised as *"a genius"* whose formal study *"has not been allowed to impair his individual style"* and with whom *"the right hand became important around the mid-thirties."* | [1954 *Melody Maker*](https://ratical.org/ratville/MaryLouWilliams/MMiview1954.html); [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may) |
| **Rock 'n' Roll / R&B (Aretha Franklin & James Brown) vs. "Commercial Rock"** | In 1964, praised her nephew's rock 'n' roll friends for *"creating like jazz musicians did in the thirties… just like Leadbelly… it has an awful lot of soul. My nephew had a number with a release of a kind I'd been trying to write for fifteen years. 'My goodness,' I said, 'I'm going to steal that!'"* In an undated essay: *"Rock… is the new era of rhythm and blues and was created by Black Americans…. Even with the loudness of this music there was a great deal of soul (feeling)…. When the commercial world grabbed it..[.] It became amateurist (sic) and lousy."* In her 1977 letter to Phyl Garland, exempted **Aretha Franklin** and **James Brown** as ***"the only real mccoys,"*** while condemning corporate TV/radio *"slop"* (*"It destroys a natural talent"*). | [Fledderus](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc278129/m2/1/high_res_d/1002656666-fledderus.pdf); [Brooks, NPR, 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/09/14/758077669/drag-em-how-movement-shaped-the-music-of-mary-lou-williams); [MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html) |
| **Electronic Instruments & Amplification** | Admitted utility for composing while condemning physical and spiritual harm: *"I do use them for writing arrangements. But it's not good for your ears, and it's not good for your soul"* (1977); *"with the electronic, my ear has gone out--and I thought because I was older--and the doctor tells me that's happening to young kids"* (1977/1978). | [Lyons, 1977](https://cerra.substack.com/p/mary-lou-williams-may-10-1910-may); [MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html](https://daveratcliffepiano.com/MaryLouWilliams/MLW-ItsAllGreat-1977.html) |
